### Summary

LSAT argument structure reviewed premise and conclusion definitions with analogy, followed by strategies for identifying components and addressing test-day constraints.

**Argument Components Defined**  
The LSAT argument consists of a premise, defined as evidence, and a conclusion, which is the author’s claim. An argument must contain at least 1 piece of evidence and 1 claim, and they must be accepted as the single most important concept for the LSAT.

**Identifying Structure and Logic**  
Participants must identify the conclusion themselves using indicator words and the 'Why/Because Test' to determine the relationship between premises and conclusions. Premises must always be accepted as true, while conclusions are the components intended to be challenged.

**LSAT Strategy and Practice**  
The Logical Reasoning section is 2/3 of the LSAT, making it the primary focus, while a slow reading strategy is implemented to improve accuracy. The primary decision is to prioritize accuracy over speed during initial practice to master methodologies.

* **Definition of Premise**: A premise is defined simply as evidence ([00:02:10](#00:02:10)). It is a statement that provides evidence for another statement ([00:03:12](#00:03:12)). Joshua Fisseha's Presentation mentioned they would use the terms 'evidence' and 'premise' interchangeably ([00:07:35](#00:07:35)).

* **Definition of Conclusion**: A conclusion is simply the author's claim. An argument must contain both a conclusion supported by a premise; if one component is absent, it is not considered an argument ([00:03:12](#00:03:12)).

* **Argument Components and Necessity**: An argument must contain a claim (conclusion) supported by evidence (premise) ([00:04:12](#00:04:12)). It does not have to be a reasonable or good argument, but it must include one piece of evidence and one claim to be categorized as an argument. Arguments are described as the single most important concept to understand for the LSAT ([00:05:17](#00:05:17)).

* **Identifying the Conclusion**: The conclusion is not explicitly labeled by the test, and identifying the main conclusion from the lines in the stimulus is part of the test's challenge. dakota brown inquired whether the claim is obvious, and Joshua Fisseha's Presentation confirmed that they must identify the conclusion themselves ([00:06:04](#00:06:04)).

* **LSAT Approach to Arguments**: They discussed that the LSAT should be viewed from a structural perspective, similar to an architectural engineer, where the goal is to identify the structure ([00:06:50](#00:06:50)). The conclusion is at the top of the structure, and it requires a premise to be valid ([00:07:35](#00:07:35)).

* **Analogy for Argument Structure**: Joshua Fisseha's Presentation used the analogy of a wedding cake on blocks to illustrate the relationship between the conclusion and the premises ([00:07:35](#00:07:35)). Premises are the solid blocks, or foundational pillars, that support the conclusion (the cake), which cannot stand on its own ([00:08:30](#00:08:30)) ([00:13:09](#00:13:09)).

* **Handling Premises (Evidence)**: Participants are strictly instructed never to negotiate or question the premises regarding their truthfulness; they must be accepted as true regardless of how reasonable they seem ([00:10:56](#00:10:56)). The premises are the foundational pillars of the argument, and they do not require support from the conclusion or each other ([00:13:09](#00:13:09)).

* **Premise Indicator Words (FABS)**: There are specific indicator words on the LSAT that signal a premise will follow ([00:13:09](#00:13:09)). The acronym FABS (For, After all, Because, Since) represents the four most common premise indicator words ([00:14:10](#00:14:10)).

* **Premise Indicators as Indirect Conclusion Indicators**: Premise indicator words are indirectly conclusion indicator words because whatever is stated before them is generally a conclusion. It was noted that there can be more than one conclusion in an argument, but the ultimate goal is to find the main conclusion ([00:15:48](#00:15:48)).

* **Handling Conclusions**: Conclusions are the part of the argument to be challenged, in contrast to premises which must be accepted ([00:18:18](#00:18:18)). Conclusions are where the author goes "out on a limb" to infer something new from the evidence ([00:17:12](#00:17:12)). The goal is to identify the conclusion and fight against it, acting as a devil's advocate ([00:18:18](#00:18:18)) ([00:33:08](#00:33:08)).

* **Conclusion Indicator Words**: Conclusions also have indicator words, though they are not as "ironclad" as premise indicators due to the existence of different types of conclusions ([00:19:55](#00:19:55)) ([00:23:16](#00:23:16)). Common conclusion indicator words include "therefore," "thus," and "so" ([00:22:12](#00:22:12)).

* **Validity of Conclusions (LSAT Spoiler)**: Joshua Fisseha's Presentation gave a "spoiler alert" that conclusions on the LSAT are almost never valid ([00:19:08](#00:19:08)) ([00:31:40](#00:31:40)). A valid conclusion must be guaranteed by the evidence with certainty and must be a "must be true" statement based off the evidence ([00:27:00](#00:27:00)).

* **Intermediate Conclusions**: The discussion covered intermediate conclusions, which are statements supported by premises but also support another statement (the main conclusion) ([00:19:55](#00:19:55)) ([00:21:21](#00:21:21)). Intermediate conclusions essentially become premises for the main conclusion ([00:52:26](#00:52:26)).

* **Argument Structure Identification (The Why/Because Test)**: To identify whether a statement is a premise or a conclusion, participants can use the "Why Test" or "Because Test" ([00:36:14](#00:36:14)). By inserting 'Why' or 'Because' between the two ideas, the statement that follows the word is the premise, and the coherent structure reveals the argument's parts ([00:38:12](#00:38:12)) ([00:39:50](#00:39:50)).

* **Non-Argument Content in Stimulus**: Not every statement in an LSAT argument is a premise or conclusion; some statements provide background information or opposing viewpoints. The author is assumed to disagree with any opposing viewpoint that is mentioned ([00:25:20](#00:25:20)).

* **Translating the Stimulus (Reading Strategy)**: For practice, they established a drill where, after reading a complete thought in the stimulus, dakota brown should pause, look away, and rephrase the content into natural, basic language ([00:41:59](#00:41:59)). * **LSAT Structure and Focus**: Joshua Fisseha's Presentation clarified that the single-paragraph arguments discussed fall under the Logical Reasoning section, distinct from the Reading Comprehension section, which uses multiple paragraphs for several questions ([00:58:24](#00:58:24)). * **LSAT Section Breakdown and Focus on Logical Reasoning (LR)**: The LSAT has two main section types: Logical Reasoning (LR) and Reading Comprehension (RC). The test includes three scored sections, two of which are LR, making LR twothirds of the test, and one is RC. The focus is currently on LR because it constitutes the majority of the exam ([00:59:15](#00:59:15)).
