### Details

* **Causal Claims and Alternative Explanations**: Joshua Fisseha and dakota brown discussed strategies for evaluating causal claims in the LSAT. Joshua Fisseha explained that causal arguments can be challenged by considering alternative explanations, such as a third factor that causes both the purported cause and effect, or by showing the possibility of a reverse relationship ([00:00:00](#00:00:00)). They discussed that simply observing two events occurring near each other in time, such as dakota brown's LSAT studies and the war in Iran, does not establish a causal link ([00:02:03](#00:02:03)). A key strategy for weakening causal arguments is identifying a scenario where the relationship might be purely coincidental, meaning the effect occurs without the cause, or the cause occurs without the effect ([00:03:45](#00:03:45)).

* **Strengthening and Weakening Arguments**: Joshua Fisseha emphasized that every LSAT argument should be approached by identifying its logical weaknesses to effectively defend or attack it. When attempting to weaken an argument, one should act like a prosecutor to expose vulnerabilities; when strengthening, the goal is to protect those vulnerabilities ([00:06:37](#00:06:37)). They confirmed that denying the other side's explanation or proposing an alternative, third-factor explanation are two viable methods for weakening a causal claim ([00:08:22](#00:08:22)).

* **Correlation vs. Causation**: The participants clarified the distinction between correlation and causation. While correlation—two things occurring together—is a requirement for a causal claim, it is not sufficient to establish one ([00:09:19](#00:09:19)). Joshua Fisseha noted that the LSAT often uses "active" language (e.g., "makes," "leads to") to signify causal claims, whereas "static" or descriptive language often signals mere correlation ([00:12:13](#00:12:13)).

* **Paradox Questions**: They transitioned to discussing "paradox" questions, which require reconciling two seemingly contradictory facts by introducing a new piece of information ([00:16:08](#00:16:08)) ([00:32:19](#00:32:19)). Joshua Fisseha stated that mastering the underlying principles of causation and logical gaps helps in solving these types of questions naturally ([00:17:18](#00:17:18)).

* **Chimpanzee Paradox Exercise**: They worked through a sample problem regarding chimpanzees. The paradox involved the observation that chimpanzees use threat gestures when angry and also attack out of anger, but almost never do both concurrently ([00:22:59](#00:22:59)). They identified the need to find an explanation (a "C" factor) that accounts for both behaviors being mutually exclusive ([00:26:10](#00:26:10)). Through evaluating the provided answer choices, they determined that the correct explanation was that threat gestures serve as an outlet for aggression, thereby reducing the need for physical attacks ([00:33:10](#00:33:10)).

* **Mosquito Population Paradox Exercise**: They analyzed a second problem concerning the unintended consequences of planting fruit trees to reduce mosquito populations. The paradox was that while birds were attracted and ate mosquitoes, the mosquito population increased ([00:39:35](#00:39:35)) ([00:45:22](#00:45:22)). They discussed how, in ecological scenarios, a common LSAT trick is to introduce a factor where the birds also eat the natural predators of the mosquitoes, ultimately leading to a population surge ([00:43:30](#00:43:30)) ([00:48:57](#00:48:57)).

* **Paradox Resolution Strategy**: Joshua Fisseha highlighted that for paradox questions, one should always examine the information immediately before and after "pivot" words like "but," "however," or "yet" to identify the point of tension ([00:46:16](#00:46:16)) ([00:52:33](#00:52:33)). They concluded that the correct answer in the mosquito example was that the birds ate many predators of the mosquitoes, thus counteracting the intended reduction ([00:48:57](#00:48:57)) ([00:51:43](#00:51:43)).
