# Naming the Flaw Before the Choices

A flaw question asks what is wrong with an argument. Most students read the answer choices and wait for one to sound right. The choices are written in abstract language, so two of them often sound right at once and you end up guessing. There is a shorter path. More than half of flaw questions are built on one of four mistakes, so you can name the mistake in the stimulus first and then look for the choice that says it. The four are: enough treated as required, together treated as because, a group and its members confused, and no proof treated as proof of the opposite.

## Say the flaw before you look at A

On the practice question from our session, a counselor argued that harsh criticism is necessary for people to change. You named the flaw correctly: sufficient treated as necessary. That is the hardest part, and the rest comes easily once you have it. The next step is to hold that flaw in your head as one plain sentence, so the choices become a matching job.

1. **Find the conclusion and the evidence.** The conclusion is what the author wants you to accept. The evidence is the reason. "A doctor's note gets you excused from the exam. So a note is the only way to be excused." The second sentence is the conclusion.
2. **Say exactly what the evidence gives.** Not what it sounds like. This evidence says a note is enough to get you excused. It does not say a note is required.
3. **Say exactly what the conclusion adds.** It says a note is the only way. That is a bigger claim than "enough."
4. **Say the flaw in your own words.** "The evidence shows one route works. The author says it is the only route." Now read A to E looking for the choice that says that.

**The lesson is the order.** Name the flaw first, then read the choices. If you read the choices first, you are deciding between sentences you only half understand.

## Sufficient or necessary?

This is the flaw in the counselor question, and it is one of the two most common flaws on the test. You need to tell these two ideas apart without stopping to think.

**Sufficient.** Enough to guarantee the result. If you have it, the result happens.
Looks like: A doctor's note gets you excused from the exam. Test: If this is true, does the result have to follow?

**Necessary.** Required for the result. Without it, the result cannot happen.
Looks like: You must be enrolled in the course to sit the exam. Test: If this is missing, is the result impossible?

**A guaranteed way is not the only way.** A doctor's note gets you excused. That does not make it the only way, because a family emergency might get you excused too. The same goes for "work hard and study and you will do well." Someone who skipped all of that and still did well does not break the rule. The rule named one guaranteed path. It never said it was the only one.

## Two questions, one flaw

Run both questions on any conclusion that sounds like it is about what gets a result.

1. Does the evidence say this is enough to get the result?
2. Does the conclusion say this is the only way to get the result?

If both are yes, the author has treated sufficient as necessary. If only the first is yes, this pair is not the flaw; if the conclusion says that without it the result cannot happen, ask the second question. If only the second is yes, the evidence does not say this route is enough. Check whether the evidence gives any other reason to think nothing else works. If it does not, the conclusion has gone past its support. If neither is yes, look for a different flaw.

## A word like cause does not name the flaw

Words like cause, because, leads to and only.

Words like these tell you what the sentence is about. They do not tell you what is wrong with it. The word *cause* tells you the author is talking about causes. It does not tell you the author made a causal mistake. In the counselor question the evidence showed that harsh criticism gives a person a motive to change, and the conclusion said only harsh criticism will do. The conclusion used a cause word, but the flaw was the word *only*.

**The trap that costs the most.** Seeing the word cause and choosing correlation versus causation. A real causal flaw has two parts: the evidence reports that two things go together, and the conclusion says why. If the evidence does not report that, the word may be a decoy. Ask what the conclusion adds to the evidence instead.

You said it yourself. You saw the word cause and thought causation. Then you read the conclusion again and asked, *"Why is it only harsh criticism? ... It could be gentle, it could be constructive ... it could be other things."* That second read is the method. It is the sufficient-versus-necessary question in your own words.

## Together is not the same as because

On the test the evidence usually reports a study: two things happen together. The conclusion then says one of them is why the other happens. A correlation is easy to show. A cause is very hard to prove.

**Correlation.** Two things happen together. That is all it says.
Looks like: People who read a lot are less likely to drop out of high school. Test: Does this only say they go together?

**Causation.** One thing is the reason the other happens.
Looks like: Reading keeps students in school. Test: Does this say why?

**Three stories fit the same fact.** Reading could keep students in school. Staying in school could give students more time to read. Or richer parents could give a child more books and more help, and that drives both. A correlation does not choose between them. Sometimes two things go together by coincidence. When the author picks one story, ask which others are still standing.

## A group is not each of its members

What is true of a whole is not necessarily true of each part. What is true of one part is not necessarily true of the whole. The flaw runs in both directions.

**Whole to part.** A trait of the group gets pushed onto each member.
Looks like: The team is a star team, so every player on it is a superstar. Test: Can the group have this trait while one member does not?

**Part to whole.** A trait of one member gets pushed onto the group.
Looks like: She is the best guard in the country, so her team must be great. Test: Can one member have this trait while the group does not?

**You already do this.** You told me you had seen questions like this and it felt like common sense. Your words were, *"Just because the team does well overall doesn't mean every single player on the team does well."* The name is new. The reasoning is yours. Watch for a noun that changes size between the evidence and the conclusion: a team and a player, a campus and a building.

## No proof is not proof of the opposite

A lack of evidence is not evidence of lacking. If nobody has shown a claim is true, all you know is that nobody has shown it. You do not know it is false.

**Unproven.** No one has shown the claim is true. All we know is that it has not been shown.
Looks like: The prosecution could not prove you are guilty. Test: Has anyone shown the opposite, or has no one shown anything?

**Proven false.** Someone has shown the claim is false.
Looks like: The records show you were in another city that night. Test: Is there evidence for the opposite, not just a lack of evidence?

**It fails in both directions.** *"Because I can't prove you're innocent, you're guilty."* And the reverse: because I can't prove you're guilty, you're innocent. You put it that way yourself, and I called it the best example. The same move shows up on the test whenever an author says nobody has shown X, so not X.

## Name the flaw, then check

Which of the four is it? Say it in your own words before you read the answer.

- A clear syllabus will cause students to start, because it gives them a plan. Students need a plan before they can start. So a clear syllabus is the only thing that gets students started. Answer: Sufficient treated as necessary. The word cause is a decoy. A syllabus is enough to get students started. The conclusion says it is the only way.

- People who walk to work report better moods. So walking to work improves your mood. Answer: Correlation versus causation. The evidence says walking and good mood go together. People in good moods may be the ones who walk, or something like a free morning could drive both.

- The restaurant has a great reputation, so every dish on its menu must be great. Answer: Whole to part. A restaurant can have a great reputation while one dish on the menu is poor.

- The lead engineer on the project is brilliant, so the project must be going well. Answer: Part to whole. One brilliant member does not make the whole project go well.

- Nobody has shown the old footbridge is unsafe, so it must be safe. Answer: Unproven versus proven false. No one showing it is unsafe is not the same as someone showing it is safe. All we know is that nothing has been shown.

## Turn each answer choice into a plain question

Flaw answer choices are written in abstract language. They sound like a foreign language until you have seen them a few times. Your job is to translate them into plain questions about the stimulus.

1. **Name the flaw first.** One plain sentence about what the author did.
2. **Translate each choice into a question.** A choice that says the author refutes an opposing argument becomes: did the author attack anyone's argument? In the counselor question nothing was refuted, so that choice described something that never happened.
3. **Cut a choice for a reason you can say.** You told me you sometimes just know a choice is wrong. Write the reason, even a short one: "The stimulus never talks about avoiding anything." That reason is the skill.
4. **Read the wrong choices after the question, not only your pick.** Look up what each one means. A wrong choice today can be the right answer on the next question. After you have seen the same language a few times, it stops sounding abstract.
