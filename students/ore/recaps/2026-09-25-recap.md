# Find the exception the conclusion cannot survive

An argument can contain true premises and still reach a conclusion that does not follow. Your job is to accept what the author states, identify exactly what the author is trying to prove, and ask whether those premises leave room for an exception.

## Keep the evidence and the conclusion separate

Read the argument in two parts:

| Part | What to ask |
|---|---|
| **Premises** | What facts or rules has the author given me? Accept these for this question. |
| **Conclusion** | What exact claim does the author say those premises establish? |

Do not challenge a premise because it seems unlikely in real life. For this exercise, grant it. Then test the link: **could every stated premise be true while the conclusion is false?** If so, the argument has a gap.

The objection is not a new story you must prove. It is a possible case the author has failed to rule out.

## Read the strength of the conclusion

“Parker **must** get the assignment” is stronger than “Parker **could** get the assignment.” To defeat *must*, you only need one possible alternative. You do not need to prove that the alternative will actually happen.

The wording matters in other conclusions too. A premise about someone *killing* a person does not, by itself, establish that the person committed *murder*. The conclusion uses a different term. The author needs a rule or definition connecting the two. The issue is the move from one claim to the other, even when it sounds natural in conversation.

When a conclusion uses a word such as “better,” ask what standard gives that word meaning. More degrees or more prestigious jobs could be evidence under one definition of “better.” Without a stated standard, those facts do not automatically settle the conclusion.

## Build an objection from what the author left open

Use this sentence to begin:

> Just because **[the actual premises]** are true, it does not necessarily follow that **[the exact conclusion]** is true. What if **[a possible exception]**?

Replace the brackets with the argument's own content. “The evidence does not prove the conclusion” is too general to show where the problem lies.

Suppose the premises say that Larson cannot take an assignment because of a scheduling conflict, Frank cannot take it because the task requires more assertiveness, and Parker is the only other supervisor in the shipping department. The author concludes that Parker **must** take the assignment.

Accept every premise. Then ask: **who is allowed to take this task?** The premises have not said the assignee must be a shipping-department supervisor. Someone outside that group could be eligible. If that is possible, Parker is not yet required.

You may notice more than one open possibility. Another department's supervisor and a qualified non-supervisor are different examples. You only need one sound objection to show the conclusion is not guaranteed.

## Use the gap to test a necessary assumption

A necessary assumption is something the argument needs to stay standing. In the Parker example, the author needs the task to be restricted to shipping-department supervisors. Otherwise the fact that Parker is the only remaining supervisor *in that department* cannot make Parker the only possible assignee.

Test a candidate assumption by negating it. If the task **can** be assigned to someone outside that group, the stated reasons no longer force it to Parker.

Keep your eyes on the conclusion while reading choices. A choice about Larson's assertiveness may repeat a word from the stimulus, but Larson already has a scheduling conflict. Whether Larson is assertive does not close the alternative-person gap or make Parker necessary.

## A repeatable process

1. State the author's main conclusion in your own words, preserving its strength: *must*, *all*, *better*, or another important term.
2. List the actual premises separately. Accept each one.
3. Ask whether the premises can all be true while the conclusion is false. Look closely at new terms, undefined standards, and people or options the premises never excluded.
4. State one specific “what if” case. It does not have to match the answer choice word for word.
5. Only then use the question type to decide what to do with the gap. For a necessary assumption, look for a statement the author cannot give up; negate it to check.
