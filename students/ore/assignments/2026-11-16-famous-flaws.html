LSAT ULogical Reasoning

Famous Flaws

Ten classic flaws in LSAT arguments

LSAT arguments cover all sorts of subjects, but many go wrong in the same ways. Some of these mistakes are so common that they have been given names. We call them the famous flaws.

Once you understand how a flaw works, you can spot it in a new argument and explain why the reasoning goes wrong. Knowing the flaw helps you find an assumption and decide what new information would strengthen or weaken the argument.

    Flashcards

    If A guarantees B, A is sufficient for B. B is necessary for A: whenever A is true, B must be true too. Both descriptions tell you about the same direction. To go from B back to A, you need evidence for that direction as well.

    Example

    Anyone who brings a doctor’s note gets excused from class. Mina was excused, so she must have brought a doctor’s note.

    Evidence and conclusion

    Premises
    Anyone who brings a doctor’s note is excused from class. Mina was excused from class.
    Conclusion
    Mina must have brought a doctor’s note.

    Why the reasoning fails

    Bringing a doctor’s note guarantees you’ll be excused. That makes the note sufficient. Being excused is necessary for anyone who brings a note: the school can’t take the note and still mark that student unexcused. Mina was excused, but she might have had a family emergency. The author treats the note as required even though the school has only said a note is enough. Other evidence, such as a record of Mina handing in a note, could establish that she brought one.

    What can we logically conclude?

    Given: the original conditional · A → B
    Brings a doctor’s note→Gets excused

    A is sufficient. B is necessary whenever A is true. The conditional alone doesn’t tell us who brought a note.

    Invalid reversal · B → A
    Gets excused⇢Must have brought a note

    Mina might have been excused for a family emergency and never brought a note.

    Invalid negation · Not A → Not B
    Doesn’t bring a note⇢Can’t get excused

    Mina’s family emergency defeats this inference too: she has no note and is excused.

    Valid contrapositive · Not B → Not A
    Didn’t get excused→Didn’t bring a note

    If they had brought a note, the school would have excused them.

    Membership is required. Is it enough?

    “Only members can borrow tools.” Being a member is necessary. To borrow a tool, you have to be a member. Membership satisfies one requirement. You might also need a reservation.

    “Kai is a member, so Kai can borrow a drill” treats a requirement as enough. “Kai couldn’t borrow a drill, so Kai isn’t a member” makes the related mistake. A member without a reservation could be turned away.

    What if one group fits inside another?

    Every violinist in the orchestra is a musician. Picture the violinists inside the group of musicians. This example has other musicians. If the orchestra consisted entirely of violinists, membership in either group would tell you the other. You’d need that extra information.

    Musicians
    Violinists
    Cellists, flutists, and others

    A musician could play the cello. That’s why knowing someone is a musician doesn’t tell you they play the violin. Someone who isn’t a violinist could still be a musician.

    Conditional logic walkthrough by Kevin Lin

    “Every excused student brought a note” says the same thing as “Everyone without a note stayed unexcused.” Mina, excused for a family emergency without a note, is a counterexample to both.

    How it appears on the LSAT

    Illustrative answer-choice wording
    Treats a condition that guarantees an outcome as a condition required for that outcome.

    In your own words, say what the author knows and what they claim must be true. In Mina’s case, the missing step is that every excused student had to bring a note. With the tool library example, the missing step goes the other way: every member can borrow a tool.

    Example

    A survey found that remote employees reported higher job satisfaction than office employees. Working remotely must make employees more satisfied.

    Evidence and conclusion

    Premise
    Remote employees reported higher satisfaction.
    Conclusion
    Remote work causes higher satisfaction.

    Why the reasoning fails

    The survey links remote work with higher satisfaction. It can support a causal hypothesis, but it cannot determine which explanation is correct. More satisfied workers may be likelier to request remote work. Flexible managers could offer remote work and improve satisfaction in other ways. The groups might have different jobs, or the association might arise by chance in this sample.

    Proposed cause

    Remote work affects job satisfaction.

    Reverse direction

    Job satisfaction affects who chooses remote work.

    Common cause

    Flexible management affects both.

    How it appears on the LSAT

    Illustrative answer-choice wording
    Infers a causal relationship from an observed association without ruling out plausible alternatives.

    Evidence that comparable employees became more satisfied after switching to remote work strengthens the proposed direction. Evidence that remote employees already had unusually flexible managers weakens it. If that management difference would account for the entire association, the argument needs to assume it does not.

    Example

    The Tigers basketball team finished the season at the top of its league standings. Every player on that roster must be a star.

    Evidence and conclusion

    Premise
    The Tigers basketball team finished the season at the top of its league standings.
    Conclusion
    Every player on the Tigers is a superstar.

    Why the reasoning fails

    Finishing first describes how the Tigers performed together. Coaching, teamwork, and complementary skills can produce the best record without a roster full of stars. That is division, an inference from whole to parts. Composition goes the other way: individually talented players can still make a poor team. Whether a trait carries over depends on what it describes. If every player is under 30, the roster consists entirely of players under 30.

    Try it in both directions
    Whole to part
    Team finished first⇢Every player must be a star

    A strong team can have ordinary players.

    Part to whole
    Every player is a star⇢Team must finish first

    A team full of stars can lose if the players don’t work well together.

    How it appears on the LSAT

    Illustrative answer-choice wording
    Assumes a characteristic of a group must also characterize each member.

    Identify what has the property. Sampling uses some members to infer what is true of other members. Composition asks whether a property carries over to what the members form together.

    Example

    I checked the town archive and found nothing that says the tree was planted in 1902. It must have been planted some other year.

    Evidence and conclusion

    Premise
    The researcher found no record in the town archive showing the tree was planted in 1902.
    Conclusion
    The tree must have been planted in a different year.

    Why the reasoning fails

    The archive search establishes that the researcher found no record. A tree planted in 1902 could have gone unrecorded. The missing entry carries more weight if the archive is known to list every tree planted that year. The same distinction works in reverse: a failure to show the tree was planted in another year does not establish 1902.

    Discrediting the argument

    A separate mistake reaches the same result by defeating an argument. If the photo used to date the tree turns out to show another tree, that discredits the evidence for 1902. It does not settle the planting date. Discrediting an argument against 1902 likewise gives you no proof that 1902 is right.

    How it appears on the LSAT

    Illustrative answer-choice wording
    Treats the failure to establish a claim as sufficient reason to reject it.

    Separate what the author has shown about the evidence from what they claim about the conclusion.

    Example

    I pulled ten marbles off the top, and every one was red. Most of the marbles in this jar must be red.

    Evidence and conclusion

    Premise
    I scooped ten marbles from the top of the jar. All ten were red.
    Conclusion
    Most of the marbles in the jar are red.

    Why the reasoning fails

    The ten marbles represent what the scoop reached. If the jar was filled in layers, the top could be entirely red while most of the jar is gray. Drawing more marbles from that same layer would leave the selection problem intact. Sample size matters too: a small sample may be adequate for a narrow claim and too imprecise for a population-wide estimate.

    What can go wrong with a sample or survey?

    Biased sample
    A town polls 300 people at its dog park and concludes that most residents own a dog. The place they chose makes dog owners especially likely to be included.
    Sample too small
    A librarian asks two visitors at random whether the library should open earlier. Both say yes. Two answers give her very little to go on about what most visitors want.
    Leading question
    A survey asks, “Should we waste money keeping the library open on Sundays?” People are already being pushed toward saying no.
    Unreliable answers
    A teacher asks students to raise their hands if they used an answer key. Some students may keep their hands down to avoid admitting it in front of the class.
    Conflicting surveys
    One poll says most residents support Sunday hours. Another says most oppose them. Check who was asked, when, and how the question was worded before relying on either result.

    A survey can still be good evidence. Before calling it flawed, point to a reason its results might give the wrong impression.

    How it appears on the LSAT

    Illustrative answer-choice wording
    Draws a conclusion about a population from a sample that may not represent it.

    Ask who could be selected, who actually responded, and how the answers were collected. For a Weaken question, look for a difference between the sample and the population that bears on the conclusion.

    Example

    Leah says the new bus route would get us downtown faster. Of course she’d say that. The stop would be right outside her apartment. The ride downtown wouldn’t be any faster.

    Evidence and conclusion

    Premise
    Leah says the new bus route would shorten the ride downtown. The new route would stop outside her apartment.
    Conclusion
    The new route wouldn’t shorten the ride downtown.

    Why the reasoning fails

    Leah would benefit from the bus stop. That explains why she might favor the proposal, but the travel-time claim depends on the route and schedule. Her credibility could matter if her own timing was the only evidence. A reason to doubt that timing would lower confidence in her estimate without establishing that the new route wouldn’t be faster.

    How it appears on the LSAT

    Illustrative answer-choice wording
    Rejects a claim because of the interests of the person advancing it.

    Identify which part of the evidence depends on the speaker’s reliability. Compare a motive for supporting a route with a documented history of falsifying the times the argument relies on.

    Example

    A renowned architect recommends this supplement for insomnia. Given her professional accomplishments, it must work.

    Evidence and conclusion

    Premise
    A prizewinning architect recommends the supplement.
    Conclusion
    The supplement works for insomnia.

    Why the reasoning fails

    Expertise in architecture does not establish expertise in treating insomnia. The architect could be right about the supplement, but the argument gives no reason to trust her judgment on that question. A qualified specialist citing relevant evidence would give us more to consider.

    Other ways an appeal can go wrong

    Popularity. A poll can tell you which cleaner people prefer. It cannot tell you which cleaner kills more germs.

    Emotion. Sympathy for a driver might bear on whether to waive a fine. It does not establish that the driver parked legally.

    Tradition. The fact that a library has always closed on Sundays does not show that Sunday closure serves patrons best. Attendance and operating costs would bear on that decision.

    How it appears on the LSAT

    Illustrative answer-choice wording
    Relies on an opinion without establishing the relevance of the person’s expertise.

    A vote can establish what people prefer. It takes different evidence to establish that their preference is safer. Sympathy can matter to a decision about hardship. It cannot settle a factual claim about what happened. A longstanding practice might have a record of success. Its age alone tells us nothing about how well it works.

    Example

    This is the best café in town because the people with the best taste go there. We know they have the best taste because they picked the best café.

    Evidence and conclusion

    Premises
    People with the best taste choose this café. The only reason given for calling them discerning is that they chose what the author already calls the best café.
    Conclusion
    This is the best café in town.

    Why the reasoning fails

    Ask what supports the judgment that the regulars have good taste. The speaker’s only answer is that they chose the best café, which is the claim under debate. Someone who doubts that the café is best has received no independent reason to believe it.

    How it appears on the LSAT

    Illustrative answer-choice wording
    Presupposes the truth of the conclusion it seeks to establish.

    A premise can repeat a conclusion verbatim, restate it in different words, or depend on it through another claim. Shared vocabulary alone does not make an argument circular. An independent blind tasting would offer evidence about the café even to someone who doubts the conclusion.

    Example

    No employee should be promoted without three years of experience. Sarah has two years, and the company should promote her now.

    The two claims

    Premises
    Three years are required. Sarah has two.
    Conclusion
    Sarah should be promoted now.

    Why the reasoning fails

    The author says no one should be promoted without three years of experience. The author grants that Sarah has two years and recommends promoting her now. Both claims concern Sarah’s promotion now. The recommendation conflicts with the principle the author accepts. If the principle allowed exceptions or the recommendation concerned next year, both statements could be consistent.

    How it appears on the LSAT

    Illustrative answer-choice wording
    Draws a conclusion that conflicts with a principle the argument accepts.

    Find the two commitments and read them together. Different people, times, circumstances, or senses of a word can dissolve an apparent conflict. Two speakers disagreeing is also different from one speaker committing to incompatible claims.

    Example

    The map shows a bench beside the bank of a river. A bank is a place where you can deposit money, so you can deposit money beside the bench.

    Evidence and conclusion

    Premises
    The bench is beside the riverbank. Financial banks accept deposits.
    Conclusion
    You can deposit money beside the bench.

    Why the reasoning fails

    A riverbank is the land beside a river. A bank that accepts deposits is a financial institution. The argument gets from a bench by the river to somewhere you can deposit money by reading the same word in two different senses.

    How it appears on the LSAT

    Illustrative answer-choice wording
    Relies on a shift in the meaning of a key term.

    The shift can be subtle. A study’s finding may be statistically significant, meaning it clears a statistical threshold. Concluding that the effect is large enough to matter in practice uses ‘significant’ in another sense. The flaw depends on the argument using that shift to reach its conclusion.
