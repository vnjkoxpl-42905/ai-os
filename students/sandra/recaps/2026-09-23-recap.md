# Intermediate Conclusions: Follow the Support to the Main Claim

An intermediate conclusion is a claim the author supports and then uses to support a further claim. Its position comes from the job it does in the argument, not from whether it sounds important or appears near the end. Finding it helps you identify the true main conclusion and see exactly where an argument's reasoning may fail.

## See the three levels of an argument

An argument links evidence to a claim. When it has an intermediate conclusion, the support runs through two links:

```text
Premise → intermediate conclusion → main conclusion
```

For example:

```text
He still plays at an age when most players have retired.
    ↓ supports
He is the most dominant player of the modern era.
    ↓ supports
He is the greatest player of all time.
```

The middle statement is a conclusion because the first statement supports it. It is also support because the author uses it as a reason for the final claim. If the final claim were removed, the middle statement could become the main conclusion of the shorter argument. Its role depends on the whole chain.

## Ask “Why should I believe this?” twice

Take a candidate main conclusion and ask: “Why should I believe this?” If another statement answers, you have found support. Then ask the same question of that supporting statement. If a third statement answers, the one in the middle is an intermediate conclusion.

Consider:

1. People who value wealth often choose better-paid work even when the location is less desirable.
2. What people value is strongly related to how they act.
3. Knowing a person's values can help predict their actions.

Why believe statement 3? Because values and actions are related, as statement 2 says. Why believe statement 2? Statement 1 gives a concrete example of that relationship. The order of support is `1 → 2 → 3`, even if the statements appear in a different order on the page.

Test the reverse direction when two claims both sound like conclusions. “We can predict actions because values and actions correlate” gives a reason. “Values and actions correlate because we can predict actions” does not give the same support. The better support relationship tells you which conclusion is intermediate and which is main.

## Let indicator words guide you, then verify the relationship

*Because*, *since*, *for*, and *after all* often introduce a premise. *So*, *thus*, and *therefore* often introduce a conclusion. *But* often signals a turn in the author's position. Use those words when they appear, but still ask what the statements do for one another. An argument can omit indicators or use one indicator for only part of its structure.

Read references and changes in position carefully. “Many people assume conflicts are inevitable, **but** that assumption is false” means the author concludes conflicts are **not** inevitable. A later explanation that conflicts arise largely from irrational judgments can support that main claim. Examples of judging one bad act harshly while requiring years of good conduct to trust someone support the intermediate claim about irrationality.

```text
Uneven judgments about others
    → people often act irrationally in conflicts
    → conflicts are not inevitable
```

The examples are not the main point. The claim about irrationality is supported by them and supports the author's larger conclusion.

## Keep an “if” from turning into a recommendation

Small wording changes matter when you compare an answer with a conclusion. “**If** people in modern communities cooperated on shared tasks, their communities would probably become closer” describes a predicted result under a condition. It does not say that people **should** undertake those tasks. An answer that turns the condition into a recommendation adds a claim the author did not make.

Once you find the main conclusion, mark it in the stimulus. Then compare each candidate answer directly with its wording. This removes the need to hold the sentence in memory while reading the choices. Check whether the answer preserves qualifications such as *if*, *probably*, and *some*, as well as the author's actual claim.

## Use the structure to find a reasoning gap

There are two links to examine in a three-level argument:

1. Does the premise support the intermediate conclusion?
2. Does the intermediate conclusion support the main conclusion?

An argument can be weak at either link. Identifying the intermediate conclusion lets you say precisely where the support stops. This matters beyond main-conclusion questions because many other questions ask you to assess the argument's reasoning.

## A repeatable process

1. Tell back each statement in plain words. Resolve phrases such as “this process” or “that assumption.”
2. Mark indicator words, if present. Treat them as clues about support, not substitutes for understanding.
3. Ask “Why should I believe this?” of the candidate main claim, then of the statement that supports it.
4. Label the statement that both receives and gives support as the intermediate conclusion.
5. Mark the main conclusion and compare answers against it, checking direction, strength, and any conditional wording.
