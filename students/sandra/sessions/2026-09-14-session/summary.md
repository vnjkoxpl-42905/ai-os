Definition of a Main Conclusion: Joshua Fisseha and Sandra Kooh discuss the definition of a conclusion, noting that unlike an elementary school summary or general main point, an LSAT conclusion is a specific claim made within the argument that represents the author's primary point. Joshua Fisseha explains that conclusions are often mistaken for general summaries, but they represent one specific claim.

Criteria for Identifying a Conclusion: Joshua Fisseha outlines the two essential requirements for a conclusion: it must be the author's opinion rather than a factual claim, and it must be explicitly supported by at least one other statement. Joshua Fisseha uses an analogy of playing by local rules in a legal context to explain that students must accept the premise rules presented in the LSAT framework (00:02:16).

Testing for Conclusions Using Probing Questions: Joshua Fisseha introduces a foolproof method for identifying conclusions by aggressively asking why they should believe a claim and checking if another statement in the stimulus provides a coherent answer (00:04:17) (00:07:24). Using an example about Ethiopia and East Africa, Joshua Fisseha demonstrates how the initial statement that answers this probing question functions as the conclusion while the supporting statement acts as the premise (00:05:07).



Subconclusions and Intermediate Conclusions: Joshua Fisseha explains the concept of a subconclusion, or intermediate conclusion, which serves a dual function by being supported by a premise while simultaneously supporting the main conclusion. Using analogies of a layover flight through Belgium on the way to Rwanda and basketball player hierarchies with LeBron James and Kyrie, Joshua Fisseha and Sandra Kooh examine how intermediate conclusions act as secondary points within the argument structure (00:08:08) (00:11:21).



Premise Indicator Words (FABS): Joshua Fisseha highlights premise indicator words as the most powerful structural tools because a premise cannot exist without being attached to a conclusion. Joshua Fisseha introduces the acronym FABS, standing for "for," "after all," "because," and "since," to help identify premises instantly, noting that an associated conclusion will be positioned directly before or after these indicators (00:15:58) (00:18:30).



Identifying Opposing Viewpoints: Joshua Fisseha describes opposing viewpoints in stimuli as peripheral dirt that authors intend to clear away, comparing authors to narcissists who only introduce external groups, such as scientists, economists, or proponents of critical race theory, to argue that those groups are wrong (00:20:21). Sandra Kooh and Joshua Fisseha discuss how identifying an opposing group's stance automatically signals that the author's conclusion will reject that stance (00:22:34).



Author Approaches to Rebutting Opposing Viewpoints: Joshua Fisseha outlines the two methods authors use to handle opposing viewpoints: a diplomatic concession where authors acknowledge valid points before pivoting to a refutation, or a direct, combative refutation (00:24:46). Joshua Fisseha cautions that granting a minor concession or praising external scholars does not mean authors agree with them, emphasizing that the underlying conclusion remains a rejection of the opposing view (00:25:34).

Rearticulating Implicit Conclusions: Joshua Fisseha and Sandra Kooh analyze an example regarding Ethiopia, poverty, and American dismissals to demonstrate how LSAT questions hide conclusions behind referential pronouns like "they" and "mistaken" (00:29:06). Joshua Fisseha explains that test-takers must rearticulate implicit conclusions by uncovering who "they" refers to and negating the dismissed claim to reveal the author's true stance (00:31:13).



Opinion Indicators and Pivot Words: Joshua Fisseha and Sandra Kooh discuss additional structural markers, noting that opinion indicators like "should" and "ought" signal a high likelihood of a conclusion, while pivot words like "but," "yet," and "however" indicate that authors are changing directions to reject prior statements (00:36:18). Joshua Fisseha illustrates how pivot words introduce either a conclusion followed by a premise or a premise followed by a conclusion (00:37:07).
