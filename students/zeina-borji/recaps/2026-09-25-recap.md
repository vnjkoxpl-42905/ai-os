# Conditional Rules: What They Guarantee and What They Leave Open

A conditional is a rule: **if one condition is met, another must follow**. The left side is the trigger, or sufficient condition. The right side is the required result, or necessary condition. The rule tells you what happens when its trigger is present. It does not tell you that the trigger actually happened.

```text
If A, then B.     A → B
If not B, then not A.     not B → not A
```

The second line is the contrapositive. It reverses and negates both sides, so it carries the same information as the first. Neither `B → A` nor `not A → not B` follows.

## A condition and an assumption answer different questions

A **conditional rule** describes a relationship that the problem gives you. A **sufficient assumption** or **necessary assumption** concerns an argument with evidence and a conclusion. You ask what unstated idea would complete the argument or what it depends on.

The shared words “sufficient” and “necessary” can make the two jobs sound identical. First read the question stem. If it asks what **must be true**, accept the given statements and look for an inference. If it asks for an **assumption**, identify the conclusion, its support, and the gap between them. A conditional may appear in either question, but its presence alone does not tell you which job to do.

For a main-conclusion or argument-role question, your first job is to identify what each claim does in the argument. You do not need to solve every conditional relationship just because one appears in the stimulus.

## A guaranteed path is not the only path

Suppose a fictional admissions rule says:

```text
High LSAT score AND high GPA → admission
```

Having both would guarantee admission under that rule. Having only one gives you no conclusion about admission. Lacking one also does not prove rejection: another route to admission might exist. The rule names a sufficient route, not the only route.

The opposite trap starts with a requirement:

```text
Admitted → completed the application
```

Everyone admitted must have completed the application. Completing it alone does not guarantee admission. A necessary condition is a requirement, not a promise of the result.

## Keep the smaller group inside the larger one

If every violinist in a club is a musician, the violinists are a subset of the musicians.

```text
violinist → musician
not musician → not violinist
```

A musician could play another instrument. The rule does not allow `musician → violinist`. Picture the violinist circle inside the musician circle; do not turn the larger circle into the smaller one.

## Translate the sentence before reasoning from it

1. Find the indicator word or phrase.
2. Write the two complete ideas in ordinary words. Keep a negative such as “cannot” as part of the idea until you apply the translation rule.
3. Put the ideas into an if/then form. Write words rather than single letters while you learn the pattern.
4. Write the contrapositive and check that it says the same thing.
5. Ask what, if anything, in the problem actually activates the rule.

| Wording | Placement | Example |
|---|---|---|
| If, every, whenever | The indicated idea is the trigger. | `Every violinist is a musician` → `violinist → musician` |
| Requires, must, only if | The required idea goes on the right. | `Admission requires an application` → `admitted → applied` |
| Unless, until, without | Negate one idea and place it on the left; the other goes on the right. | `You cannot enter unless you have a pass` → `no pass → cannot enter` |
| No, never, cannot, not both | Put one idea on the left and the negation of the other on the right. | `No cyclists are pilots` → `cyclist → not pilot` |

For “unless,” you can also use the equivalent contrapositive. In the pass example, `enter → pass` says the same thing as `no pass → cannot enter`. Notice that having a pass does **not** prove you entered.

When “cannot” and “unless” appear in the same sentence, keep “cannot enter” as one of the ideas and apply the “unless” translation once. Applying two independent translation rules to the same sentence can change its meaning.

## Know the role before choosing the work

On argument-role questions, ask what the targeted claim does **relative to the main conclusion**. It may be the conclusion, support, an opposing view, or neutral background. An intermediate conclusion receives support and also supports a later claim.

If you are unsure whether a sentence is support, ask of the conclusion: **Why should I believe it?** If the sentence answers that question, it supports the claim. If it merely sets the scene, it is background. An author's judgment can also rebut someone else's view; the labels are not mutually exclusive. Find the supported claim and its relationship first.

Read the question stem before the stimulus so you know which of these jobs the question asks for. During practice, diagram conditional rules even when the current question does not require a full diagram. That builds the recognition you will later use without writing every rule out.
