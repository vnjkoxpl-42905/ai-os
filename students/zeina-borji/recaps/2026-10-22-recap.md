# Know what the sentence gives you

A rule tells you what follows if a condition is met. A fact tells you what is true about a particular situation. Before drawing another arrow, decide which one you have. That small distinction determines what you can conclude.

## Read on three levels

First ask what the sentence does in the argument: does it support another claim, or is it the claim being supported? Then ask what kind of information it contains. Finally, keep its strength. A possible result and a guaranteed result are different conclusions.

| Kind | What it says | Simple example |
|---|---|---|
| Fact | What is true about a particular person, object or situation | This parcel has a blue sticker. |
| Conditional rule | If one condition is met, another must be met | Every blue-sticker parcel must be inspected. |
| Causal claim | One thing produces or changes another | The heat warped the shelf. |
| Correlation | Two things occur together more often | People who commute farther are more likely to arrive late. |
| Normative claim | What should happen, or what is better | The office should open earlier. |
| Quantity claim | How many members of a group fit a description | Most parcels arrived before noon. |

These labels can overlap. A quantity claim can be a premise. A causal claim can be a conclusion. Knowing the topic is not enough; you want to know what the author is doing with it.

The word *for* does not by itself make a sentence conditional. In “The path is muddy, for it rained,” it introduces a reason. In “For entry, a ticket is required,” the requirement gives you the conditional relationship. Read the complete sentence rather than turning every familiar word into an arrow.

## Enough is different from required

Suppose the office has this rule: **Every blue-sticker parcel must be inspected.** A blue sticker is sufficient for inspection under this rule. Knowing the parcel has that sticker is enough to know it has been inspected. Inspection is necessary: the rule does not allow a blue-sticker parcel without it.

**Blue sticker → must be inspected**

An inspection stamp does not prove there was a blue sticker. The office could inspect other parcels too. Being required for one route does not make something a guarantee that the route was taken.

The contrapositive says the same rule another way: **If a parcel has not been inspected, it cannot have a blue sticker.** If it did have one, inspection would have to come with it. Reverse the two complete conditions and negate both. You do not need a diagram that looks positive; you need one that says exactly what the original says.

## Two jobs, with different inputs

### 1. Apply a rule to a fact

Rule: Blue sticker → must be inspected.

Fact: This parcel has a blue sticker.

Result: This parcel must be inspected. The fact supplies the left side of the arrow.

Change the fact to “This parcel has not been inspected.” Now the contrapositive tells you it cannot have a blue sticker.

Change it again to “This parcel has been inspected.” The rule does not settle whether it has a blue sticker. Nor does the absence of a sticker settle whether it has been inspected. In those two cases, keep the uncertainty.

### 2. Link rules to make another rule

Rule 1: Blue sticker → must be inspected.

Rule 2: Inspected → must be logged.

The complete shared idea is *inspected*. The result required by the first rule meets the condition that starts the second.

New rule: **Blue sticker → must be logged.**

We still do not know that any parcel has a blue sticker. Rules can connect without telling us that their starting condition occurred. If a fact later supplies a blue-sticker parcel, we can apply the whole chain to it.

You explained why a finding of guilt did not tell us what specific act had occurred. That was the distinction we needed: the required result can be present for another reason.

## Count things without adding an upper limit

With five cookies, *some are chocolate* means at least one. All five could be chocolate. *Most are chocolate* means more than half: three, four or five. *All* means all five.

| Word | What it requires | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| Some | At least one | Whether most or all fit |
| Most | More than half | Whether all fit |
| All | Every member | No exception within the group |

For a group known to contain members, all implies most and some; most implies some. You cannot go back upward. One chocolate cookie does not prove a majority. Use *more than half*, rather than memorizing 51 percent: 50.5 percent is most too.

## Possibility is not a head count

“Some parcels arrived” counts parcels. “The parcel could arrive” describes a possibility. Keep those questions separate.

| Wording | What it says |
|---|---|
| Could or might | It is possible; it is not ruled out |
| Must | It is required by the information |
| Cannot | It is ruled out |
| Not necessarily | It is not guaranteed; it could fail to happen |

You do not need percentage ranges for these words. They are not numerical forecasts. Also, not knowing that something happens is not the same as proving that it cannot happen. A rule that leaves a case unsettled does not, by itself, prove a specific real-world possibility either.

## Match the reasoning before the subject

For parallel questions, find the conclusion and name its type and strength. Then name how the premises were used. “It took a required condition as enough” is more useful than “It was about participation.”

If the original uses **if A then B; B; therefore A**, look for that same reversal. The fact must supply the required side, and the conclusion must infer the sufficient side. A valid application of a rule is not parallel to this mistake.

Before the next sentence of an answer, predict what it would need to say. Then check the prediction. Weak wording alone does not make an answer match; *might* still needs the same reasoning behind it.

## Say it first, then check

1. Rule: Every blue-sticker parcel must be inspected. Fact: This parcel was not inspected. What follows?
2. Five cookies: some are chocolate. Could all five be chocolate? Must all five be chocolate?
3. Blue sticker → inspected. Inspected → logged. Do these rules prove that a blue-sticker parcel exists?

### Answers

1. It cannot have a blue sticker. This is the contrapositive.
2. All five could be chocolate, but all five are not required. One would satisfy “some.”
3. No. They give the new rule blue sticker → logged, without supplying a fact that starts it.
