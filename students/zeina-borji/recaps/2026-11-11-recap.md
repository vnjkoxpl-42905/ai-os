Session recap · November 11

What the Author

Has to Assume

On a necessary assumption question, it is easy to pick an answer because it helps the argument. Ask how much the author actually needs. Find the gap between the evidence and the conclusion, then see what breaks when you treat an answer as false.

Read it first

Say what the author is trying to prove

Before you look at the choices, say the conclusion and the evidence in plain words. On the harder questions, a detail you skip can change what the author needs.

Find the conclusion. What does the author want you to believe?

Name the evidence. What reason did the author give you?

Identify the reasoning. Is the author applying a rule, claiming a cause, or doing both?

Ask what could break the argument. Say one way the evidence could be true while the conclusion no longer follows.

Find the gap

Ask what the author has to accept or rule out

When an argument applies a rule, look for a missing connection. If only paid members can vote and the author concludes that Lina can vote, check what the evidence actually says about Lina's membership. When the author gives a cause, ask what other explanation could make sense of the same evidence.

Both can show up together. If someone says a pill cured a problem that can only be resolved by reducing stress, the pill and stress reduction may be part of the same explanation. The pill could reduce stress.

You asked whether one argument could be conditional and causal at the same time. That was the right question in the medicine example. The rule gave you a limit, and the possible effect of the medicine gave you the gap to test.

Test the choice

Take away exactly what the answer says

Negate the answer you are considering. Treat it as false. “Some buses arrived late” becomes “No buses arrived late.”

Go back to the author's reasoning. Keep the evidence in place. Would the author lose a reason they need for that conclusion?

Watch how much the answer asks you to accept

Slow down when you see most or primarily. If the author needs only one example or one connection, a claim about most cases adds a burden the argument can do without.

Try it

What would make the whole statement false?

Every library branch opens on Monday.

At least one

At least one. At least one library branch does not open on Monday. You only need one exception to defeat “every.”

A countercase

A countercase. The alarm can sound while the doors stay locked. Keep the if part true and make what follows false.
