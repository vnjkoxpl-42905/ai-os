Keep the Reasoning.Shorten the Story.

A shorter version should make the argument easier to inspect. Keep the conclusion, the reason and the details that control their relationship. Those are the pieces an abstract answer must describe.

Start with the target

What are you being asked to believe?

A conclusion is the claim the author wants you to accept. Evidence gives reasons for it. Once you know the conclusion, return to the evidence and ask how those reasons are supposed to get you there.

Suppose an author says a whole festival went over budget, so its children's workshop must have gone over budget. You don't need to know the festival's name. You do need to keep the difference between the whole festival and one workshop.

EvidenceThe whole festival went over budget.

→ConclusionOne workshop went over budget.

The music stage could have caused the overspend while the workshop stayed within budget. The evidence can be true while the conclusion is false.

Simplify carefully

Keep what controls the reasoning

The story

Names and setting can often be shortened: a festival, a workshop, a budget. Understand the original meaning before replacing them.

The relationship

Keep whole versus part, some versus all, sometimes versus always, and what is offered as a reason for what. Those details can decide the answer.

Generalizing is not just replacing nouns with letters. You can use short ordinary labels. Your version should be simpler while leaving the author making the same claim for the same reason.

On the project question, you identified that the evidence concerned the whole while the conclusion concerned a part. Keep trusting that distinction. It is exactly what the argument needed us to track.

Read the direction

Whole to part is different from part to whole

Whole to partThe whole festival was expensive, so this workshop was expensive.

Part to wholeThis workshop was expensive, so the whole festival was expensive.

Neither inference is guaranteed by the information shown. But they are different errors. If an answer describes evidence about each part supporting a conclusion about the whole, it does not match an argument going from the whole to one part.

Some properties do transfer. If every object in a box is red, a particular object in that box is red. That evidence directly covers each object. Don't reject every sentence involving a group; check what the evidence actually says.

Keep the time

Missing now does not mean never there

Someone says a recording was deleted. The reply is, “There is no recording in the folder now.” Could both claims be true?

Think first, then check the reply

Yes. Deleting the recording could explain why it is missing now. Its current absence does not show it never existed, and it does not clear someone of deleting it.

The reply describes the current situation. The accusation concerns what happened earlier. Shortening both to “no recording” would erase the difference the question tests.

You gave this distinction yourself: something could be missing because it was destroyed. You also explained it without relying on the original topic.

Check whether the reason is new

A conclusion cannot support itself

“This bakery is the best because its bread is better than every other bakery's bread.” The reason has restated the judgment. It hasn't offered an independent reason to believe it.

That is circular reasoning: the support already assumes what the author is supposed to prove. Compare it with “This bakery won a blind tasting against the other bakeries.” That gives a separate reason, even if we still need to inspect how good the test was.

Before naming the flawSay what the author wants you to accept, then say what the author gives you. If the second just repeats the first, there is no independent support.

Keep the amount

Sometimes does not mean always

Suppose two duties are said to apply without exception, but one situation makes it impossible to fulfil both. That single situation challenges the claim that both duties can always be fulfilled together.

It does not show that the duties can never be fulfilled together. A different situation might allow both. In parallel reasoning, keep that difference when comparing conclusions.

Try the distinction

Explain before opening

A whole orchestra is loud. Therefore its quietest instrument is loud. What is the direction?

Whole to part. The conclusion concerns one instrument, but the evidence concerns the orchestra together. Other instruments could account for the volume.

The museum is trustworthy because everything it says can be trusted. Is there an independent reason?

No. The reason repeats the claim of trustworthiness. It gives no separate support for believing it.

Two tasks conflict on one afternoon. Does that show they can never be completed together?

No. It shows at least one conflict. Other afternoons might allow both. Preserve “sometimes” rather than replacing it with “never.”

When the wording gets dense

Translate, then match both halves

State the conclusion in a complete short sentence.

State the evidence and keep its group, time and strength.

Describe the gap in ordinary language. Try a case where the evidence is true and the conclusion fails.

Translate the answer. Point to exactly what each part describes in the argument.

Check the direction. The same two ideas in the wrong roles do not match.

Practise the explanation, not just the letter
