# Reading Comprehension: Map What Each Paragraph Does

The passage stays in front of you when you answer the questions. Your first read should give you a map of its argument: where each viewpoint appears, what each paragraph does, and which claim the passage is built to support. You can return for a date or detail when a question needs it.

## Give each paragraph a job

At the end of a paragraph, pause and tell it back in your own words. Ask what the author is doing, not just what the paragraph is about.

| Move | What to look for | What a useful note might say |
|---|---|---|
| Compare | Two ideas, theories, or viewpoints are set beside each other. | `P1: A says yes; B says no` |
| Explain | The author develops one idea or gives the basis for a position. | `P2: why A thinks so` |
| Evaluate | The author assesses an idea or points out a strength or flaw. | `P3: problem with A's reason` |
| Recommend | The author proposes what should be done or which approach to adopt. | `P4: adopt B's approach` |

A paragraph may make more than one move. Record the one or two that tell you why it is there. The note should be short enough to act as a locator, not a second copy of the passage.

On scratch paper, a three-paragraph passage might become:

```text
P1  Compare: A says the price collapse was a bubble; B disagrees
P2  Explain A: the sharp rise and fall
P3  Explain B: reproduction increased supply; lower prices can still yield returns
```

This map tells you where to look when a question asks for A's evidence, B's reply, or the purpose of paragraph two. You can mark the first mention of each viewpoint in a distinct color. Mark the change in speaker, not every sentence attributed to that speaker.

## Separate an event from its explanation

Two thinkers can agree that prices rose and then fell while disagreeing about *why*. A rapid rise and fall is part of a speculative-bubble account, but it does not prove speculation caused the change. A new product can start scarce and expensive, then become cheaper as more units are produced.

In the tulip example, the competing accounts shared the price history. One treated the rise and collapse as evidence of speculative hype. The other said the original bulbs could still be a rational purchase because they produced many descendant bulbs. Those descendants could sell at lower prices while providing a reasonable total return.

Keep the shared fact and disputed explanation on separate lines:

```text
Agreed: price rose, then fell.
Disputed: speculative demand or ordinary production and supply?
```

This prevents a common mistake: assuming that someone who rejects an explanation must also reject the event being explained.

## Find the passage's main point, then check every word of the answer

A main point is the claim the passage works to establish. It is not a list of topics or a summary that two people disagree. In a debate passage, ask which position receives the fuller explanation and how the author frames the competing view. That can help you locate the favored claim, especially when the author does not say “I agree.” Treat the amount of discussion as a clue, then verify the stance against the passage.

Read the candidate answer literally. Each part needs support. Watch for an answer that:

- turns a claim about two scholars into a claim about what *all economists* believe;
- says a scholar made a particular calculation when the passage never says they did;
- treats every price rise and fall as a speculative bubble, leaving out the proposed cause; or
- gives a reason for the favored view while missing the view's conclusion.

The same discipline helps with paragraph-purpose questions. First predict the move from your map: “This paragraph explains the basis for A's inference.” Then compare the answers. “Explains A's inference” differs from “undermines A” and from “identifies A's factual errors.” A later paragraph can challenge A's explanation even when the paragraph describing A does neither of those things.

## Use indicator words to follow the argument

Words such as *but*, *yet*, and *however* often mark a turn. *For*, *because*, and *since* often introduce support. *Thus* and *therefore* often introduce a conclusion. Their function in Reading Comprehension is the same as in Logical Reasoning: they help you see which statement supports which claim and where a viewpoint changes.

When you see a pivot followed by a reason, put the pieces together. For example: “The individual bulbs became cheaper, **but** the original purchase was not irrational, **for** one bulb could produce many bulbs for sale.” The claim after *but* is supported by the explanation after *for*.

## A repeatable first read

1. After each paragraph, tell back its main claim and label its move.
2. Mark where a viewpoint begins or changes. Note what the sides agree on and the precise point of disagreement.
3. At the end, identify the claim the passage supports. Use the structure and the author's treatment of each view as evidence.
4. For each question, predict the kind of answer your map calls for. Return to the passage for details and reject any answer whose wording goes beyond it.
