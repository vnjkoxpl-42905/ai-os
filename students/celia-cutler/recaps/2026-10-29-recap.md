# Choose the Answer With the Fewest Problems

Some questions have a rule that settles the answer. Most Strongly Supported and Weaken don't. You weigh the choices, and none of them will be perfect. That's why these questions feel flimsy, and it's why the goal changes: find the answer with the fewest problems.

## Name what each sentence is doing
*Before the answers*

On both questions you brought, the first job was the same: read each sentence and say what kind of claim it is. Is it a rule, a cause, a quantity? Each kind pushes the right answer in a different direction, so naming them lets you predict what the answer will play with.

**Conditional.** A hard line. If the first idea is true, the second one has to be true. There's nothing in between.
Switch: On the guest list: Everyone on the guest list gets a wristband. Not on the list: Not on the list? That tells you nothing about the wristband.
Sounds like: Everyone on the guest list gets a wristband.
Test: Wristband on? That tells you nothing about the list. Not on the list? That tells you nothing about the wristband.

**Causal.** A dial, not a switch. Think of a volume knob: more of the cause makes the effect more likely, and less of it makes the effect less likely.
Dial, daily practice: More practice makes faster reading more likely. Less practice makes faster reading less likely. No practice? Faster reading gets less likely. It doesn't become impossible.
Sounds like: Daily practice leads to faster reading.
Test: No practice? Faster reading gets less likely. It doesn't become impossible.

**A conditional chain.** Two rules that share an idea link into a new rule. Without the first idea, you learn nothing new about the last.
**A causal chain.** Without the cause, the effect is less likely. If the effect keeps showing up with no cause around, doubt the cause.

**When you see a pivot.** But, yet and however mark a point of tension between two ideas. The right answer almost always settles that tension. Look at the idea just before the pivot and the idea just after it.

Words: but, yet, however

**Point of tension**
The idea just before the pivot / but / The idea just after it

## The stronger the answer, the heavier the lift
*Most Strongly Supported*

A Most Strongly Supported question never gives you an argument. It gives you information, and the right answer is the conclusion that information supports. Treat it like a Must Be True question with a lighter touch.

Picture an Olympic weightlifter. The stimulus is the lifter. Each answer is a weight. The heavier the weight, the less likely he gets it overhead. So lean toward the answer that asks the least of what you've read.

**The weightlifter**
Pick a word an answer might use. Watch what it asks of the stimulus.
Words: can, most, every, always, inevitably
- can: Asks for very little. Easy to support.
- most: Soft enough to be supported. You are looking for most supported.
- every: Reaches past what the stimulus said. Check it is there.
- always: Needs the stimulus to say it just as strongly.
- inevitably: Inevitably means every time. Too heavy unless the stimulus says so.

**Check the verb.** Say a stimulus tells you that construction noise pulls students' attention away from their reading. That supports the noise can make reading harder. It doesn't support students who hear it inevitably forget what they just read. Pulling attention away is not the same as losing it, and inevitably means every time, with no exceptions.

Words: inevitably, always, never, every, must

The stimulus is your whole data set. If an answer reaches for a bigger idea, or a different idea that sounds close, it has added something. A soft answer still has to be about what the stimulus said. A strong word is fine when the stimulus says it just as strongly.

The right answer also doesn't have to be perfect. Say a stimulus tells you that background chatter pulls attention away from a task. An answer saying chatter in the room can make an exam slightly harder to finish is supported. Can and slightly ask for very little. You're not looking for guaranteed. You're looking for most supported.

> You picked out inevitably as the strong word on your own, and you said what it means: it always happens. Keep looking for that word first.

## Ask which world the answer is about
*Weaken*

Weaken questions are argument questions. They mostly run on cause or comparison, so you won't often get a rule to take apart.

Many of these arguments end with a plan. A city says downtown workers drive because biking feels unsafe, so a protected bike lane will make fewer of them drive.

**Add a bike lane → fewer workers drive**

That's a prediction about a new world, one that doesn't exist yet. Your job is to find a way the prediction could fail even if the city builds the lane.

**Two worlds**
Which world is the answer about? Tap one.
- The world as it isWhere the author starts.
- The new worldFewer workers drive. That is the prediction.
- Add a bike lane
- An answer about the world as it is
- An answer about the new world
- An answer about the world as it is: A trap. An answer about how things are right now says nothing about the new world.
- An answer about the new world: Better. Find a way the prediction could fail even if the city builds the lane.

The people don't change. Maybe the workers live too far to bike. Maybe they never notice the lane.

A little is enough for the author. If a few more workers bike, the prediction holds. So many workers already ride doesn't hurt it. You'd need the workers already ride as much as they possibly can.

**Land the heaviest blow.** A good Weaken answer hits the prediction hard and directly. Answers about some people, or one case, are rarely heavy enough. That's a tendency in how these tests are built, not a law.

> You pointed out that a single failed case is enough to break a conditional claim. That's valid. Keep it, and add a second check: the answer also has to hit exactly what the conclusion says.

**A trap: the world as it is.** An answer about how things are right now says nothing about the new world. If I tell you practice in the morning and you'll read faster, and you answer I already read fast, you haven't touched my claim. Ask which world each answer is about.

One more check. Some effects arrive over time, as things wear out and get replaced. If an answer seems to change nothing right away, run the new world forward and compare the whole of it with the whole of the old one.

## The answer with the fewest problems wins
*Both question types*

Neither question hands you a perfect answer. A Most Strongly Supported answer doesn't have to be guaranteed. A Weaken answer doesn't have to knock the argument over. It has to push further than the others do.

So cross out the answers with obvious problems first. Then compare what's left by how many problems each one has, and take the one with the fewest. Process of elimination is your best tool here. Strengthen works the same way. It's the flimsiest of the three, and the one I find hardest.

**Process of elimination**
Step through how one question gets decided.
- Five answers. None of them is perfect.
- Cross out the answers with obvious problems first.
- Compare what is left by how many problems each one has.
- Take the one with the fewest problems. It will not be perfect.

**Spend your time where it counts.** Some questions are hard enough that you can afford to miss them. What costs you points is missing the ones you should get. When a question is this slippery, give yourself room and keep moving.

## When no answer will be perfect
*The checklist*

1. Know what you're holding. Information on Most Strongly Supported. An argument on Weaken.
2. Name each claim. Conditional, causal or quantity. Mark any pivot.
3. Say the range. What could the right answer play with? For a plan, what's the old world and what's the new one?
4. Check each answer against the words on the page. On Most Strongly Supported, look for strong words and swapped-in ideas. On Weaken, look for the wrong world, a light blow, or some.
5. Take the answer with the fewest problems. It won't be perfect.
