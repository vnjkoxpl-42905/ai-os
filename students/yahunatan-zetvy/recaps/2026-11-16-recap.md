# Reading an Argument in Three Steps

When a question won't click, you're usually missing one of five points. Three of them live in the stimulus: where the argument starts and ends, how it gets from one to the other, and what it leaves out. Run them on every argument and you'll see exactly where you got stuck.

## Five points, and where they sit

Here are five points to run on Logical Reasoning questions. Three of them work on the stimulus. Two work on the answer choices. This note covers the three on the stimulus.

1. **Structure.** Where does the argument start, and where does it end?
2. **Method of reasoning.** How does the author get from the start to the end?
3. **Assumption.** What did the author leave out?

The other two points are keyword mapping and putting abstract answer choices in plain English. They're in the next note, Mapping Abstract Answers Back to the Argument.

> **The steps show you where you got stuck.** When a question isn't connecting, run them in order. The first one you can't answer is the one to work on. After a miss, do the same. It takes a lot of practice to get good at this, so use it on every argument.

## Find where the argument starts and ends

An argument starts with evidence, which is the premise, and ends with the conclusion. On the page the conclusion can come first, but the reasoning still runs from the evidence to it. That's the structure. Here's a short one.

Some people say the new bus line is too slow. A few routes do run late. But it cut the average commute by ten minutes, so the city should keep it.

> **Evidence:** It cut the average commute by ten minutes.
> **Conclusion:** The city should keep the new bus line.

Two sentences in there aren't the evidence or the conclusion. Mark them anyway, because they show you where the author is headed. Here are four kinds of sentence like that. The first two are in the passage above. The last two show up in other arguments.

- **Opposing view.** What other people say. The author brings it up to push back on it. “Some people say the new bus line is too slow.”
- **Concession.** The author grants a point, then pushes back. “A few routes do run late.”
- **Background information.** Context that sets the scene. It isn't a reason for the conclusion.
- **Intermediate conclusion.** A claim that has support and also gives support. If it has support, it's a conclusion. If it gives support, it's a premise. If it does both, it's an intermediate conclusion.

To test whether a claim is a conclusion, ask yourself why you should believe it. If the author gives you a reason, it's a conclusion.

## Then name how the author gets there

You can get from Florida to Canada by plane, train or bus. Any of them works if you do it properly. An argument is the same. Say the start is that you study hard and the finish is a 160. There's more than one way to get from one to the other.

**Conditional.** Uses a rule. If one thing is true, the other has to be. Looks like: If you study hard, you'll score a 160. Test: Is something that's only required being treated as enough?

**Causal.** Says one thing produces another. Looks like: Studying hard will cause you to score a 160. Test: Does the evidence show a cause, or only that the two go together?

**Comparative.** Says what happened to one case will happen to another. Looks like: Richard studied the way you do and scored a 160, so you will too. Test: Are the two cases alike where it matters?

Those are the most common. There are others, like generalizing from a group (“Everyone in the study group who studied hard scored a 160”) or ruling out alternatives. The list isn't complete. Name whichever you see. Sometimes it's more than one.

> **Name the method and you know where it breaks.** In a flawed argument, the conclusion goes wrong somewhere, and the method points at the spot. “People who study hard are more likely to score a 160” is a correlation. “Studying hard will cause a 160” is a cause. They aren't the same claim.

> **Evidence:** People who study hard are more likely to score a 160.
> **Doesn't follow:** Studying hard will cause you to score a 160.

> **What you said:** “This reminds me of flaws ... if it's a comparison, then I'm looking for that in the answer choice.”
>
> That's the link. The method tells you which flaw to expect, and you already do this on flaw questions.

## Then find what the author left out

An assumption is a missing piece of evidence. The author left it out, so the evidence doesn't guarantee the conclusion. If the argument already says it, it isn't the assumption.

A common mistake with a conditional is treating a requirement as if it were a guarantee, or treating something that is enough as if it were required.

> **Evidence:** Studying hard is required to score a 160. You study hard.
> **Assumption:** If you study hard, you'll score a 160.
> **Conclusion:** You'll score a 160.

Required isn't enough. Put the missing sentence back and the argument works. Leave it out and the conclusion doesn't follow.

A license is required to rent a car. Having one doesn't get you a car. You still need a deposit and a car on the lot.

> **What you said:** “It doesn't mean that you're going to get a 160 ... You can't say that it guarantees anything.”
>
> That's it. A requirement is something you need. It doesn't guarantee the result.

### All three steps on one argument

Some meals that critics call plain taste wonderful. A meal is excellent only if it tastes wonderful. So some meals that critics call plain are excellent.

1. **Structure.** The conclusion is that some of those meals are excellent. The two sentences before it are the evidence.
2. **Method of reasoning.** The second sentence is a conditional. As an if/then: if a meal is excellent, then it tastes wonderful. The contrapositive: if it doesn't taste wonderful, it isn't excellent. Those two say the same thing. “If it tastes wonderful, it's excellent” says something different, and the rule doesn't give it to you.
3. **Assumption.** The conclusion treats tasting wonderful as if it made a meal excellent.

> **Evidence:** Some meals that critics call plain taste wonderful. A meal is excellent only if it tastes wonderful.
> **Assumption:** If a meal tastes wonderful, it's excellent.
> **Conclusion:** Some meals that critics call plain are excellent.

Tasting wonderful is required for excellent. The argument needs it to be enough. You caught the same swap on a real question in the session. The conclusion replaced one idea with another and treated them as the same thing.

## Check yourself

**Say it out loud, then tap to check** Which part of the argument is each sentence?

*Some say the museum should keep its entry fee. It's true that fees bring in money. But free entry would double attendance, so the museum should drop the fee.*

- Some say the museum should keep its entry fee. **Opposing view.** The author mentions it to push back on it.
- It's true that fees bring in money. **Concession.** The author grants a point, then pushes back.
- Free entry would double attendance. **Evidence.** It's the reason the author gives for the conclusion.
- The museum should drop the fee. **Conclusion.** It's where the argument ends, and the evidence is the reason to believe it.

**Name the method** Is each one conditional, causal, comparative or something else?

- If the pool is open, the lifeguard is on duty. **Conditional.** It's an if/then rule.
- Heavy traffic makes people late. **Causal.** It says one thing produces another.
- Ana trains the way Maya does, and Maya ran a marathon. So Ana will run one too. **Comparative.** It says what happened to one case will happen to another.
- Every student in the study group who practiced daily improved, so you will too. **Generalization.** It takes what happened to a group and applies it to you.
- People who study hard tend to score higher, so studying hard causes higher scores. **Causal.** The evidence is only a correlation, so the conclusion breaks.

**Find the assumption** Which one is the missing piece?

*Only members may use the pool. Lena is a member. So Lena can use the pool today.*

- Lena can use the pool today. **Conclusion.** It's where the argument ends.
- Only members may use the pool. **Evidence.** It's stated, so it can't be the assumption.
- Being a member is enough to use the pool today. **Assumption.** Nobody said it, and the conclusion needs it. A requirement isn't a guarantee.
- Lena is a member. **Evidence.** It's stated too.

## The three steps, in order

Do this in the stimulus, before you read the answer choices.

*Stimulus on the left. Choices on the right.*

1. **Mark the structure.** Find the conclusion, then the evidence. Note any opposing viewpoint or concession.
2. **Name the method.** Conditional, causal, comparative or something else. Say it out loud: “This is a conditional.”
3. **Ask what's missing.** With a conditional, is something required being treated as enough? With a cause, is the evidence only a correlation? With a comparison, are the two cases alike where it matters?

# Mapping Abstract Answers Back to the Argument

The LSAT writes many answer choices abstractly. Say the same thing in plain English and it's usually easy to see. The last two points do that: put each abstract word in plain English, then find each keyword in the argument.

## Put the abstract word in plain English

When an answer choice sounds abstract, swap the abstract word for a plain one. Plain English and keyword mapping both work on the answer choices only. They don't apply to the stimulus.

- **Explanation.** Means causation. What's explained is the effect. The explanation is the cause.
- **Illustration.** Means example. A specific instance of a general idea.
- **Presupposes, assumes, supposes.** Think assumption: something the author takes for granted and never says.
- **Justify.** Think evidence or premise: the reason given for the conclusion.
- **Principle.** A general rule that applies to many cases, not just one.

> **What you said:** “I always see them say justify, like all the time.”
>
> Then justify goes on your list. You'll see the same words again and again.

> **Build your own list of these words.** Write each abstract word with its plain meaning beside it. Add to it every time you meet a new one.

> **An assumption is never stated.** On an assumption question, an answer that only repeats something the argument already says can't be the assumption. An assumption is a belief the author never says.

## Then find each keyword in the argument

Keyword mapping means taking the key words in an answer choice and finding them in the argument. When an answer describes the argument, as on flaw, method and role questions, and you can't find one, that part of the answer doesn't describe this argument. On strengthen, weaken and assumption questions, a right answer can add something new. Check each answer for these:

1. **Nouns.** If the answer says “a certain property,” which property? Name it, then find it in the argument.
2. **Adjectives.** Does the adjective match what the argument actually says?
3. **What the author does.** If the answer says the author claims something, does the author really claim it?
4. **A form of logic.** If the answer mentions a conditional or a cause, the argument has to contain one. A correlation isn't a cause.
5. **Abstract words.** Put each in plain English before you look for the others, using your list.

Here's one in action.

> **Evidence:** A license is required to rent a car at this shop. Dev has a license.
> **Doesn't follow:** Dev can rent a car at this shop.

Say one answer choice is: “It takes a condition that's necessary for an outcome to be sufficient for it.”

1. **Plain English.** A requirement treated as if it were enough.
2. **Find the nouns.** The outcome is renting a car. The condition is having a license.
3. **Check the form of logic.** The argument is a conditional, so this answer is a contender.

Say another is: “It concludes that every member of a group has a trait because a few do.” There's no group and no sample here, so the nouns don't map. Not this one.

> **Naming the method narrows the choices.** That's why you name the method of reasoning first. If the argument is only about a cause, an answer about necessary and sufficient conditions has nothing to match.

## Strong words are harder for the argument to prove

Words to slow down on: most, all, every.

Most means more than half. All and every mean no exceptions. The more an answer claims, the more the argument has to prove. So slow down when you see these words. It's a reason to check carefully, not a reason to cut the answer.

- **Must Be True and Most Strongly Supported.** Lean toward weaker answers. The stronger the claim, the less likely the argument guarantees it.
- **Necessary assumption.** Lean weak. It's the least the argument needs.
- **Sufficient assumption.** Strong wording is fine. It has to be enough.
- **Flaw and Method answers.** The answer has to describe the argument accurately. If it says every society, the argument has to be about every society.

Weak wording still has to be relevant, and strong wording is sometimes required. Judge the idea, not only the word.

## Check yourself

**Say it out loud, then tap to check** What does each phrase say in plain English?

- The argument offers an explanation of the trend. **Cause.** An explanation is a cause. The trend is the effect.
- The author gives an illustration of the idea. **Example.** An illustration is a specific example of a general idea.
- The author presupposes that sales were steady. **Assumption.** It's something the author takes for granted and never says.
- The author cites the survey to justify the claim. **Evidence.** The survey is the reason given for the claim.
- The author appeals to a principle. **General rule.** It applies to many cases, not just this one.

**Does the answer describe this argument?** Find the answer's keywords in the passage.

*The café's sales rose after it changed its menu. So the new menu caused the rise.*

- It concludes that one thing caused another because the first came earlier. **Matches.** There's a cause in the passage. The menu change is the cause and the rise in sales is the effect.
- It applies a rule about a group to one member of that group. **Not this one.** There's no group and no rule in the passage to find.
- It treats something required as if it were enough. **Not this one.** Nothing in the passage is required. There's no conditional to find.
- It rejects an opposing view without answering its evidence. **Not this one.** No one disagrees with the author here, so there's no opposing view to find.

**How much does each answer claim?** Which one claims the least, and which claims the most?

- Most customers liked the new menu. **Stronger.** More than half have to have liked it.
- Every customer liked the new menu. **Strongest.** One customer who didn't like it makes it false.
- Some customers liked the new menu. **Weakest.** One customer is enough to prove it.

## Before you commit to an answer

*Stimulus on the left. Choices on the right.*

1. **Run the three stimulus steps first.** Structure, method of reasoning, assumption. They tell you what a good answer has to match.
2. **Find the abstract words.** Put each in plain English.
3. **Map the keywords back.** Nouns, adjectives, what the author does, the form of logic. On flaw, method and role questions, if you can't find one in the argument, that answer isn't describing it.
4. **Watch for strong words.** Most, all and every ask a lot. Check them carefully.
