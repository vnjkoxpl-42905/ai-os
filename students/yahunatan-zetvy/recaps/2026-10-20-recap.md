# Necessary Assumptions and Negation

A necessary assumption is something the argument needs for its reasoning to work. Start with the evidence and the exact conclusion. Then ask what connection the author is taking for granted.

## Split the argument in two

First, identify what the author wants you to believe. Then identify the reasons given for it.

**The evidence** Accept every word as true. You don't fight it.

**The conclusion** Ask whether the evidence supports it. Do not take it as proven.

Grant nothing that isn't written

Common sense will fill in the gaps for you, and that's exactly what the test is counting on. **Primary doesn't mean only.** Most means more than half, but does not guarantee all. If it isn't on the page, the author doesn't get it.

Finding the gap

## What the author never said

The town promised to repave Elm Street. Not one block has been finished this month. So the town has broken its promise.

The author treats no finished block this month as proof that the promise is broken. But a slow start doesn't show that a promise was broken unless the job had to show progress within a month. The author never said that.

The assumption

**If the town were keeping its promise, at least one block would be finished within a month.** Nobody said how long the promise was supposed to take. Maybe a block takes six weeks.

On the audit question, after we separated the conclusion from its evidence, you noticed that the crackdown plan had no stated one-year deadline. The same check matters here: was this month actually the deadline?

Before you test the answers

## Pay attention to answer strength

Think of the old man who keeps the bills paid. He doesn't need to be the life of the party. He just makes sure the lights stay on. A necessary assumption is like that: boring, conservative, just enough to keep the argument running. That's why correct answers often sound weak: **some, may, might, at least one.** The stronger the claim, the less likely the argument actually requires it.

Take this argument: **The prize went to a member of the hiking club. So the prize went to someone who lives nearby.** What do we need to know about the club?

Some members live nearby

The argument needs at least this much. If no club members lived nearby, the prize could not have gone to both a club member and someone nearby.

**What it leaves open** Other members could live far away. We haven't shown that the particular member who won lives nearby, so this answer doesn't prove the conclusion on its own.

Most or all members live nearby

These say more. Most means more than half. All means every member. The conclusion is about one winner, so we don't need either claim about the rest of the club.

**Try it** Suppose the winner lives nearby and the other ninety-nine members live far away. The conclusion can still be true. Most and all were not required.

Be wary of strong answers

With words such as all, always, most and primarily, ask: **does the author really need that much?** Often the argument only needs a smaller claim. Strong wording isn't automatically wrong, and weak wording doesn't make an answer relevant. Check how much this argument needs.

A plain sentence can make a strong claim

*Alex will go to Tokyo next summer* gives a definite prediction. *Alex will probably go to Tokyo next summer* leaves room for him to stay home. You don't need a word such as all or always to make a definite claim. A word can sound casual and still make a strong claim. If an answer states something flatly, with no probably or may, read it as a firm commitment and ask whether the author needs that much.

Sufficient assumption questions ask for enough to prove the conclusion when added to the evidence. In our club example, **all club members live nearby** would prove that the winner lives nearby. It's sufficient, but it isn't necessary. Stronger language can do useful work when the task asks you to prove the conclusion.

Now check an answer

## Negate it: treat it as not true

Negate an answer you are considering. That means treating it as not true. Leave the author's evidence alone. Then ask whether that evidence can still support the conclusion.

Our answer says **some hiking-club members live nearby.** Treat that as not true: **none of them lives nearby.** Now the evidence says a club member won, but no club member lives nearby. The author's conclusion no longer works. That's why the answer is necessary.

Notice that we did not turn some into most or all. To make some false, there must be none. To make all false, one exception is enough. Work out what would make the answer false, rather than choosing a much stronger opposite.

Keep these separate

## Negation and the contrapositive do different jobs

Start with the same sentence for both: **If the café is busy, Sam works late.**

The contrapositive says the same thing

**If Sam does not work late, the café is not busy.** Why? A busy café would mean Sam works late. If Sam did not work late, the café could not have been busy. We have said the original rule another way.

**Use it** When you want to know what the rule tells you.

The negation makes it false

**The café is busy, and Sam does not work late.** That's exactly what the original sentence rules out. Both things happen together: the café is busy, but Sam does not do what the sentence said he would do.

**Use it** When you want to test an assumption by treating it as not true.

If the café was quiet, Sam could work late or leave early without breaking the original sentence. It only told us what happens when the café is busy. That's why the negation needs a busy café and Sam not working late.

You wrote the New York and US contrapositive yourself, then distinguished that from negation. That is the difference to keep: saying the same rule another way versus making the original false.

## Negate it

### Say the negation out loud, then tap to check

Say what would make each sentence false. Then check your answer.

* All of the guests arrived. Answer: Not all of the guests arrived
* Some of the guests arrived. Answer: None of the guests arrived
* The shop is open on Monday. Answer: The shop is not open on Monday
* If it rains, the game is off. Answer: It rains, and the game is still on

### What is actually required?

The club's rule says that a person may vote only if registered. The author concludes that Maya may vote. What must be true?

Maya is registered.

Required. Negate it: Maya is not registered. The rule says an unregistered person may not vote. Registration alone doesn't prove permission, but it is necessary.

Every registered person may vote.

Not required. Other registered people could be barred while Maya is allowed. The conclusion concerns Maya, not every registered person.

## On every necessary assumption question

1. **Separate the reasons from the conclusion.** Accept the reasons as true. Ask what they actually tell you about the conclusion.
2. **Find the gap.** What does the conclusion need that the evidence never said?
3. **Check how much the answer claims.** Some is less demanding than most or all. Does the argument need that much, or would a smaller claim do?
4. **Keep the subject straight.** The answer has to address the people, situation and missing information in this argument.
5. **Negate an answer you are still considering.** Treat it as not true, without changing the evidence. If the reasons still support the conclusion without it, it wasn't necessary.

# Necessary Assumptions: Find the Gap, Then Negate It

A necessary assumption is something the author has to believe for the argument to work, but never says. You don't invent it out of thin air. You find it in the space between what the evidence says and what the conclusion claims.

## Split the argument in two

Put the conclusion on one side and the evidence on the other.

- **Evidence:** you accept all of it as true. Every word.
- **Conclusion:** you don't accept any of it yet. The author has to earn it.

Then grant the author nothing that isn't written down. If it's not on the page, you don't give it to him.

Here's an argument:

> The town promised to repave Elm Street. Not one block has been finished this month. So the town has broken its promise.

The evidence says no block was finished this month. The conclusion says the promise is broken. Those aren't the same thing. To get from one to the other, the author has to believe something he never said:

**If the town were keeping its promise, at least one block would be finished within a month.**

That's the assumption. Nobody said how long the promise was supposed to take. Maybe a block takes six weeks.

## Negation is not the contrapositive

These two get mixed up because both use "not." They do different things.

**The contrapositive flips the rule and negates both sides.** It says the same thing as the original.
*If you're in New York, you're in the US.* becomes *If you're not in the US, you're not in New York.*

**Negation doesn't flip anything.** You put a "not" on the whole idea, and you get the weakest claim that contradicts it.

To negate an if-then assumption, keep the first part and deny the result:

| Assumption | Negation |
|---|---|
| If the town were keeping its promise, a block would be finished within a month. | The town is keeping its promise, and still no block was finished within a month. |

If the negation is true, the argument falls apart. That's how you know the assumption was necessary.

## How strong is the claim?

**"Primary" doesn't mean "only."** The main tool for something is not the only tool for it. Same with "most": most is not all.

**A plain sentence is a certain sentence.** *Alex will go to Tokyo next summer* sounds relaxed. On the LSAT it means Alex goes no matter what, the same as *Alex must go.* Only words like *likely* or *probably* leave room for it not to happen.

Necessary assumptions are conservative. They're the bare minimum the argument needs. So be scared of strong answers: *most*, *primarily*, *always*, or a flat certain claim. The stronger an answer is, the less likely the argument needed it. (Sufficient Assumption questions are the opposite. Strong answers are often right there.)

## What the right answer tends to look like

Look at the reasoning before you look at the answers.

- **Conditionals, a causal chain, or ideas that repeat and link up:** the right answer usually reads like a plain positive claim.
- **Causation, comparison, or anything else:** the right answer usually has a "not" in it.

This is a preview. We'll go deeper on it next time.

## Pop Quiz

1. Negate: *If the café is busy, Sam works late.*
   **Answer: The café is busy, and Sam doesn't work late.** One case where the first part happens without the result is enough.
2. *Email is the primary way the office contacts clients.* Does the office ever call clients?
   **Answer: you can't tell.** Primary means main, not only.
3. Argument: *The bakery switched to cheaper flour, and then its sales dropped. So the cheaper flour drove customers away.* Which does the argument need? (a) *Cheaper flour always makes bread taste worse.* (b) *The drop in sales was not caused entirely by a new bakery opening next door.*
   **Answer: (b).** Negate it: the drop was caused entirely by the new bakery. Then the flour had nothing to do with it, and the argument falls apart. (a) is far stronger than the argument needs. Notice (b) has a "not" in it: this is a causal argument.

## What changed

You came in thinking an assumption had to come out of thin air. By the end you were building it from what was on the page, and you said it yourself: the rule never said the work had to be finished.

You also caught the time limit on the first question before I pointed it out. Nobody said the plan had to be done in a year. That's the kind of detail that decides these questions.

And you put the difference in your own words: you don't flip it, you just put a "not" on it. Worth carrying: **negation adds a "not." The contrapositive flips and negates.**
