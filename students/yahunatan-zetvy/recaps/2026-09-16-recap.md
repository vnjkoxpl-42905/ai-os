# Conditional Must Be True: Keep the Whole Statement

Only people willing to compromise should undergo mediation. This tells you what is required for mediation to be appropriate. It does not tell you who actually attends, or whether willingness alone is enough. Keeping those distinctions intact is the work of conditional reasoning.

## Combine what the statements guarantee

A Must Be True answer must follow from the information you were given. Sometimes you combine facts. Sometimes you connect rules. Identify what each sentence gives you before choosing a method.

Suppose one type of flavonoid found in apples is an antioxidant, and antioxidants help prevent heart disease. You can conclude that at least one type of flavonoid found in apples helps prevent heart disease.

Why at least one? The first statement identifies one type with the relevant property. The second tells you what that property does. Together they support a claim about that type. They do not show that every flavonoid has the property, or that eating any apple prevents heart disease.

Keep the strength of the conclusion equal to the strength of the evidence. Helps prevent does not mean always prevents.

A rule works differently. If a person should undergo mediation, that person is willing to compromise tells you a relationship. It does not tell you that anyone actually should undergo mediation. A rule does not establish its own starting condition.

### Check your reasoning

An apple flavonoid is an antioxidant, and antioxidants help prevent heart disease. Can you conclude that every apple flavonoid prevents heart disease? Explain.

**Worked answer:** No. The evidence establishes the relevant property for at least one type, not every type. It says helps prevent, not always prevents. A second type without that property would fit the evidence and defeat the stronger answer.

## Put the requirement on the right

Consider this hypothetical rule: Only people willing to compromise should undergo mediation to resolve their conflict.

Mediation means trying to resolve a dispute with help from a neutral person. The rule limits who should use it. Willingness to compromise is required.

First flatten the sentence into complete English:

If a person should undergo mediation, then that person is willing to compromise.

Now abbreviate the complete ideas:

M = the person should undergo mediation.

W = the person is willing to compromise.

M → W

The left side is sufficient: knowing M lets you conclude W. The right side is necessary: M cannot be true without W. Necessary does not mean enough by itself. A person can meet a requirement without qualifying for the activity.

Only if works the same way. Litigation should be pursued only if a person is sure their position is correct becomes: If the person should pursue litigation, then the person is sure their position is correct.

L = the person should pursue litigation. C = the person is sure their position is correct.

L → C

Being sure is required. It does not establish that the person should litigate. Also, being sure that a position is correct is not the same as the position actually being correct.

### Check your reasoning

Only trained volunteers should lead the workshop. Write the complete if-then rule. Does training alone prove that a volunteer should lead?

**Worked answer:** If a volunteer should lead the workshop, then that volunteer is trained. Training is required, but training alone does not prove the person should lead. Other considerations may matter. Reversing the rule would add a guarantee we were never given.

## Keep should in the statement

A recommendation and an action are different ideas. A person may do something they should not do. A person may also fail to do something they should do.

M → W is about who should undergo mediation. It is not a rule about everyone who actually undergoes mediation.

Suppose Alex actually attends mediation but refuses to compromise. That does not contradict this rule. Alex may be doing something Alex should not do. But if we say Alex should undergo mediation and Alex is unwilling to compromise, those two claims violate the rule.

Keep words such as should, can, must, and actually when you translate. Removing one can change what the statement proves. An abbreviation saves writing only when you remember the whole idea behind it.

### Check your reasoning

We know only that Morgan actually underwent mediation. Does the rule prove that Morgan was willing to compromise? Why?

**Worked answer:** No. The rule starts with should undergo mediation, not actually underwent mediation. Morgan could have attended despite being unwilling. The actual action does not activate this recommendation rule.

## Reverse and negate to get the contrapositive

The rule M → W forbids one combination: M true with W false. If willingness is absent, the recommendation cannot be present.

M → W

not W → not M

In English: If a person is unwilling to compromise, then that person should not undergo mediation.

You reverse the order and negate both complete ideas. The new statement describes the same restriction as the original. This is why it is valid.

Reversing without negating does something different: W → M. It says willingness guarantees that mediation is appropriate. The original rule never promised that. A willing person might have a conflict for which mediation is inappropriate for another reason.

Negating without reversing is also invalid: not M → not W. A person who should not undergo mediation could still be willing to compromise.

The rule lets you move forward from M, or backward from not W. W alone and not M alone leave the other side open.

### Test every possible combination

| Should undergo mediation | Willing to compromise | Fits M → W? |
|---|---|---|
| True | True | Yes |
| True | False | No |
| False | True | Yes |
| False | False | Yes |

Only M true with W false violates the rule. Both states with M false are allowed. Both states with W true are allowed.

### Check your reasoning

A volunteer is not trained. What follows from the workshop rule? What if the volunteer is trained instead?

**Worked answer:** The untrained volunteer should not lead the workshop. That is the contrapositive: not trained → should not lead. A trained volunteer may or may not be someone who should lead. Affirming the requirement does not establish the trigger.

## A statement about a whole group can be a rule

Suppose a stimulus says: People whose conflicts are based on ideology are unwilling to compromise.

In this hypothetical stimulus, the statement applies to every person in the specified group. It is not a claim about all people, and it is not a general claim you should import into other questions.

I = the person’s conflict is based on ideology.

I → not W

This is a conditional relationship even without the word if. If a person belongs to the named group, the description applies to that person. It does not establish that any particular person belongs to that group.

Now connect it to the contrapositive:

I → not W → not M

Therefore: People whose conflicts are based on ideology should not undergo mediation.

The middle idea matches exactly. The group rule gets you to unwilling to compromise. The contrapositive takes you from unwilling to compromise to should not undergo mediation.

If the stimulus also says Lena’s conflict is based on ideology, that fact activates the chain. You can then conclude that Lena is unwilling to compromise and should not undergo mediation. Without that fact, your conclusion remains a rule about the group.

### Check your reasoning

Use I → not W and M → W. What must follow for anyone whose conflict is based on ideology? Does that prove someone actually has such a conflict?

**Worked answer:** I → not M. Contrapose M → W to get not W → not M, then connect I → not W → not M. The conclusion is should not undergo mediation. The group rule does not establish that a member of that group actually exists.

## Do not invent a choice between two activities

The separate rule L → C gives a requirement for litigation. It does not connect litigation to mediation.

Someone who should not undergo mediation does not automatically become someone who should pursue litigation. The stimulus has not said those are the only choices, or that every person must choose one.

A counterexample makes the gap visible. Imagine a person who should use neither activity. That can fit both rules: the person does not have to meet the requirements for an activity they should not pursue.

The two requirements do not create an either-or choice. You need an additional statement connecting the activities before moving from one to the other.

### Check your reasoning

If a person should not undergo mediation, must that person pursue litigation instead? Use a counterexample.

**Worked answer:** No. The person could appropriately pursue neither activity. That violates neither requirement. The premises do not say that mediation and litigation are exhaustive choices, or that rejecting one requires the other.

## Use the same process on a new question

1. Identify what each sentence supplies: a specific fact or a general relationship.
2. Translate each relationship into a complete if-then sentence. Preserve the subject, negation, and words such as should.
3. Name the complete ideas before abbreviating. Put the required condition on the right.
4. If you need the opposite direction, reverse and negate both ideas.
5. Connect rules only when the ending of one matches the beginning of the next. Apply a fact only when it supplies a valid starting point.
6. Read the result back in English. Test a tempting answer by trying to make the premises true and that answer false.

If you can build that counterexample, the answer is not guaranteed. If you cannot see a valid connection yet, do not add a fact or an extra choice to make the answer work.

### Put the moves together

1. A file may be released only if it has been reviewed. This file has not been reviewed. What follows?

**Worked answer:** This file may not be released. May be released → reviewed; not reviewed → may not be released. Preserve permission: this does not prove that nobody actually releases it.

2. Only licensed drivers may operate these vans. Sam is licensed. Must Sam be permitted to operate one?

**Worked answer:** No. May operate → licensed. Licensing is necessary, but could be insufficient. Sam could be licensed and still lack permission for another reason.

3. Every priority application needs supervisor approval. Approval is required only if the application is incomplete. What follows for priority applications?

**Worked answer:** Priority → needs approval → incomplete. Every priority application is incomplete under these hypothetical rules. No particular priority application is asserted to exist.

Transfer question: Only certified guides should lead night tours. All new guides are uncertified. Nora is a new guide. State the inference and one tempting answer that goes too far.

**Worked answer:** New → not certified; should lead → certified; not certified → should not lead. Nora should not lead night tours. Nora never actually leads night tours goes too far: the rules concern who should lead, not what someone actually does.

## 2026-09-30 concept extension: Choose the move the information supports

A rule tells you what follows if a condition is met. A fact tells you what is true in a particular case. Before drawing an inference, decide which kind of information you have and where it lets you start.

## Apply a fact to a rule

Suppose every application submitted after the deadline requires special approval. Maya submitted her application after the deadline. Maya's application therefore requires special approval.

The first sentence supplies the rule. The second supplies its starting condition for one application. Together they support a conclusion about that application.

Late application → requires special approval.

Maya's application is late → Maya's application requires special approval.

The fact must match the complete condition. If the rule concerns applications submitted after the deadline, knowing that an application was started after the deadline would not be enough. Started and submitted are different claims.

This is the same issue as keeping should in the mediation statement. Actually attending mediation does not establish that someone should attend. A fact cannot activate a rule by matching only part of its wording.

### Check your reasoning

Every application submitted after the deadline requires special approval. Omar's application requires special approval. Must Omar have submitted it after the deadline?

**Worked answer:** No. Special approval is the result, not the starting condition. Other applications might also require it. An on-time application that needs approval for another reason fits the rule and defeats the proposed inference.

## Find the two starting points a rule gives you

Use the same rule: late application → requires special approval.

There are two facts that let you draw a definite conclusion:

- The application is late. It requires special approval.
- The application does not require special approval. It is not late.

The second move uses the contrapositive. A late application without the required approval condition would violate the rule, so an application lacking that condition cannot be late.

Two other facts leave the opposite side open:

- It requires special approval. It may or may not be late.
- It is not late. It may or may not require special approval.

| Rule | Fact supplied | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| A → B | A | B |
| A → B | not B | not A |
| A → B | B | A remains open |
| A → B | not A | B remains open |

Do not choose a direction merely because a sentence mentions a familiar term. Locate the fact on the rule first. A fact about the trigger or the absence of the requirement allows a move. A fact about the requirement or the absence of the trigger does not.

### Check your reasoning

Every application submitted after the deadline requires special approval. Tess's application does not require special approval. What follows? What would follow if her application were simply not late?

**Worked answer:** Tess's application is not late, by contraposition. If all we knew was that it was not late, the rule would not determine whether it required special approval. An on-time application could require approval for a different reason.

## Link rules before applying a fact

Suppose every application submitted after the deadline requires special approval. Every application requiring special approval needs a supervisor's signature.

Late → requires special approval → needs a supervisor's signature.

Therefore, every late application needs a supervisor's signature. The result of the first rule supplies the starting condition of the second. That is why the chain works.

This is still a rule. It does not establish that any application was late. If you add the fact that Maya's application was late, you can apply the whole chain and conclude that her application requires approval and needs a signature.

Match the complete middle idea, including its subject and wording. Requires approval is not the same as has received approval. A rule about applications requiring approval cannot automatically connect to a rule about people granting approval.

### Check your reasoning

Every urgent request needs a review. Every request needing a review requires a supervisor's signature. Does every urgent request require a supervisor's signature? Do these rules establish that an urgent request exists?

**Worked answer:** Every urgent request requires a signature: urgent → needs review → requires signature. The rules do not establish that any urgent request exists. An additional fact would be needed to apply the rule to a particular request.

## Shared words do not always create a chain

Consider two different rules:

Late → requires special approval.

Late → needs a supervisor's signature.

Both start from late. Neither tells you that requiring approval is enough to need a signature. You cannot connect their results into requires approval → needs a signature.

Try a counterexample: an on-time application requires special approval but does not need a supervisor's signature. Both rules still hold because their starting condition is absent. This defeats the proposed connection between the results.

The same problem appears when two rules share a result: A → C and B → C do not establish A → B. Different starting conditions can lead to the same result.

### Check your reasoning

Every registered participant receives an email. Every registered participant has a name badge. Must everyone who receives an email have a name badge?

**Worked answer:** No. Someone who is not registered could receive an email without having a badge. Both rules concern registered participants; they do not connect email recipients to badge holders. For a registered participant, however, both results follow.

## Negate the whole condition

Suppose an archive file may be released only if it has been reviewed AND has consent.

May be released → reviewed AND consent.

The file lacks a necessary requirement if either review or consent is missing. The contrapositive is:

not reviewed OR not consent → may not be released.

You do not need both requirements to be missing. One is enough to rule out permission. A reviewed file without consent satisfies the OR starting point and cannot be released under this rule.

By contrast, if a rule requires at least one of two alternatives, both alternatives must be absent to show the requirement is unmet:

May enter → badge OR invitation.

not badge AND not invitation → may not enter.

Keep permission distinct from actual behavior. May not enter does not prove that a person never enters.

### Check your reasoning

A document may be shared only if it is reviewed AND authorized. It is reviewed but not authorized. May it be shared under this rule?

**Worked answer:** No. Authorization is one of the two required conditions. Its absence triggers the contrapositive. The document does not need to be both unreviewed and unauthorized for permission to fail.

## Choose your next move

1. Separate the facts from the rules.
2. Translate each rule while keeping the complete subject, negation, and words such as should or may.
3. If you have a fact, locate it on a rule. Move forward from the trigger, or use contraposition from the absence of the requirement.
4. If you have rules to connect, match the result of one to the trigger of another. Contrapose first if that creates a valid match.
5. State exactly what follows. Preserve the strength and scope of the evidence.
6. Test a tempting stronger conclusion: can the premises be true while that conclusion is false?

### Put the moves together

All priority applications require review. Every application requiring review needs supervisor approval. Ari's application is a priority application. What follows?

**Worked answer:** Ari's application requires review and needs supervisor approval. The fact activates priority → requires review → needs approval. This does not establish that either review or approval has actually happened.

All priority applications require review. Bea's application does not require review. What follows?

**Worked answer:** Bea's application is not a priority application. Contrapose priority → requires review. This does not show that Bea never receives a review voluntarily.

Transfer question: Every application needing supervisor approval requires a signature. Every application requiring a signature must be archived. Dev's application does not have to be archived. What follows, and what remains open?

**Worked answer:** Dev's application does not require a signature and does not need supervisor approval. Contrapose both rules, then link not required to be archived → not requiring signature → not needing approval. Whether it is actually archived remains open. The premises concern requirements, not completed actions.
