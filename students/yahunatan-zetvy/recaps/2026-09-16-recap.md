# Conditional Must Be True: Keep the Whole Statement

Only people willing to compromise should undergo mediation. This tells you what is required for mediation to be appropriate. It does not tell you who actually attends, or whether willingness alone is enough. Keeping those distinctions intact is the work of conditional reasoning.

## Combine what the statements guarantee

A Must Be True answer must follow from the information you were given. Sometimes you combine facts. Sometimes you connect rules. Identify what each sentence gives you before choosing a method.

Suppose one type of flavonoid found in apples is an antioxidant, and antioxidants help prevent heart disease. You can conclude that at least one type of flavonoid found in apples helps prevent heart disease.

Why at least one? The first statement identifies one type with the relevant property. The second tells you what that property does. Together they support a claim about that type. They do not show that every flavonoid has the property, or that eating any apple prevents heart disease.

Keep the strength of the conclusion equal to the strength of the evidence. Helps prevent does not mean always prevents.

A rule works differently. If a person should undergo mediation, that person is willing to compromise tells you a relationship. It does not tell you that anyone actually should undergo mediation. A rule does not establish its own starting condition.

### Check your reasoning

An apple flavonoid is an antioxidant, and antioxidants help prevent heart disease. Can you conclude that every apple flavonoid prevents heart disease? Explain.

**Worked answer:** No. The evidence establishes the relevant property for at least one type, not every type. It says helps prevent, not always prevents. A second type without that property would fit the evidence and defeat the stronger answer.

## Put the requirement on the right

Consider this hypothetical rule: Only people willing to compromise should undergo mediation to resolve their conflict.

Mediation means trying to resolve a dispute with help from a neutral person. The rule limits who should use it. Willingness to compromise is required.

First flatten the sentence into complete English:

If a person should undergo mediation, then that person is willing to compromise.

Now abbreviate the complete ideas:

M = the person should undergo mediation.

W = the person is willing to compromise.

M → W

The left side is sufficient: knowing M lets you conclude W. The right side is necessary: M cannot be true without W. Necessary does not mean enough by itself. A person can meet a requirement without qualifying for the activity.

Only if works the same way. Litigation should be pursued only if a person is sure their position is correct becomes: If the person should pursue litigation, then the person is sure their position is correct.

L = the person should pursue litigation. C = the person is sure their position is correct.

L → C

Being sure is required. It does not establish that the person should litigate. Also, being sure that a position is correct is not the same as the position actually being correct.

### Check your reasoning

Only trained volunteers should lead the workshop. Write the complete if-then rule. Does training alone prove that a volunteer should lead?

**Worked answer:** If a volunteer should lead the workshop, then that volunteer is trained. Training is required, but training alone does not prove the person should lead. Other considerations may matter. Reversing the rule would add a guarantee we were never given.

## Keep should in the statement

A recommendation and an action are different ideas. A person may do something they should not do. A person may also fail to do something they should do.

M → W is about who should undergo mediation. It is not a rule about everyone who actually undergoes mediation.

Suppose Alex actually attends mediation but refuses to compromise. That does not contradict this rule. Alex may be doing something Alex should not do. But if we say Alex should undergo mediation and Alex is unwilling to compromise, those two claims violate the rule.

Keep words such as should, can, must, and actually when you translate. Removing one can change what the statement proves. An abbreviation saves writing only when you remember the whole idea behind it.

### Check your reasoning

We know only that Morgan actually underwent mediation. Does the rule prove that Morgan was willing to compromise? Why?

**Worked answer:** No. The rule starts with should undergo mediation, not actually underwent mediation. Morgan could have attended despite being unwilling. The actual action does not activate this recommendation rule.

## Reverse and negate to get the contrapositive

The rule M → W forbids one combination: M true with W false. If willingness is absent, the recommendation cannot be present.

M → W

not W → not M

In English: If a person is unwilling to compromise, then that person should not undergo mediation.

You reverse the order and negate both complete ideas. The new statement describes the same restriction as the original. This is why it is valid.

Reversing without negating does something different: W → M. It says willingness guarantees that mediation is appropriate. The original rule never promised that. A willing person might have a conflict for which mediation is inappropriate for another reason.

Negating without reversing is also invalid: not M → not W. A person who should not undergo mediation could still be willing to compromise.

The rule lets you move forward from M, or backward from not W. W alone and not M alone leave the other side open.

### Test every possible combination

| Should undergo mediation | Willing to compromise | Fits M → W? |
|---|---|---|
| True | True | Yes |
| True | False | No |
| False | True | Yes |
| False | False | Yes |

Only M true with W false violates the rule. Both states with M false are allowed. Both states with W true are allowed.

### Check your reasoning

A volunteer is not trained. What follows from the workshop rule? What if the volunteer is trained instead?

**Worked answer:** The untrained volunteer should not lead the workshop. That is the contrapositive: not trained → should not lead. A trained volunteer may or may not be someone who should lead. Affirming the requirement does not establish the trigger.

## A statement about a whole group can be a rule

Suppose a stimulus says: People whose conflicts are based on ideology are unwilling to compromise.

In this hypothetical stimulus, the statement applies to every person in the specified group. It is not a claim about all people, and it is not a general claim you should import into other questions.

I = the person’s conflict is based on ideology.

I → not W

This is a conditional relationship even without the word if. If a person belongs to the named group, the description applies to that person. It does not establish that any particular person belongs to that group.

Now connect it to the contrapositive:

I → not W → not M

Therefore: People whose conflicts are based on ideology should not undergo mediation.

The middle idea matches exactly. The group rule gets you to unwilling to compromise. The contrapositive takes you from unwilling to compromise to should not undergo mediation.

If the stimulus also says Lena’s conflict is based on ideology, that fact activates the chain. You can then conclude that Lena is unwilling to compromise and should not undergo mediation. Without that fact, your conclusion remains a rule about the group.

### Check your reasoning

Use I → not W and M → W. What must follow for anyone whose conflict is based on ideology? Does that prove someone actually has such a conflict?

**Worked answer:** I → not M. Contrapose M → W to get not W → not M, then connect I → not W → not M. The conclusion is should not undergo mediation. The group rule does not establish that a member of that group actually exists.

## Do not invent a choice between two activities

The separate rule L → C gives a requirement for litigation. It does not connect litigation to mediation.

Someone who should not undergo mediation does not automatically become someone who should pursue litigation. The stimulus has not said those are the only choices, or that every person must choose one.

A counterexample makes the gap visible. Imagine a person who should use neither activity. That can fit both rules: the person does not have to meet the requirements for an activity they should not pursue.

The two requirements do not create an either-or choice. You need an additional statement connecting the activities before moving from one to the other.

### Check your reasoning

If a person should not undergo mediation, must that person pursue litigation instead? Use a counterexample.

**Worked answer:** No. The person could appropriately pursue neither activity. That violates neither requirement. The premises do not say that mediation and litigation are exhaustive choices, or that rejecting one requires the other.

## Use the same process on a new question

1. Identify what each sentence supplies: a specific fact or a general relationship.
2. Translate each relationship into a complete if-then sentence. Preserve the subject, negation, and words such as should.
3. Name the complete ideas before abbreviating. Put the required condition on the right.
4. If you need the opposite direction, reverse and negate both ideas.
5. Connect rules only when the ending of one matches the beginning of the next. Apply a fact only when it supplies a valid starting point.
6. Read the result back in English. Test a tempting answer by trying to make the premises true and that answer false.

If you can build that counterexample, the answer is not guaranteed. If you cannot see a valid connection yet, do not add a fact or an extra choice to make the answer work.

### Put the moves together

1. A file may be released only if it has been reviewed. This file has not been reviewed. What follows?

**Worked answer:** This file may not be released. May be released → reviewed; not reviewed → may not be released. Preserve permission: this does not prove that nobody actually releases it.

2. Only licensed drivers may operate these vans. Sam is licensed. Must Sam be permitted to operate one?

**Worked answer:** No. May operate → licensed. Licensing is necessary, but could be insufficient. Sam could be licensed and still lack permission for another reason.

3. Every priority application needs supervisor approval. Approval is required only if the application is incomplete. What follows for priority applications?

**Worked answer:** Priority → needs approval → incomplete. Every priority application is incomplete under these hypothetical rules. No particular priority application is asserted to exist.

Transfer question: Only certified guides should lead night tours. All new guides are uncertified. Nora is a new guide. State the inference and one tempting answer that goes too far.

**Worked answer:** New → not certified; should lead → certified; not certified → should not lead. Nora should not lead night tours. Nora never actually leads night tours goes too far: the rules concern who should lead, not what someone actually does.

## 2026-09-30 concept extension: Choose the move the information supports

A rule tells you what follows if a condition is met. A fact tells you what is true in a particular case. Before drawing an inference, decide which kind of information you have and where it lets you start.

## Apply a fact to a rule

Suppose every application submitted after the deadline requires special approval. Maya submitted her application after the deadline. Maya's application therefore requires special approval.

The first sentence supplies the rule. The second supplies its starting condition for one application. Together they support a conclusion about that application.

Late application → requires special approval.

Maya's application is late → Maya's application requires special approval.

The fact must match the complete condition. If the rule concerns applications submitted after the deadline, knowing that an application was started after the deadline would not be enough. Started and submitted are different claims.

This is the same issue as keeping should in the mediation statement. Actually attending mediation does not establish that someone should attend. A fact cannot activate a rule by matching only part of its wording.

### Check your reasoning

Every application submitted after the deadline requires special approval. Omar's application requires special approval. Must Omar have submitted it after the deadline?

**Worked answer:** No. Special approval is the result, not the starting condition. Other applications might also require it. An on-time application that needs approval for another reason fits the rule and defeats the proposed inference.

## Find the two starting points a rule gives you

Use the same rule: late application → requires special approval.

There are two facts that let you draw a definite conclusion:

- The application is late. It requires special approval.
- The application does not require special approval. It is not late.

The second move uses the contrapositive. A late application without the required approval condition would violate the rule, so an application lacking that condition cannot be late.

Two other facts leave the opposite side open:

- It requires special approval. It may or may not be late.
- It is not late. It may or may not require special approval.

| Rule | Fact supplied | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| A → B | A | B |
| A → B | not B | not A |
| A → B | B | A remains open |
| A → B | not A | B remains open |

Do not choose a direction merely because a sentence mentions a familiar term. Locate the fact on the rule first. A fact about the trigger or the absence of the requirement allows a move. A fact about the requirement or the absence of the trigger does not.

### Check your reasoning

Every application submitted after the deadline requires special approval. Tess's application does not require special approval. What follows? What would follow if her application were simply not late?

**Worked answer:** Tess's application is not late, by contraposition. If all we knew was that it was not late, the rule would not determine whether it required special approval. An on-time application could require approval for a different reason.

## Link rules before applying a fact

Suppose every application submitted after the deadline requires special approval. Every application requiring special approval needs a supervisor's signature.

Late → requires special approval → needs a supervisor's signature.

Therefore, every late application needs a supervisor's signature. The result of the first rule supplies the starting condition of the second. That is why the chain works.

This is still a rule. It does not establish that any application was late. If you add the fact that Maya's application was late, you can apply the whole chain and conclude that her application requires approval and needs a signature.

Match the complete middle idea, including its subject and wording. Requires approval is not the same as has received approval. A rule about applications requiring approval cannot automatically connect to a rule about people granting approval.

### Check your reasoning

Every urgent request needs a review. Every request needing a review requires a supervisor's signature. Does every urgent request require a supervisor's signature? Do these rules establish that an urgent request exists?

**Worked answer:** Every urgent request requires a signature: urgent → needs review → requires signature. The rules do not establish that any urgent request exists. An additional fact would be needed to apply the rule to a particular request.

## Shared words do not always create a chain

Consider two different rules:

Late → requires special approval.

Late → needs a supervisor's signature.

Both start from late. Neither tells you that requiring approval is enough to need a signature. You cannot connect their results into requires approval → needs a signature.

Try a counterexample: an on-time application requires special approval but does not need a supervisor's signature. Both rules still hold because their starting condition is absent. This defeats the proposed connection between the results.

The same problem appears when two rules share a result: A → C and B → C do not establish A → B. Different starting conditions can lead to the same result.

### Check your reasoning

Every registered participant receives an email. Every registered participant has a name badge. Must everyone who receives an email have a name badge?

**Worked answer:** No. Someone who is not registered could receive an email without having a badge. Both rules concern registered participants; they do not connect email recipients to badge holders. For a registered participant, however, both results follow.

## Negate the whole condition

Suppose an archive file may be released only if it has been reviewed AND has consent.

May be released → reviewed AND consent.

The file lacks a necessary requirement if either review or consent is missing. The contrapositive is:

not reviewed OR not consent → may not be released.

You do not need both requirements to be missing. One is enough to rule out permission. A reviewed file without consent satisfies the OR starting point and cannot be released under this rule.

By contrast, if a rule requires at least one of two alternatives, both alternatives must be absent to show the requirement is unmet:

May enter → badge OR invitation.

not badge AND not invitation → may not enter.

Keep permission distinct from actual behavior. May not enter does not prove that a person never enters.

### Check your reasoning

A document may be shared only if it is reviewed AND authorized. It is reviewed but not authorized. May it be shared under this rule?

**Worked answer:** No. Authorization is one of the two required conditions. Its absence triggers the contrapositive. The document does not need to be both unreviewed and unauthorized for permission to fail.

## Choose your next move

1. Separate the facts from the rules.
2. Translate each rule while keeping the complete subject, negation, and words such as should or may.
3. If you have a fact, locate it on a rule. Move forward from the trigger, or use contraposition from the absence of the requirement.
4. If you have rules to connect, match the result of one to the trigger of another. Contrapose first if that creates a valid match.
5. State exactly what follows. Preserve the strength and scope of the evidence.
6. Test a tempting stronger conclusion: can the premises be true while that conclusion is false?

### Put the moves together

All priority applications require review. Every application requiring review needs supervisor approval. Ari's application is a priority application. What follows?

**Worked answer:** Ari's application requires review and needs supervisor approval. The fact activates priority → requires review → needs approval. This does not establish that either review or approval has actually happened.

All priority applications require review. Bea's application does not require review. What follows?

**Worked answer:** Bea's application is not a priority application. Contrapose priority → requires review. This does not show that Bea never receives a review voluntarily.

Transfer question: Every application needing supervisor approval requires a signature. Every application requiring a signature must be archived. Dev's application does not have to be archived. What follows, and what remains open?

**Worked answer:** Dev's application does not require a signature and does not need supervisor approval. Contrapose both rules, then link not required to be archived → not requiring signature → not needing approval. Whether it is actually archived remains open. The premises concern requirements, not completed actions.

# Facts, Rules, and What Follows

A rule tells you what follows **if a condition is met**. A fact tells you what is true in a particular case. Put them together, and you can prove something new.

Suppose a program has this policy: **Every application submitted after the deadline requires special approval.** One application was submitted after the deadline. That application requires special approval. We have a rule, a fact that fits its starting condition, and a conclusion the rule guarantees.

Change the fact, and the conclusion can change. An application that requires special approval need not have been late. It could require approval for another reason. The policy gives one route to that requirement, not the only route.

<!-- FACT-LAB -->

## 01 | Start with the fact you actually have

The rule applies to every late application. The fact identifies one of those applications. We can now apply the rule to that particular case.

**Rule:** Every application submitted after the deadline requires special approval.

**Fact:** This application was submitted after the deadline.

**What follows:** This application requires special approval.

The rule alone does not tell us that any applications were submitted late. We needed the fact to establish that we had a case to which the rule applies. A conditional rule can be true even if nothing meets its starting condition.

Now give the two complete ideas short names. Let **L** mean “submitted after the deadline” and **R** mean “requires special approval.” The notation saves space; it should not change the meaning.

`L -> R` means: if L is true, R must be true.

**Try it:** Every file marked confidential requires restricted storage. This file is marked confidential. What follows? Does the rule alone establish that a confidential file exists?

**Worked answer:** This file requires restricted storage. The fact establishes that this particular file is confidential. The rule alone establishes a requirement for any confidential file, without establishing that any such file exists.

## 02 | Use the rule forward, or use its contrapositive

There are two ways to use the application policy. A late application must require approval. An application that does **not** require approval cannot have been late. If it had been late, the policy would require approval, contradicting the fact we were given.

<!-- TWO-MOVES -->

**Forward:** Submitted late -> Requires approval.

**Contrapositive:** Does not require approval -> Was not submitted late.

The contrapositive states the same rule from its other end. Reverse the direction **and negate both complete ideas**. Simply reversing the arrow would say that requiring approval proves lateness. The original policy never said that.

Simply negating both ideas without reversing would also add something: “not late -> does not require approval.” An on-time application could require approval for a different reason. That situation does not break the policy.

The only combination the original rule rules out is **late, but does not require approval**. The other three combinations can fit the rule.

<!-- CASE-GRID -->

**Try it:** Every confidential file requires restricted storage. This file does not require restricted storage. What follows? What if all you know is that it does require restricted storage?

**Worked answer:** If it does not require restricted storage, it is not confidential. That is the contrapositive. If it does require restricted storage, its confidentiality remains unknown. A nonconfidential file could require restricted storage for another reason.

## 03 | Link rules when the whole middle idea matches

Add a second policy: **Every application that requires special approval needs a supervisor's signature.** The first rule gets us to “requires special approval.” The second starts at that exact idea. We can carry the application through both rules.

**First rule:** Submitted late -> Requires special approval.

**Second rule:** Requires special approval -> Needs a supervisor's signature.

**Combined rule:** Submitted late -> Needs a supervisor's signature.

In letters, the pattern is `L -> R` and `R -> S`, so `L -> S`. If a particular application was late, both requirements follow for that application. Without a fact establishing a late application, we still have a combined rule rather than proof that a late application exists.

You can also work backward through the combined rule. If an application does **not** need a supervisor's signature, it cannot require special approval under the second policy. If it does not require special approval, it cannot have been late under the first policy.

**Backward chain:** Does not need signature -> Does not require approval -> Was not late.

<!-- CHAIN-LAB -->

### Matching a word is not enough

“Requires special approval” and “has received special approval” both mention approval. They describe different conditions. A requirement can exist before anyone fulfills it. You cannot link a rule ending in the first phrase to a rule starting in the second unless another statement connects them.

This is why you should diagram **complete ideas**, not isolated words. Keep words such as “requires,” “has,” “should,” and “may” inside the condition they qualify.

**Try it:** Every late application requires special approval. Every application that has received special approval has a supervisor's signature. Does a late application necessarily have a supervisor's signature? Explain the missing connection.

**Worked answer:** No. A late application requires approval, but it may not have received it. The second rule applies to applications that have received approval. Nothing says the late application's requirement has been fulfilled.

## 04 | A shared starting point does not create a chain

Compare a different pair of rules: **Every late application requires special approval. Every late application needs a supervisor's signature.** Both rules start at “late.” Neither starts where the other ends.

<!-- SHARED-TRIGGER -->

If a fact tells us an application was late, we can apply **both** rules to it. That application requires approval and needs a signature. But we cannot conclude that every application requiring approval needs a signature. An on-time application might require approval for another reason without needing a signature.

Likewise, two rules that end at the same idea do not connect their starting conditions. If confidential files require restricted storage and medical files require restricted storage, a confidential file need not be a medical file. Restricted storage could cover both types separately.

Before joining two arrows, find the destination of the first and the starting condition of the second. They must match, including any negation or qualifier. If they do not match, keep the rules separate or see whether a valid contrapositive produces a match.

**Try it:** Every member of a review panel has training. Every member of that panel has a security badge. Must everyone with training have a security badge? What follows if Dana is a panel member?

**Worked answer:** Training alone does not guarantee a badge. A trained person could be outside the panel. Because Dana is a panel member, both rules apply to Dana: Dana has training and a security badge.

## 05 | Keep the kind of claim intact

A rule can tell us what someone **should** do or what they **may** do. That is different from telling us what they actually did.

Suppose **every damaged item should be inspected**. If this item is damaged, it should be inspected. We cannot conclude it has been inspected. If an item should not be inspected, then it is not damaged under that rule. But “has not been inspected” is not the same as “should not be inspected.” An inspection could be overdue.

Suppose **a visitor may enter the archive only if the visitor has a badge**. A badge is necessary for permission to enter. The rule is **May enter -> Has a badge**. Without a badge, the visitor does not have permission to enter under this rule. Having a badge alone does not establish permission; another requirement might still apply.

<!-- MODALITY -->

The rule concerns permission. It does not prove that someone physically cannot enter without permission. Keep the same kind of claim throughout your reasoning: a requirement stays a requirement, advice stays advice, and permission stays permission.

**Try it:** Every damaged item should be inspected. This damaged item has not been inspected. Does that violate the rule? What can you conclude about what should happen?

**Worked answer:** The item should be inspected. Its not having been inspected does not contradict the claim about what should happen. The rule does not state that every required inspection actually occurs.

## 06 | Negate the whole condition

Some conditions contain more than one part. Suppose **a document may be released only if it has been reviewed AND authorized**. Permission requires both parts. If either part is missing, the combined requirement is not satisfied.

**Rule:** May be released -> Reviewed AND authorized.

**Contrapositive:** Not reviewed OR not authorized -> May not be released.

To negate “reviewed AND authorized,” you only need at least one failure. You do not need both parts to fail. “Not both” does not mean “neither.”

Now suppose **a visitor may enter only if the visitor has a badge OR an invitation**. This requirement allows either route, including both. To fail the entire requirement, the visitor must have neither.

**Rule:** May enter -> Has badge OR has invitation.

**Contrapositive:** No badge AND no invitation -> May not enter.

<!-- NEGATION -->

Negating a compound condition changes **AND to OR**, or **OR to AND**, while negating each part. For the original AND requirement, one missing part is enough. For the original OR requirement, both routes must be missing.

**Try it:** A document may be released only if it has been reviewed and authorized. It has been reviewed but not authorized. What follows? Does the contrapositive require us to conclude it was not reviewed?

**Worked answer:** The document may not be released. Missing authorization is enough to fail the combined requirement. We do not conclude that review is missing too; the fact says review occurred.

## 07 | Decide what the information lets you do

Start by separating **facts about a case** from **rules about cases**. Then choose the move the information supports.

| What you have | The move | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A fact meeting a rule's starting condition | Apply the rule forward | Does the whole fact match the whole condition? |
| A fact denying a rule's required result | Use the contrapositive | Did you reverse and negate both complete ideas? |
| Two rules with a matching middle condition | Link the rules | Are direction, negation, and qualifiers identical? |
| Only the required result, or only the absence of the starting condition | Leave the other side open | Could another explanation fit the rule? |

When an inference seems tempting, try to build a case in which the statements are true and the proposed conclusion is false. If you can, the conclusion is not guaranteed. The counterexamples above work this way: an on-time application can still require approval; a requirement can remain unfulfilled; a trained person can be outside the panel.

Keep the scope of the fact too. **Some applications were submitted late. Every late application requires special approval. Therefore, some applications require special approval.** We cannot change “some” to “all.” The rule covers every late case, but the fact establishes only at least one. Other applications could be on time and require no special approval.

## 08 | Mix the moves

For each problem, write what follows and name the move. If a proposed conclusion is not guaranteed, explain a situation that would make it false while keeping the given statements true.

**Practice 1:** Every confidential file requires restricted storage. File K does not require restricted storage. What follows?

**Worked answer:** File K is not confidential. Use the contrapositive: does not require restricted storage -> not confidential.

**Practice 2:** Every late application requires special approval. Every application requiring special approval needs a supervisor's signature. Application J was late. What follows?

**Worked answer:** Application J requires special approval and needs a supervisor's signature. Apply the first rule to the fact, then carry its result through the second rule.

**Practice 3:** Every panel member has training. Every panel member has a security badge. Lee has training. Must Lee have a badge?

**Worked answer:** No. Lee could be a trained person outside the panel who has no badge. The two rules share a starting condition. Training alone does not establish that Lee meets it.

**Practice 4:** A document may be released only if it has been reviewed and authorized. Document M has not been reviewed. What follows? Must it also lack authorization?

**Worked answer:** Document M may not be released. One missing requirement is enough. Whether it has authorization remains open; it could have authorization but lack review.

## 09 | Take this into your assigned work

Do the **conditional-diagramming drills first**, then move to the **LSAT questions**. In the drills, write the complete conditions before shortening them. Check your arrow and its contrapositive. When two rules appear, look for an exact match before combining them.

In the LSAT questions, separate the facts from the rules before choosing an answer. Your job is to identify what the statements guarantee. If an answer reverses the rule, drops a qualifier, or turns a requirement into an accomplished action, test it with a counterexample.

Keep this question nearby: **What do I actually have, and which move does it support?**

**Worked answer:** Training alone does not guarantee a badge. A trained person could be outside the panel. Because Dana is a panel member, both rules apply to Dana: Dana has training and a security badge.

## 07 | Use the contrapositive when the result is absent

A different fact calls for a different move. Suppose **Niko does not have a badge**, and the same rule says **everyone who is trained has a badge**.

If Niko were trained, the rule would require Niko to have a badge. That would contradict the given fact. So **Niko is not trained**.

**Given fact:** Niko does not have a badge.

**Rule:** Trained → Has a badge.

**Match:** The fact denies the required result. Restate the rule as **No badge → Not trained**.

**Therefore:** Niko is not trained.

**Why:** Training cannot be present while the rule's required result is absent. This is the **contrapositive**: reverse the arrow and negate both complete conditions.

The contrapositive states the same rule. It does not deny the original rule, and it is not the unsupported reversal **Has a badge → Trained**.

<!-- TWO-MOVES -->

### Four facts, one unchanged rule

The rule is **Trained → Has a badge**. The rule stays the same. Change only what we know about the person.

#### Fact 1: The person is trained

**Match:** The original starting condition is met.

**What follows:** The person has a badge.

**Why:** Every trained person has a badge under this rule. Apply it to this person.

#### Fact 2: The person has no badge

**Match:** The required result is absent. This meets the contrapositive's starting condition.

**What follows:** The person is not trained.

**Why:** A trained person without a badge would contradict the rule.

#### Fact 3: The person has a badge

**Match:** We have only the original result. The rule does not say that everyone with a badge is trained.

**What follows:** Whether this person is trained remains unknown.

**Why:** An untrained visitor with a badge fits the rule. A trained person with a badge also fits it.

#### Fact 4: The person is not trained

**Match:** The original starting condition is absent. The rule only guarantees badges for trained people.

**What follows:** Whether this person has a badge remains unknown.

**Why:** An untrained person could have a badge or lack one. Both situations fit the rule.

**Trained, but no badge** contradicts the rule. The other three combinations fit.

Now explore those same moves with a different rule: **Every late application requires special approval.** First identify the supplied fact. Then see whether it triggers the original rule, triggers its contrapositive, or leaves the other condition open.

<!-- FACT-LAB -->

**Try it:** Every confidential file requires restricted storage. This file does not require restricted storage. What follows? What if all you know is that it does require restricted storage?

**Worked answer:** If it does not require restricted storage, it is not confidential. That is the contrapositive. If it does require restricted storage, its confidentiality remains unknown. A nonconfidential file could require restricted storage for another reason.

You do not have to write every contrapositive automatically. If the fact clearly meets the original starting condition, apply that rule. If the fact denies its result, or a connection is difficult to see, a contrapositive can make the supported move clear.


### Carry a negative fact through a chain

Return to the two application rules: **Submitted late → Requires special approval → Needs a supervisor's signature**.

Now add a different fact: **Application R does not need a supervisor's signature.** If R required special approval, the second rule would require a signature. So R does not require approval. If R were late, the first rule would require approval. So R was not late.

**Contrapositive chain:** Does not need signature → Does not require approval → Was not late.

**Therefore:** Application R does not require special approval and was not late.

### A contrapositive can uncover the match

Suppose these two rules are given:

**Rule 1:** Trained → Has a badge.

**Rule 2:** Not trained → Requires supervision.

“Trained” and “not trained” are opposite ideas in the same position: both are starting conditions. They do not look like a chain yet. Take the contrapositive of the first rule:

**Restated rule 1:** No badge → Not trained.

Now “not trained” is the result of that restatement and the starting condition of rule 2. We can link them:

**New chain:** No badge → Not trained → Requires supervision.

**New conditional:** Anyone without a badge requires supervision.

Again, that is a general rule. It does not prove that someone lacks a badge. If a separate fact says Niko lacks a badge, then we can apply the chain and conclude that Niko requires supervision.

<!-- HIDDEN-CHAIN -->

If the middle condition already matches, link the rules directly. You only need to restate a rule when doing so reveals a connection you could not use as written.

## 08 | Translate the complete claim

### “Only if” introduces the required condition

“A visitor may enter only if the visitor has a badge” means permission to enter requires a badge. Write **May enter → Has a badge**. Put the requirement after the arrow. A badge alone does not prove permission to enter.

“Only badge holders may enter” states that same requirement. Translate the complete sentence before shortening its conditions.

### Keep requirements separate from completed actions

“Requires special approval” and “has received special approval” are not the same condition. A requirement can remain unfulfilled. A rule ending in the first phrase cannot be linked to one starting in the second unless another statement connects them. Shared words are not enough; match the complete claim, including qualifiers and negation.

**Try it:** Every late application requires special approval. Every application that has received special approval has a supervisor's signature. Does a late application necessarily have a supervisor's signature? Explain the missing connection.

**Worked answer:** No. A late application requires approval, but it may not have received it. The second rule applies to applications that have received approval. Nothing says the late application's requirement has been fulfilled.

<!-- CHAIN-LAB -->



### Rules can connect different kinds of claims

A rule can tell us what someone **should** do or what they **may** do. That is different from telling us what they actually did.

Suppose **every damaged item should be inspected**. If this item is damaged, it should be inspected. We cannot conclude it has been inspected. But “has not been inspected” is not the same as “it is not true that it should be inspected.” An inspection could be overdue.

<!-- MODALITY -->

For **Damaged → Should be inspected**, the contrapositive starts with “it is not true that it should be inspected” and concludes “not damaged.” Negate the complete claim about what should happen. Whether the item has actually been inspected is a different claim.

Suppose **a visitor may enter the archive only if the visitor has a badge**. A badge is necessary for permission to enter. The rule is **May enter → Has a badge**. Without a badge, the visitor does not have permission to enter under this rule. Having a badge alone does not establish permission; another requirement might still apply.

The rule concerns permission. It does not prove that someone physically cannot enter without permission. Keep the same kind of claim throughout your reasoning: a requirement stays a requirement, advice stays advice, and permission stays permission.



### Negate the whole requirement

Some conditions contain more than one part. Suppose **a document may be released only if it has been reviewed AND authorized**. Permission requires both parts. If either part is missing, the combined requirement is not satisfied.

**Rule:** May be released → Reviewed AND authorized.

**Contrapositive:** Not reviewed OR not authorized → May not be released.

To negate “reviewed AND authorized,” you only need at least one failure. You do not need both parts to fail. “Not both” does not mean “neither.”

Now suppose **a visitor may enter only if the visitor has a badge OR an invitation**. This requirement allows either route, including both. To fail the entire requirement, the visitor must have neither.

**Rule:** May enter → Has badge OR has invitation.

**Contrapositive:** No badge AND no invitation → May not enter.

<!-- NEGATION -->

Negating a compound condition changes **AND to OR**, or **OR to AND**, while negating each part. For the original AND requirement, one missing part is enough. For the original OR requirement, both routes must be missing.

**Try it:** A document may be released only if it has been reviewed and authorized. It has been reviewed but not authorized. What follows? Does the contrapositive require us to conclude it was not reviewed?

**Worked answer:** The document may not be released. Missing authorization is enough to fail the combined requirement. We do not conclude that review is missing too; the fact says review occurred.

## 09 | Choose the move and test the claim

Ask what you have: a fact that can activate a rule, or two rules that can connect? The matching condition determines the move.

Keep the scope of the fact too. **Some applications were submitted late. Every late application requires special approval. Therefore, some applications require special approval.** We cannot change “some” to “all.” The rule covers every late case, but the fact establishes only at least one. Other applications could be on time and require no special approval.

| What you have | The move | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A fact meeting a rule's starting condition | Apply the rule forward | Does the whole fact match the whole condition? |
| A fact denying a rule's required result | Use the contrapositive | Did you reverse and negate both complete ideas? |
| Two rules with a matching middle condition | Link the rules | Does the first rule end with the exact condition the second starts with? |
| Only the required result, or only the absence of the starting condition | Leave the other side open | Could another explanation fit the rule? |

To disprove a proposed inference, keep every given statement true and make the proposed conclusion false. For example, an on-time application that requires approval fits **Late → Requires approval**, but disproves **Requires approval → Late**.

## 10 | Mix the moves

Use only the information given in each problem. For each problem, write what follows and name the move. If a proposed conclusion is not guaranteed, explain a situation that would make it false while keeping the given statements true.

**Practice 1:** Every certified instructor has a license. Every person who is not a certified instructor requires supervision. Instructor Q has no license. What follows? Show the connection between the rules.

**Worked answer:** Q is not a certified instructor and requires supervision. Contrapose the first rule: no license → not a certified instructor. That result meets the second rule's starting condition. The chain is no license → not certified → requires supervision. Q's fact activates it.

**Practice 2:** A visitor may enter a lab only if the visitor has a pass or an invitation. Visitor S has no pass and no invitation. What follows? If S had an invitation, would that alone establish permission to enter?

**Worked answer:** S may not enter. Permission requires at least one of the two alternatives, and S has neither. An invitation alone would not prove permission: the rule says what permission requires, not that meeting this requirement is sufficient.

**Practice 3:** Every flagged expense requires manager approval. Every expense that has received manager approval may be reimbursed. Expense E is flagged. Must E be eligible for reimbursement?

**Worked answer:** No. E requires approval, but nothing establishes that it has received approval. It could still be waiting for the manager. The second rule's starting condition is not established, so the two phrases cannot serve as a matching middle condition.

**Practice 4:** Every archive worker knows the catalog. Every archive worker has a key. Sam knows the catalog. Must Sam have a key? What changes if you also learn that Sam is an archive worker?

**Worked answer:** Catalog knowledge alone does not prove Sam has a key. Sam could know the catalog without working in the archive and have no key. If Sam is an archive worker, both rules apply to Sam: Sam knows the catalog and has a key. Shared starting conditions support both results for a matching case, without connecting the results to each other.

## 11 | Take this into your assigned work

Do the **conditional-diagramming drills first**, then move to the **LSAT questions**. In the drills, write the complete conditions before shortening them. Check your arrow and its contrapositive. When two rules appear, look for an exact match before combining them.

In the LSAT questions, identify the rule or chain that proves each answer. Check that the answer preserves the complete conditions. **Requires approval** does not become **has received approval**. If you cannot prove an answer, try a counterexample: can the given statements be true while the answer is false?
