* **Defining an LSAT Argument**: Joshua Fisseha clarified that in the LSAT context, an argument is defined strictly as a relationship between premises and a conclusion, rather than a disagreement between two parties. They emphasized that if premises and a conclusion are not both present, the text does not constitute an argument ([00:04:35](#00:04:35)).

* **Identifying Conclusions**: Joshua Fisseha defined a conclusion as a specific claim an author makes, which must be an opinion rather than a statement of fact. They explained that a conclusion must be supported by at least one other statement, which serves as a premise ([00:07:59](#00:07:59)) ([00:12:07](#00:12:07)).

* **The "Why Should I Believe This?" Test**: To identify a conclusion, Joshua Fisseha recommended asking, "Why should I believe this?" If the author provides support for the claim in the form of premises, it qualifies as a conclusion. They illustrated this using an example about political figures to clarify the distinction between a claim and the evidence supporting it ([00:14:04](#00:14:04)) ([00:20:04](#00:20:04)).

* **Sub-Conclusions and Hierarchies**: Joshua Fisseha explained the concept of sub-conclusions (intermediate conclusions), which function as both a premise for the main conclusion and as a conclusion for their own supporting premises. To distinguish the main conclusion from a sub-conclusion, they suggested asking which claim makes the other more believable ([00:22:54](#00:22:54)) ([00:27:43](#00:27:43)).

* **Identifying Opposing Viewpoints**: Joshua Fisseha noted that LSAT stimuli often include opposing viewpoints—frequently attributed to groups like scientists or critics—which the author intends to rebut. They advised students to remain alert for these viewpoints as the author typically uses them as target practice ([00:32:00](#00:32:00)).

* **Using Pivot Words**: Joshua Fisseha highlighted the importance of pivot words, such as "however" or "yet," which typically signal a transition to the author’s own argument or conclusion. These signals help students identify shifts in perspective within the passage ([00:34:17](#00:34:17)).

* **Understanding Concessions**: Joshua Fisseha described a "concession" as a scenario where an author acknowledges a point made by an opposing side while still maintaining their own stance. They clarified that this is not neutral, but rather a way for the author to address the opposing side before correcting or rebutting it ([00:36:20](#00:36:20)) ([00:39:18](#00:39:18)).

* **Background Information**: Joshua Fisseha categorized background information as context or definitions provided in a stimulus that are distinct from the argument itself. * **Categorizing Claims**: Joshua Fisseha summarized that every claim in an LSAT stimulus falls into one of four buckets: Main Conclusion, Support (including sub-conclusions), Opposing Argument (including concessions), and Background Information (Neutral).
