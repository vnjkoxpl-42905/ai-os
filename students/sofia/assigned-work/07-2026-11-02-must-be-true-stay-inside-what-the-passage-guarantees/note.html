Must Be True: stay inside what the passage guarantees

Keep this open beside a Must Be True set. The right answer is something the passage guarantees. It is not what is likely, and it is not what the author would like to be true.

The steps

1 · Mark each sentence as a rule or a fact. A rule says what follows when something happens. A fact says what is true about one case.

2 · Decide the job. Either apply a fact to a rule, or combine rules. Those are the two things a rule-based passage can ask.

3 · Read any OR or AND slowly. The test rarely adds one for decoration. Work out what it allows and what it rules out.

4 · Say what you do not know. Write the wrong version of each rule in your head, and keep it beside the real one.

5 · Sort the answers from weakest to strongest. Then check each against the passage, starting at the weak end.

6 · Take the answer the passage fully backs. If you cannot point to the sentences that guarantee it, it is not the answer.

What a rule lets you say

Rule: if the festival is on, the street is closed.

You learn You can say
The festival is on The street is closed
The street is closed Nothing about the festival
The festival is off Nothing about the street
The street is open The festival is not on

The second and third rows are the bait. Closed streets have other causes. An off festival does not open a street.

AND and OR

Where it sits AND OR
In the starting side The result follows only when both are met The result follows when either is met, or both
In the result Both follow together At least one follows, and possibly both

When one half of an OR in the result is ruled out, the other half has to be true. Combine that with a second rule and you can reach a conclusion the passage never states.

Strength

A bigger claim needs more from the passage. Picture a bet on a random student's LSAT score: 150 is safer than 180.

Wording Examples
Small at least one, some, can, may
Big every, all, never, always, only, not possible

Words are a clue. The test is how much the passage has to give you. An answer with every in it is fine when a rule already covers every case. An answer about any entity in the world is too wide when the argument is about one situation.

The same bet in two other questions

Necessary Assumption. Negate the answer. If the argument falls apart, the author needed it. The safest-bet idea applies here too, so lean toward answers that need less.

Must Be False. The right answer contradicts something the passage gives you, or something you can work out by combining two claims. In PT127 Section 1, Question 25, "it is not possible to raise cacti" tempted. The passage never ruled that out, so it could be true, which means it cannot be the answer.

Things that will fool you

A fact about a copy. A shop selling a poster tells you nothing about the original. All you know is that a poster exists.

A rule run backwards. The result being present tells you nothing about the starting side.

Soft wording about the wrong idea. A weak answer still has to be about what the passage guarantees.

A strong word the passage backs. Do not throw an answer out for one word. Check what the passage gives you.

Strengthen and weaken questions. They treat strength differently. Know which question you are in before you judge an answer's strength.
