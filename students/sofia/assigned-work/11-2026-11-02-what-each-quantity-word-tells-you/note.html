What Each Quantity
Word Tells You

Some, most, a minority and "not lower than" each allow one amount and rule out another. When you read them as a loose feeling, the answers that look close get through. Read them as amounts, and a few of them flip.

Start here

Say each word as an amount

These words are easy to read past. Say what each one means in numbers before you use it.

Some

At least one. It could be one, or it could be all of them.

Sounds likesome, sometimes, can, not all
Use itSay "at least one" out loud every time. "Some are not" means at least one is not.

Most

More than half of the group.

Sounds likemost, majority, usually, typically
Use itIf most are X, then at least one is X too.

A minority

50% or less. The easy way to remember it: not most.

Sounds likeonly a minority, a minority of
Use itFlip it. A minority are X means most are not X.
A minority are hardworkingMost are not hardworking.
A minority are not hardworkingMost are hardworking.

The "not" and the "is" swap places. That is all the flip does.

Your plan was to match the quantity words in the argument with the quantity words in the answer. That plan was sound. The test only hid "most" behind the word "minority."
The flaw in the argument

A small group can sit entirely inside a big one

Picture a flying club with 400 members. The whole town has only 12 pilots, and all 12 belong to the club.

Twelve out of 400 is a small share, so pilots are a minority of the club. But nobody is left outside. Every pilot in town is already in the club.

  1. Draw two groups. The big group is the club. The small group is pilots.
  2. Ask where the small group can sit. It can sit entirely inside the big one.
  3. Check what an argument would need. An argument that says "so there must be pilots outside the club" needs someone outside. The numbers do not say anyone is.
The trap that costs the mostA small share of a big group tells you how big the small group is next to the big one. It does not tell you anyone is outside. When you hear "so there must be others," ask where the argument got them.
You put it in your own words: just because they're a minority doesn't mean there's some left over. And you called it a set question before I did.
Parallel Flaw

The answer cannot bring in a new kind of claim

It is tempting to think a parallel flaw answer can match loosely. It cannot. The flaw has to match, and so does every kind of claim in the argument.

  1. List the kinds of claims in the argument. Is there an if-then rule? A cause? A comparison? A "should"? A quantity word?
  2. Look at each answer for a kind that is not on your list. If the argument has no "should," an answer with a "should" is out.
  3. Look the other way too. If the argument has an if-then rule, the answer needs one as well.
should / oughtbecause / causesif ... thentaller than
Flip first, then compare"Minority" and "most" line up only after the flip: "a minority are X" says the same thing as "most are not X." "All" and "if" can both be conditional rules. Look at what kind of claim it is. Then decide.

Do not pick an answer that breaks this because the others look worse. If you are down to one answer that adds a "should," go back and read the others again.

Must Be False

Most gives you a number to work with

Must Be False is Must Be True turned around. It asks which answer has to be not true, so you want the one the passage contradicts. An answer that could be true is out.

Say a camp has 20 kids, and more than half of them swam today.

  1. Turn "more than half" into a count. More than half of 20 is at least 11.
  2. Work out what is left over. At least 11 swam, so at most 9 did not.
  3. Test each answer against that. "Exactly 10 did not swim" must be false. "Exactly 9 did not swim" could be true. "Every kid swam" could be true.

The cactus and orange tree question ran on the same count. More than half of the country was easy for one plant or the other, so less than half could be both humid and cold. "Exactly half" had to be false.

Why the other answer failsA warm, dry region is not humid and not cold, and it follows every rule in the passage. So "some parts are neither cold nor humid" could be true, which makes it a wrong answer on Must Be False.
You saw that half of the country being both humid and cold would be impossible for most of the country. Then I asked for the most that could be left over, and the count did the rest.
Where to start

Start with the firmest sentence

When a passage has several sentences, begin with the one that leaves the least room. Words like each, all and no are the firmest. Everything else gets read against it.

Here is the idea in a new setting. Each runner who finished in under 40 minutes got a medal. Dana did not get a medal.

  1. Start with the firm sentence. Under 40 minutes, then a medal.
  2. Flip it. No medal, then not under 40 minutes.
  3. Bring in the fact. Dana had no medal, so Dana did not finish in under 40 minutes.
  4. Read the edge word. "Not under 40" includes exactly 40. It does not mean "over 40."
The sneaky one"Not lower than" includes the line. "Higher than" does not. That was the Spanish question: B minus was inside "not lower than B minus." Ask the same about "at least," "no more than" and "more than." Does the line itself count?
You flipped the rule and carried it to "most" before I added anything. Every part of your reasoning held. The only change was one word.
Many sentences

Anchor on the idea that repeats

Here is the idea in a new setting. Most of the club's members are volunteers. Every volunteer wears a badge. No one under 16 can be a volunteer.

The word "volunteer" shows up in all three sentences. Make it your anchor.

  1. Find the idea that repeats. Here it is "volunteer."
  2. Ask what you know about it. A volunteer wears a badge and is 16 or older.
  3. Combine as you read. Most members are volunteers, so most members wear a badge and are 16 or older.
This is about speedYou can get these right without an anchor. You did, with a full chain. The anchor makes the same work take less time, because you are not holding every sentence in your head at once.
Must Be True EXCEPTFour answers must be true. The odd one out could be false. This question type is less common, but it uses the same reasoning as Must Be True.
You built the chain, saw that one answer did not fit it, and picked that answer. Reps will make the chain come faster.

The order to follow

On a question full of quantity words

  1. Say each quantity word as an amount. Some is at least one. Most is more than half. A minority is 50% or less.
  2. Flip any minority into most. A minority are X means most are not X.
  3. Start with the firmest sentence (each, all, no) and write its flip.
  4. Find the idea that repeats. Ask what you know about it, and combine as you read.
  5. When most or half is involved, work out what is left over.
  6. Read the edge words: not lower than, at least, more than. Does the line itself count?
  7. On Parallel Flaw, list the kinds of claims in the argument. Strike any answer that adds a new kind.
