# Session recap · 2026-09-09

Hey Sofia,

Two parallel questions today, one causal and one conditional, and a new way to write your wrong answer journal. You had the right instincts on both questions. The work now is turning instinct into a check you run before you look at the answers.

## What we worked on

- SMACKER: the five places a question can beat you. Structure, Method of reasoning, Assumption, Keywords, Ranking the choices. Every miss is one of these five.
- Wrong answer journal, upgraded. "I didn't understand it" is true but useless. Which of the five went wrong? That is the entry.
- The principal question (causal parallel). The argument is a causal claim, then a correlation, then a fact about what the principal concluded. The right answer needs all three, in that order.
- The societal values question (conditional parallel). The stimulus denies the sufficient condition and concludes the necessary condition fails. False contrapositive.
- Keywords that actually matter: "must", "invariably", and the pivot "but". Premise indicators like "since" tell you nothing, because every argument has a premise.

## The one thing to hold onto

Find the flaw before you touch the answer choices.

On the principal question, the flaw was a root cause assumed fixed just because the symptom disappeared. Once you name that, A falls apart on its own: A runs the timeline backwards (they stopped overeating, then the weight gain stopped). D runs it the right way (the complaints stopped, then the manager assumed the workers were busy). You could not see that difference until you had the flaw in hand.

On the societal values question, the flaw was sufficient versus necessary. Once you named it, B was dead before you read it to the end: "usually" does not match "invariably", and there is no pivot.

Name the crime, then go find the answer that commits the same crime. Half of parallel flaw questions are a classic: sufficient versus necessary, or correlation read as cause.

## Where it slipped

You held onto "since" and "must" and nearly picked B over D. Normal. Premise indicators feel important because they are easy to spot. They are not a standout feature. A pivot is, because not every argument has one. When you see "but", "yet", or "however" in the stimulus, the right answer almost has to carry one too.

The other slip was strength. "Invariably" means every time. An answer that says "usually" is off before you check anything else. Match the strength first; it is the fastest elimination you have.
