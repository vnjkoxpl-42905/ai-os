# Must Be True and Sufficient Assumption: Which Way Does the Proof Run?

The two question types can use the same logical chain. What changes is the missing piece. In Must Be True, the statements are already given and must prove the answer. In Sufficient Assumption, the conclusion is already stated and the answer must supply enough support to prove it.

| Question | What you have | What the answer must do |
|---|---|---|
| Must Be True | Given facts or rules | State something the given information guarantees. |
| Sufficient Assumption | An argument with a conclusion | Add a premise that makes the conclusion follow. |

The wording of a question stem can be unfamiliar. Ask where the proof must start, then use that direction to identify the task.

## For Must Be True, carry the chain forward

Suppose a passage says that when a crop fails, nearby suppliers lose business. It also says that a fuel seller cannot sell enough to make a profit. The supported takeaway is modest: the crop failure can affect businesses beyond the farm.

Two tempting changes would overstate the evidence:

- **Changing the trigger:** Below-average rainfall is not the same as a failed crop. The crop could survive a slightly dry season. If the passage starts its chain at crop failure, an answer cannot start it at any rainfall below average.
- **Changing the result:** Losing business does not necessarily mean losing money overall. A supplier could earn less than expected and still make a profit.

Also check whether an answer reverses the chain. From *crop failure leads suppliers to lose business*, you cannot conclude that every supplier who loses business experienced a crop failure. Suppliers can lose business for other reasons.

Use this sequence:

1. Mark the exact event that starts the chain.
2. Follow only the consequences the passage supplies.
3. State a conservative result before reading the choices.
4. For each choice, ask: Which sentence proves every part of this claim? If one part introduces a new judgment, stronger trigger, or larger consequence, set it aside.

That last check catches words such as *responsible* or *national crisis* when the passage only describes local economic effects. A believable claim is still insufficient if the passage does not prove it.

## For Sufficient Assumption, bridge the premise to the conclusion

This time the conclusion is the target. Separate it from the premises, then locate the change in subject or scope.

In the animal example, the evidence concerned living animals with similar skeletons but different hunting behavior. The conclusion concerned what we can infer about extinct animals from skeletal anatomy alone. The observation about living animals does not automatically establish the conclusion about extinct animals. The answer must connect those two ideas.

An effective prephrase names both sides of that bridge: **if the observation about living animals holds, then skeletal anatomy alone is inadequate for the proposed inference about extinct animals.** The exact wording can vary, but the premise must reach the conclusion without an extra unstated step.

Check a proposed assumption in two passes:

1. Does its **if** side connect to what the premises actually establish?
2. Does its **then** side reach the conclusion as written, including its subject and strength?

If either side changes the claim, the bridge is incomplete. If both connect, you have a proof. That is a better reason to keep an answer than a vague sense that its words match.

## Use your first supported answer, then move

On a hard question, you identified the premise-to-conclusion bridge and selected the answer that supplied it. Under a clock, keep that choice once you can explain both connections. Reopening it without a specific flaw can turn a sound inference into second guessing.
