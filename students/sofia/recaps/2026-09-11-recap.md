# Session recap · 2026-09-11

For conditional Must Be True questions, the first job is to label what each sentence is doing. That keeps the stimulus manageable and prevents an answer choice from quietly changing the logic.

## Separate facts from rules

A **fact** is something the stimulus gives you as true about a case or situation.

A **rule** is a general conditional: if its trigger is present, its result must follow. A rule does not tell you that the trigger actually occurred.

That distinction matters because some Must Be True stimuli contain rules but no facts. The contract-negotiator set was an example. The statements supplied several general relationships, but no separate case that triggered one of them. The job there was to chain the rules.

When a fact is supplied, ask whether it matches a rule's trigger. If the direct trigger is absent, check whether a known result is false so that you can use the contrapositive.

## Find the bridge

When there are multiple rules, look for the idea that appears in the necessary position of one rule and the sufficient position of another. That repeated idea is the bridge.

```text
A → B
B → C

Therefore: A → C
```

The useful inference is usually between the two loose ends, A and C. The repeated idea, B, is there to connect them. Do not chain two statements merely because they share a word; the word has to sit in the position needed for the link.

## Keep the arrow honest

Write the trigger first and the guaranteed result second. Then test the answer against that exact direction.

```text
Burning a large amount of trash in an incinerator → wasting resources

Valid contrapositive:
not wasting resources → do not burn a large amount of trash in an incinerator

Invalid reversal:
wasting resources → burning a large amount of trash in an incinerator
```

The first line is the conditional relationship. The second is its contrapositive: reverse the direction and negate both sides. The third is the invalid reversal. Wasting resources does not prove that this particular incinerator practice caused it.

When your reading and your diagram seem to disagree, return to the sentence that created the arrow. Ask two questions:

1. What is the trigger?
2. What result does the sentence guarantee?

That check is more reliable than rebuilding the whole stimulus from memory. Keep each statement in the role the stimulus gives it. Do not invent a fact to trigger a rule, and do not turn a general rule into a claim that its trigger occurred.
