# Conditional Must Be True: Facts, Rules, and Bridges

A conditional Must Be True question has two basic moves. You either connect one rule to another, or use a fact to activate a rule. Before you combine anything, identify which kind of move the stimulus gives you.

Unlike an argument question, you are not looking for a conclusion to challenge. Accept every statement as true. Your only question is: **what new information do these statements guarantee?**

## The method at a glance

| Pieces you have | Move | What you produce |
|---|---|---|
| Rule + rule | **Chaining** | A new rule |
| Fact + rule | **Triggering** | A new fact |

Do not start diagramming everything together. First separate the rules from the facts. Then ask whether you should be chaining or triggering.

## 1. Know what each statement does

| Type | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Fact** | Describes the situation you actually have | Lena is a pilot. |
| **Rule** | States what must follow if a condition is met | `pilot → trained` |

A rule describes a relationship. It tells you what follows **if** its trigger is met. A fact tells you what is true in the situation. The rule does not prove its own trigger. You need a fact to do that.

### Look for certainty, not just a keyword

Words such as **if**, **only**, **all**, **every**, and **none** often signal a rule because they create a guarantee. Words such as **often**, **can**, **some**, and **most** do not create that same guarantee by themselves.

The real test is simple: if the first part is true, **must** the second part follow? If not, you do not have a conditional rule.

## 2. Chaining: rule + rule produces a new rule

When the stimulus gives you several rules and no fact that activates them, look for a **bridge**.

A bridge is the overlapping idea that sits at the end of one rule and the beginning of another. It is the middle step that lets you move from the first idea to the last.

A usable bridge appears in one of two ways:

1. The **same idea appears in opposite positions**: the result of one rule and the trigger of another.
2. **Opposite versions of an idea appear in the same position**: taking a contrapositive will place the shared idea in opposite positions.

### Step 1: Diagram each rule

```text
pilot → trained
trained → licensed
```

### Step 2: Find the overlapping idea

`trained` is the bridge because it is:

- the result of the first rule; and
- the trigger of the second rule.

The first rule takes you **to** trained. The second rule takes you **from** trained. The two rules interlock there.

### Step 3: Connect the loose ends

```text
pilot → licensed
```

The bridge is used to build the inference, but it usually disappears from the new rule. The conclusion connects the two ideas left on the outside: `pilot` and `licensed`.

### A repeated word is not always a bridge

The position has to work:

```text
A → B
B → C

Valid chain: A → C
```

But this does not chain:

```text
A → B
C → B
```

Both rules end at `B`. Neither one continues from it. The same idea appears twice, but the rules do not interlock.

### Sometimes the bridge is hidden in a contrapositive

If the ideas almost line up, write the valid contrapositive and check again:

```text
pilot → trained
not licensed → not trained

Contrapositive of the second rule:
trained → licensed

Now they chain:
pilot → trained → licensed
```

Contraposing can reveal a bridge. Reversing a rule cannot.

### The answer may test any link you had to build

With a longer chain, the answer does not have to connect the very first and very last ideas. If the chain is:

```text
A → B → C → D
```

then `A → C`, `B → D`, and `A → D` are all valid inferences. The test can reward any connection that required you to move through at least one bridge.

## 3. Triggering: fact + rule produces a new fact

When the stimulus gives you a rule and a fact, compare the fact with both sides of the rule. The fact can activate the rule in one of two valid ways.

### The fact confirms the trigger

```text
Rule: pilot → trained
Fact: Lena is a pilot.

Therefore: Lena is trained.
```

The fact places Lena on the left side of the rule. Once the trigger is established, the result must follow.

### The fact denies the required result

```text
Rule: pilot → trained
Fact: Lena is not trained.

Therefore: Lena is not a pilot.
```

This uses the contrapositive. Reverse the rule and negate both sides:

```text
pilot → trained
not trained → not pilot
```

### The two facts that do not activate the rule

Starting rule: `pilot → trained`

```text
Fact: Lena is trained.
```

That does not prove she is a pilot. She could have received training for another reason.

```text
Fact: Lena is not a pilot.
```

That does not prove she is untrained. She could still be trained for something else.

The rule has no power in either situation. Do not force an inference.

## 4. Know the four possible moves

Starting rule: `A → B`

| What the fact establishes | What you may conclude | Result |
|---|---|---|
| `A` | `B` | Valid: apply the rule forward |
| `not B` | `not A` | Valid: use the contrapositive |
| `B` | Nothing about `A` | Invalid reversal |
| `not A` | Nothing about `B` | Invalid negation |

### Use the contrapositive when the move is hidden

You do not need to write the contrapositive of every rule forever. If the chain or trigger is already clear, use it. If you are staring at the rules and cannot see what to do, contrapose one of them and check again. The missing connection will often become visible.

### Diagram when the notation will do work

Diagram when you have several rules, a longer chain, or answer choices that are difficult to track in words. The notation should help you:

- see which ideas can actually connect;
- test an intermediate link in a longer chain;
- catch an answer that reverses or negates a rule; or
- take the contrapositive of the whole chain.

If the valid move is already clear from the sentence, make it and keep going. The diagram is a tool for making the relationship visible, not an extra task you must perform on every question.

### Test a conditional answer by its trigger

When an answer choice gives you a new conditional rule, start with its trigger. Ask:

1. Does this idea appear as a trigger anywhere in the stimulus or in a valid inference?
2. If not, could a contrapositive place it there?
3. Can the proposed result actually be reached from that trigger?

If the answer begins from a condition the stimulus never establishes in that position, the answer cannot be derived. Eliminate it. This is faster and more reliable than trying to make the whole answer sound reasonable.

## 5. Use the same process every time

1. Label each statement as a **fact** or a **rule**.
2. If you have **rule + rule**, look for an overlapping idea that lets the rules interlock.
3. If you have **fact + rule**, check whether the fact confirms the trigger or denies the required result.
4. Write only the inference the rule guarantees.
5. Check the answer choices for that inference. For a conditional answer, test its trigger first. If an answer reverses or negates the rule, eliminate it.
