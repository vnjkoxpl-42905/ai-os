# Sufficient Assumptions: Find the Missing Link

When a conclusion is conditional, diagramming the premises is only the first step. A sufficient assumption must complete a route from the conclusion's sufficient side to its necessary side. Keep the conclusion separate from the evidence until the answer supplies that route.

## Separate the claim from the support

Suppose an author concludes:

```text
If a democracy can thrive, it has effective news media.
Thrive → effective media
```

That arrow is the claim to be proved. You cannot place it among the established rules merely because the author wrote it. The premises might establish only:

```text
Thrive → knowledgeable electorate
Knowledgeable electorate → access to unbiased information
```

Together, the premises show `thrive → access to unbiased information`. They still do not show `thrive → effective media`. The path stops at unbiased information. The missing link is what would take that endpoint to effective media.

One sufficient answer would give:

```text
Access to unbiased information → effective media
```

Then the entire route works:

```text
Thrive → knowledgeable electorate → access to unbiased information
       → effective media
```

An answer can express the same link as its contrapositive: `not effective media → no access to unbiased information`. Reverse and negate both sides to check it. Do not merely reverse the arrow.

## Use both halves of the conclusion to locate the gap

Start with the conclusion's two sides. Ask which side appears in the premises and how far its path goes. Here, *thrive* is the starting point in both the conclusion and the evidence. The evidence extends it through knowledge to unbiased information. The other side, *effective media*, has no established connection to that chain.

Your prediction is therefore specific: the answer must connect something on the established path to effective media. It might connect the last endpoint, unbiased information, or an earlier point such as a knowledgeable electorate. A link from unbiased information is the most direct route. In every case, test the completed chain from the conclusion's left side all the way to its right side.

This prevents a subtle mistake: treating the conclusion's own arrow as evidence. If the only place two ideas are linked is the sentence the author is trying to prove, the argument still has a gap.

## Sufficient Assumption and Must Be True use the same skill differently

| Question | Your job |
|---|---|
| Sufficient Assumption | Add a rule that makes the stated conclusion follow from the premises. |
| Must Be True | Use the given rules and facts to find a conclusion that already follows. |

With `A → B` and `B → C` in the premises, a Must Be True answer can state `A → C`. If the argument *concludes* `A → C` but supplies only `A → B`, a Sufficient Assumption answer could add `B → C`. The arrows are familiar in both tasks; the difference is whether you are extracting a result or supplying the connection needed to prove one.

## Make the arrows answer a real question

A diagram represents what the words mean. It is not a reason to skip their meaning. “Specialized training is required to become a literature professor” means `literature professor → specialized training`. It does not mean `specialized training → literature professor`. The ability to judge literary greatness may also require specialized training. Two routes to the same necessary condition do not establish a relationship between their starting points.

```text
Judge greatness → specialized training
Literature professor → specialized training
```

From these two arrows alone, you cannot infer that everyone who can judge greatness is a professor, or that everyone who is not a professor cannot judge greatness. But if a person lacks specialized training, the contrapositives do let you rule out both activities that require it. Check each conclusion against an actual path in the correct direction.

## A repeatable process

1. Identify the conclusion and diagram its sufficient and necessary sides. Set that arrow aside as the claim to prove.
2. Diagram the premises in ordinary language. Join only arrows whose conditions truly match.
3. Follow the premise chain from the conclusion's sufficient side. Mark where it stops.
4. Predict the missing link from that stopping point to the conclusion's necessary side. Check the answer in both its original form and its contrapositive.
5. Run the complete chain. If it reaches the conclusion's necessary side without adding any other assumption, the answer is sufficient.
