# Session recap · 2026-09-09

Hey Charles,

We finished method of reasoning and spent most of the hour on conditionals. That was a lot in one sitting, and it is the part of Logical Reasoning that everything else leans on. Expect it to take a few passes before it feels automatic.

## What we worked on

- Structure first, then method. Find where the author starts (the premise) and where they end (the conclusion), including any stop along the way (a sub-conclusion). Then ask how they got there: conditional, causal, or comparative.
- Concession: the author gives the other side a little credit before disagreeing. It is not a premise.
- Comparative reasoning. Two things alike in one way are not automatically alike in another. The comparison only works when the similarity is relevant to the conclusion.
- Sufficient and necessary. A sufficient condition guarantees the outcome. A necessary condition is a requirement. A 180 gets you in; an LSAT score is required to apply. Those are different claims.
- The contrapositive: negate both sides and reverse them. It is the only other thing a conditional tells you.
- The three traps, all one mistake: treating a requirement as a guarantee, treating one guaranteed path as the only path, and mixing up overlapping groups ("all Americans are patriots" does not make every patriot American).
- Translation. Group 1 words (if, when, all, any) introduce the sufficient side. Group 2 (only if, must, requires) introduce the necessary side. "Unless": negate the sufficient side, keep the necessary side. When Group 3 and Group 4 words both appear, Group 3 wins.
- Chains. Questions either apply a fact to a rule, or combine rules into a chain and ask what follows. To find the contrapositive of a chain, negate the point you are testing and walk backwards. Everything after that point is irrelevant.

## The one thing to hold onto

If the train to Toronto is the rule, "took the train" guarantees "arrived in Toronto". That is all it says.

Not taking the train tells you nothing. You could have flown. Arriving in Toronto tells you nothing either. The one inference you get for free is the contrapositive: if you never arrived in Toronto, you did not take the train.

Every conditional trap on this test is a version of assuming more than that. When a question feels intuitive, that is the moment to slow down and check which side of the arrow you are on.
