# Some, Most, and All: Which Conclusions Actually Follow

Quantity statements tell you how much of one group has a trait. The group after “of” is your starting group. Keep it fixed as you reason. Most mistakes come from silently switching to the other group or treating a possible amount as a proven amount.

## The three quantities

| Statement | What it guarantees | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| Some A are B | At least one A is B | It could be a small minority, a majority, or all A. |
| Most A are B | More than half of A are B | It could be all A. Nothing establishes how much of B is A. |
| All A are B | Every A is B | B may include many things outside A. |

“Most” means **more than 50%**, not necessarily exactly 51%. The minimum whole-number count depends on the size of the group. Among five apples, three is most. Among one hundred apples, fifty-one is most.

If a group exists, `all A are B` establishes `most A are B`, and `most A are B` establishes `some A are B`. The reverse steps do not work. “Some” permits “all” as a possibility; it does not prove it.

## Keep the direction when you switch groups

`Some apples are green` also means `some green things are apples`. The same apple witnesses both statements. “Some” is reversible.

`Most apples are green` establishes `some green things are apples`, but it does **not** establish `most green things are apples`. Green things may include a vast number of objects that are not apples. Likewise, “most apples are green” gives you no basis to claim that any apples are *not* green. All apples could be green.

When you read “most A are B,” ask: **Most of which group?** The answer is A. The proportion is measured against A, not B.

## Two majorities of one group must meet

Suppose most of a class takes history, and most of the same class takes biology. At least one student must take both. If the groups did not overlap, two separate groups each containing more than half the class would not fit inside the class.

```text
Most A are B.
Most A are C.
Therefore, some B are C.
```

The repeated starting group matters. Two “most” statements about different starting groups do not automatically create this overlap.

## Carry a quantity through a universal rule

```text
Some apples are green.
All green apples are edible.
Therefore, some apples are edible.
```

The second rule applies to the green apples identified by the first statement. You can carry that same group forward. If **most** apples are green, then most apples are edible under that universal rule.

Check the middle idea before chaining. The rule must apply to the things already identified. “Some apples are green” plus “most green things are edible” is insufficient: the green apples might all be among the green things that are not edible.

## Compare total group sizes without reversing “most”

Consider two groups, banjo players and guitar players. Suppose **most banjo players also play guitar**, while **most guitar players do not play banjo**.

Let the overlap be the people who play both. The overlap is more than half of the banjo group. Yet it is less than half of the guitar group, because the majority of guitar players are outside the overlap. The same overlap cannot be more than half of a larger group and less than half of a smaller group. The guitar group must therefore be larger.

You did not reverse either “most” statement. You compared how the shared overlap sits inside each total group.

## A check for each quantity question

1. Name the starting group in each statement.
2. Mark what is guaranteed: at least one, more than half, or every member.
3. If the groups overlap, ask whether the same people are being counted on both sides.
4. Reject a backward “most” inference unless the premises independently establish it.
5. Test a tempting answer against a possible arrangement of the groups. If the premises can be true while the answer is false, the answer is not guaranteed.
