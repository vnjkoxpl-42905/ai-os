*Session recap · November 14*

# Sort the Rules from the Facts

Most students read a must be true stimulus as one block and hunt the answer choices for something that sounds right. There is no conclusion to find in these. There are rules, which say what would follow if something happened, and facts, which say what actually happened. The check that works every time is to sort them, then see which rule a fact sets off.

## A rule says what would follow. A fact says what happened

*Rules and facts*

Think of rules as laws. A lawyer takes what a client says happened and checks it against the law to see what follows. Must be true questions ask you to do the same thing.

**Rule.** What would follow if something is true. It's a hypothetical, so it never says the thing actually happened.
Looks like: If a book is returned late, the borrower pays a fine.
Test: Does it say what would follow?

**Fact.** What actually happened, about a particular person or thing.
Looks like: Dana returned her book late.
Test: Is it about what really happened here?

Put the two together and you can say something about one person. The rule says late books are fined. The fact says Dana's book was late. So Dana pays a fine.

> **A rule never says it happened.** "If a book is returned late, the borrower pays a fine" doesn't tell you that anyone returned a book late. **Never invent a fact to set a rule off.** Some stimuli are rules only, and section 4 shows what to do with those.

## Sort first, then look for the rule a fact sets off

*The method*

A must be true stimulus has no conclusion. If you hunt for one, you end up treating a fact as the thing being argued for. So don't read for a conclusion. Read for rules and facts, and keep an eye out for the conditional words you already know: if, when, all, every, any, only.

1. **Find the rules.** Each conditional word starts one. Say it as a plain if-then sentence, and keep full words, not one or two letters.
2. **Find the facts.** A fact is a sentence about what happened to a particular person or thing. Keep it apart from the rules.
3. **Write each rule's contrapositive.** It's the same rule said another way: flip the two parts and negate both. The test writers often hand you the fact that sets off the contrapositive, and students miss it because they never wrote it down.
4. **See which rule a fact sets off.** If the fact is the "if" half of a rule or of a contrapositive, follow it to the "then" half. Rule: if a book is returned late, the borrower pays a fine. Contrapositive: if the borrower pays no fine, the book wasn't returned late. Fact: Sam paid no fine. That's the "if" half of the contrapositive, so Sam's book wasn't returned late.
5. **Underline the sentence that proves your answer.** Small details like tense and "no" slip out of memory. With the support underlined, you can check every choice against it.

> **There's no conclusion to find.** Don't highlight for a conclusion on these. Look for the rules, look for the facts, and ask what they prove together. **The answer is the choice that is proven by what you were given.**

> When I asked how to find a contrapositive, you said to flip the two parts and negate both, before I'd shown you anything. That's the right method, and it's the one these questions are usually built around.

## Not shorter doesn't mean longer

*Negating*

To negate an idea, say it didn't happen. Don't reach for its opposite. Here's a rule and its contrapositive.

**New desk is open → wait time drops**

**Wait time doesn't drop → new desk isn't open**

Look at the "if" half of the contrapositive: the wait time doesn't drop. That could mean it stayed the same. It doesn't have to mean the wait went up. Your bank balance works the same way. If it didn't go down, it may have stayed put.

> **The trap that costs the most.** Swapping a negation for its opposite. **Not decreased isn't increased.** It means decreased didn't happen, and increased is only one of the ways that can be true.

Two more checks come from the same place. Both are about reading what the stimulus actually says.

> **Check the tense.** A rule about what would have happened if something had been done ten years ago says nothing about what happens from now on. An answer about the future can't be proven from a stimulus about the past. **Underline the words that tell you when.**

The last check is about the answer choices themselves.

> **Match the meaning, not the words.** A stimulus can say a rule was not followed while the right answer says it was violated. Those mean the same thing in different words. **Ask what each choice says, not which words it shares with the stimulus.**

> On the emissions question, you said the regulations weren't followed before you looked at a single answer choice. That was the right inference, and you got there fast. Say what must be true first, then find the choice that says the same thing in different words.

## No fact? Link the rules instead

*Only rules*

Some stimuli give you no fact at all. They hand you two or three rules, and the work is to link them. Look for an idea that shows up twice: once at the end of one rule and once at the start of the next.

**Book is returned late → borrower pays a fine**

**Borrower pays a fine → account is flagged**

**Book is returned late → account is flagged**

The repeated idea, "pays a fine," is where the two rules connect. Once it has done that job, it drops out. The answer is usually the first idea and the last one, the head and the tail of the chain. The repeated idea in the middle is rarely the answer, because it's too easy to spot. The whole chain has a contrapositive too: if the account isn't flagged, the book wasn't returned late.

> **Facts and rules are two different jobs.** If the stimulus gives you a fact, apply it to a rule. If it gives you only rules, link them. **Keep the two situations apart.**

## Sort, apply, then prove

*Try it*

**Rule or fact? Say it out loud, then tap to check**
*Does it say what would follow, or what actually happened?*

- If a customer buys a ticket, the customer gets a seat. **[Rule]** Rule. It says what would follow if someone buys a ticket. It doesn't say anyone did.
- Priya bought a ticket. **[Fact]** Fact. It's about one person and what she did.
- Every package over ten pounds ships by truck. **[Rule]** Rule. "Every" starts a rule: if a package is over ten pounds, it ships by truck.
- The blue package weighs twelve pounds. **[Fact]** Fact. It's about one package.
- The sensor sounds the alarm when it detects smoke. **[Rule]** Rule, even though it names one sensor. "When" starts a rule: if it detects smoke, it sounds the alarm. It doesn't say smoke was ever detected.

**What follows? Tap to check**
*Match each sentence to the rule or to its contrapositive.*

> *If a member books a class, the member gets a free towel. Lena booked Saturday's class. Mo didn't get a towel. Tess got a towel.*

- Lena got a free towel. **[Follows]** Follows. Lena booked, which is the "if" half of the rule. The towel comes with it.
- Mo didn't book a class. **[Follows]** Follows. No towel is the "if" half of the contrapositive, which says no booking.
- Tess booked a class. **[Not proven]** Not proven. A towel is the "then" half of the rule. Nothing says only members who booked get one, so Tess could have gotten hers another way.

**Prove it from what you underlined**
*Which sentence supports the choice? Look at the tense too.*

> *The council said that if the new bike lane opened in March, car traffic on Pine Street would drop by April. Traffic on Pine Street did not drop by April.*

- Traffic on Pine Street went up by April. **[Not proven]** Not proven. "Did not drop" leaves room for traffic staying the same.
- The bike lane did not open in March. **[Follows]** Follows. Traffic not dropping is the "if" half of the contrapositive, so the lane didn't open in March.
- The bike lane will cut traffic once it opens. **[Not proven]** Not proven. The rule is about opening in March. It says nothing about opening later or about the future.
