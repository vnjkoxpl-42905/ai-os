# What Some, Most, and All Mean

In everyday talk, some means a few and most means nearly all. On the LSAT each word is a number, and the numbers are looser than you'd expect. Some can be every one. Most can be every one too. Turn the word into its number, then ask what that number still allows, and you'll know what a sentence guarantees and what it leaves open.

## Some is one or more. Most is over half. All is every one

*Three words*

Picture a box of five pens. Each word sets a minimum, and the minimum is the only thing it guarantees.

**Some.** At least one. Any number from one up to every one fits.
Looks like: Some of the pens are blue.
Test: Is there at least one? Then it fits.

**Most.** More than half. Exactly half isn't enough. Every one still fits.
Looks like: Most of the pens are blue.
Test: Is it more than half?

**All.** Every one. A hundred percent, with no exceptions.
Looks like: All of the pens are blue.
Test: Is even one pen not blue? Then it's false.

Count it out with the five pens. Some needs one blue pen. Most needs three, because two is less than half of five. All needs five.

## A minimum is not a maximum

*What each leaves open*

Each word tells you the fewest. None of them tells you the most, except all. That's where students get tripped up: they hear a ceiling that isn't there.

| The sentence says | Fewest blue pens | Most blue pens |
|---|---|---|
| Some are blue | 1 | 5 |
| Most are blue | 3 | 5 |
| All are blue | 5 | 5 |

Say a room has twenty people and you hear "most of them are from Asia." You'd probably think some of them aren't. The sentence doesn't say that. Everyone in the room could be, and the sentence would still be true. It only rules out half or fewer.

> **The trap that costs the most.** Hearing "some" as "some, but not all." **Some can mean all, and most can mean all.** If every pen is blue, then some are blue and most are blue. The sentence just doesn't tell you whether any pen is left over.

A single exception only breaks all. Say the sentence is "all the pens are blue" and one pen is green: the sentence is false. Say the sentence is "most of the pens are blue" and one pen is green: the sentence can still be true.

> You said three out of five for most, and that's exactly the smallest number that works. You also saw that four and five fit as well, which is the part that feels strange the first time.

## Different words, same number

*Other wording*

The LSAT swaps in other words for these three. Treat each one as the word it matches.

Words: many, majority, every, none

**Many** means some. It sounds big, but it only guarantees at least one. **Majority** means most: more than half. **Every** means all. **None** is all turned around: it says all of them are not blue, so zero are.

> **Many is not most.** Plenty of people hear "many" and think "most." **Many only guarantees what some does.** If you need more than half, the sentence has to say most, majority, or something that means it.

## Two somes prove nothing, but two mosts must meet

*Two sentences together*

Questions often give you two quantity sentences about the same group and ask what has to follow. Start with two sentences about the pens.

**Some pens are blue + some pens are refillable**

Do any pens have to be both? No. The blue pens could all be the ones that aren't refillable, and the refillable pens could all be other pens. Two somes can miss each other. **Nothing certain follows.**

Now change both words to most.

**Most pens are blue + most pens are refillable**

Each group takes more than half of the five pens, so each takes at least three. Three plus three is six, and there are only five pens. The two groups can't fit without sharing, so at least one pen is both blue and refillable. **Some pens are blue and refillable.**

> **Two mosts about the same group must overlap.** Both sentences have to count the same group. If one counts the pens and the other counts something else, the argument doesn't hold. **Check what each sentence is counting before you combine them.**

There are more ways to combine these words, including ones that use all. The rest come up in the practice you'll get next.

## Say the number, then ask what it still allows

*Try it*

**Turn the word into a number, then tap to check**
*Fewest first. Then ask whether more, or all, still fits.*

- Some of the ten mugs are chipped. Could all ten be chipped? **[Yes]** Yes. Some means at least one, and ten is more than one.
- Most of the ten mugs are chipped. What is the fewest chipped mugs? **[Six]** Six. Five is exactly half, and half isn't more than half.
- Most of the ten mugs are chipped. Could every mug be chipped? **[Yes]** Yes. Most sets a minimum only. All ten is more than half.
- Many of the mugs are chipped. Does that mean most are? **[No]** No. Many only guarantees what some does: at least one.
- Some of the mugs are chipped, so some mugs aren't. **[Not proven]** Not proven. Some allows every mug to be chipped, so it can't tell you any mug is left over.

**Which combinations hold? Tap to check**
*Ask what each sentence counts, and whether the groups could miss each other.*

> *In a shop's lamp section, some of the lamps are brass. Most of the lamps are on sale. Most of the lamps are new.*

- At least one lamp is on sale and new. **[Follows]** Follows. Both mosts count the same lamps, and two groups that each take more than half must share at least one lamp.
- At least one lamp is brass and on sale. **[Not proven]** Not proven. The brass lamps could all be among the lamps that aren't on sale.
- Most of the lamps are both on sale and new. **[Not proven]** Not proven. They must share at least one lamp. Nothing says they share more than half of them.
