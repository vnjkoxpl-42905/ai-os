# Main Conclusion Questions

**How to use it.** Keep it open beside a Main Conclusion set. The reading routine and the traps from our session are in your session recap. This page keeps what's specific to the question type.

## What you're looking for

The main conclusion is the one claim the other sentences are there to support. On these questions you find it, say it back in plain words, and pick the answer that means the same thing.

**Question stems:** *Which one of the following best expresses the main conclusion of the argument?* and *main point*.

## The two-question check

| Check | Ask yourself | Keep it if |
|---|---|---|
| **Opinion?** | Could I measure it or look it up? | The answer is no. It's the author's interpretation. |
| **Supported?** | Does another sentence answer "Why should I believe this?" | The answer is yes. The reason is stated outright, in at least one other sentence. |

Both checks have to pass. A fact the author is only reporting fails the first, because there's nothing to argue about. An opinion with no reason behind it fails the second. If the author does argue for something checkable, the second check still decides.

The reason doesn't have to be a good one. The author only has to be trying to give it. If the author is only reporting what other people think, that isn't the conclusion either. Look for the author's own claim.

## Where to look first

Don't assume the conclusion is the last sentence. Two placements are worth checking early.

**1 · The first sentence.** The author opens with an opinion and spends the rest of the argument defending it.

**2 · After a pivot.** Someone else's view comes first. Then *but*, *yet* or *however*. Then the author's own claim. A pivot tells you the author is pushing back. It doesn't tell you the next sentence is the conclusion, so run the check on it.

## Choosing the answer

The right answer means the same thing as the conclusion, sometimes in different words. Put the real names back in for any pointer words, then compare strength: *probably*, *some*, *must* and *should* all change what a claim says.

Common wrong answers include a premise, the last sentence, and a claim that supports something further. A premise feels important because it's in the argument, but nothing supports it. The last sentence tempts you because you want the end to be the point, so check it like any other. A claim with a reason behind it that the author then uses to support something further is a middle step. The conclusion is the claim at the end of the chain.
