# Session recap · 2026-09-08

Hey Avneet,

Necessary assumptions today, start to finish: what they are, the two kinds, and four questions. You got nearly all of them, and the ones you reasoned through out loud were the strongest. That is a good place to be on the second most common question type on the test.

## What we worked on

- What a necessary assumption is: something the author clearly believes and never says. If it is false, the argument cannot work. That is why the answers are worded weakly and broadly, the opposite of a sufficient assumption.
- The two kinds. Linking assumptions must be true (they connect the evidence to the conclusion). Defender assumptions must be false as objections (the author quietly rules something out). An assumption is just an objection with a "not" in front of it.
- Reading the stimulus to predict the kind. Conditionals and repeating ideas point to a linking answer. Causal claims and comparisons point to a defender answer, usually with "not" in it. That is about half of all necessary assumption questions.
- The negation test. Take the "not" out (or put one in) and ask whether the argument collapses. If it does, that answer was required.
- New concept in the conclusion. If a word appears in the conclusion and nowhere in the evidence, the right answer almost always has to carry it. That eliminated B, D, and E on the art criticism question on its own.
- The four questions: art criticism (linking; "judgment" was the new concept), pheromones (defender; causal explanation, so a negated answer), staff reductions and savings (defender; A), deep tillage (defender; A, reverse causation).

## The one thing to hold onto

When the author gives a causal explanation, look for the answer that rules out the arrow going the other way.

The tillage argument saw that deep-tilling farmers lose ten times more topsoil and concluded that deep tilling causes the erosion. But maybe the erosion came first and the farmers are tilling deeper to fix ruined soil. If that is what is happening, telling them to stop tilling fixes nothing. So the author needs A: erosion does not make farmers till more deeply. Negate it and the plan falls apart.

C looked tempting because it also had "not" in it. Negated, it says other tilling methods are viable. The author does not care. They only want farmers to switch to no-till; they never claimed every other method is impossible. The negation has to hurt the conclusion, not just say something the author would disagree with.
