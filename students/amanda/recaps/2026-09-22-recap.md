# Conditional Inferences: Keep the Direction and the Details

A conditional rule tells you what follows when its trigger is met. The key is to identify the two complete ideas, put them on the right sides of the arrow, and keep every qualification attached. Then you can connect rules or apply a stated fact without giving the rule more power than it has.

## Build the rule before making an inference

When a sentence feels dense, take it in this order:

1. Find words that signal a conditional relationship, such as *if*, *only if*, *when*, *always*, or *never*.
2. State the two ideas in ordinary English. Resolve references such as *they* so you know exactly which words the rule concerns.
3. Place the sufficient condition, the trigger, on the left. Place what the rule guarantees on the right.
4. Write the contrapositive by reversing the two sides and negating both.
5. Ask what the rule **does not** tell you. That check catches the tempting reversal and inverse.

For example:

```text
If a word is the first, last, or a main word of a title,
then it should be capitalized.

First/last/main → should capitalize
Should not capitalize → not first/last/main
```

The rule does **not** say that every other word should remain lowercase. A sufficient condition can guarantee an outcome without being the only route to it. The rule also does not say that a word *was* capitalized. *Should* describes the correct treatment, not what someone actually typed.

## Keep the qualification inside the condition

Suppose a second rule says that an article, preposition, or conjunction **with fewer than five letters**, when it appears in the middle of a title, should never be capitalized. The word class, length, and position all matter. Do not turn that into “prepositions are never capitalized.”

```text
Middle of title + short article/preposition/conjunction
    → should not capitalize

Should capitalize
    → not a middle-of-title short article/preposition/conjunction
```

“Short” means fewer than five letters here. A preposition of five or more letters does not satisfy this rule's trigger. If an answer leaves out the length or the position, it may assert more than the rule allows.

Notice how the two title rules can connect. A first, last, or main word **should** be capitalized. Under the second rule's contrapositive, something that should be capitalized cannot be a middle-of-title short article, preposition, or conjunction. The repeated idea, *should capitalize*, is the connection. Keep the word *should* throughout; replacing it with *is capitalized* changes the claim.

## Know whether you are using facts or joining rules

A rule does not say its trigger occurred. In a Must Be True question, you may be given a fact that activates a rule, or you may be given several rules and no triggering fact.

| What the stimulus gives you | Legal move |
|---|---|
| A rule and a matching fact | Apply the rule to that fact. |
| Several rules with a shared condition | Connect the rules and infer the relationship between the endpoints. |
| A fact matching the rule's necessary side | No inference about its sufficient side follows from that alone. |

For example, “If someone burned the building, they are guilty of arson” gives `burned building → guilty of arson`. Its contrapositive is `not guilty of arson → did not burn building`. A fact that someone is not guilty of arson supports the second inference. It does not show that the person is not guilty of *any* crime.

With multiple rules, connect them as you read:

```text
A → B
B → C
Therefore A → C, and not C → not A.
```

The repeated middle idea is the point of connection. The two endpoints are often what the answer tests. But the wording may change between sentences, so check that the supposedly repeated idea really means the same thing before joining the arrows. A matching letter on paper cannot replace an understanding of the sentence.

## Predict before reading the rest of a conditional answer

When an answer begins “If X…,” pause before its “then” clause. Ask what your diagram actually guarantees from X. Say that prediction in plain English, then read the remainder of the answer and compare it. This prevents the answer's wording from persuading you that a relationship exists when it was never established.

Check the boundary as well as the direction. An answer can have the right-looking arrow while dropping *fewer than five letters*, changing *should* to *does*, or treating “not first, last, or main” as if it guaranteed “should not capitalize.”

## A repeatable process

1. Translate each sentence into two complete ideas and identify the trigger.
2. Diagram the original rule and its contrapositive. Keep qualifiers attached to the condition they limit.
3. As soon as another rule repeats an idea, check whether the wording truly matches and, if so, join the chain.
4. Label any stated facts separately. Activate a rule only when a fact satisfies its trigger.
5. Before accepting an answer, compare its exact direction, scope, and wording with what the chain proves.
