Session recap · October 21

# Rules, Facts, and What Follows

Two useful tasks from our session: join rules into a longer chain, and apply a rule to a fact. A question can need both. Start by seeing what each sentence gives you.

Read with a purpose

## What kind of claim is this?

As you finish a sentence, name what it says. These six types give you something specific to notice.

Read the meaning, then compare the example.

| Type | Meaning | Example | Ask yourself |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fact | What is true in the situation. | The library is open. | What am I told is the case? |
| Rule | If one complete condition is true, another must be true. | If the library is open, its lights must be on. | What is guaranteed, and under what condition? |
| Causal | One thing produces or influences another. | The outage caused the closure. | What is said to affect what? |
| Correlation | Two things occur together or vary together. | Libraries with longer hours tend to have more visitors. | Is this an association, or a claim about cause? |
| Normative | What should happen, or is right, wrong, permitted or required. | The library should open later. | What is recommended or required? |
| Quantity | How many or how much: some, most, all, none, a count or percentage. | Some library visitors are students. | How much is claimed, and about whom? |

### See what each claim gives you

#### Fact

A fact gives you something to work with.

*Illustration: Given: reading room open.*

**Example**  The reading room is open right now.

That is given in this example. It does not by itself say a librarian is on duty; you would need a rule connecting those conditions.

#### Rule

A rule links complete conditions, whether or not they occur.

*Illustration: If the reading room is open, a librarian must be on duty.*

**Example**  If the reading room is open, a librarian must be on duty.

An open reading room without a librarian on duty is ruled out. A librarian could be on duty while the room is closed. The rule alone does not say the room is open.

#### Causal

A causal claim says what produces or influences something.

*Illustration: Claimed cause: renovation caused library closure.*

**Example**  The renovation caused the library to close.

This claims the renovation produced the closure. It does not say the renovation was the only contributing cause, or give a rule about every renovation.

#### Correlation

A correlation describes an association.

*Illustration: Branches with cafés are associated with longer visits, without a causal direction.*

**Example**  Branches with cafés tend to have longer visits.

The two characteristics go together. This claim alone does not show that cafés caused longer visits. Perhaps branches serving people who stay longer choose to add cafés.

#### Normative

A normative claim says what ought to happen or what is permitted or required.

*Illustration: Recommended: return books by Friday. Actual returns unknown.*

**Example**  Visitors should return books by Friday.

This recommends a return deadline. Whether visitors actually return their books by Friday is unknown. Do not turn the recommendation into a fact about compliance.

#### Quantity

A quantity claim tells you how much of a group is covered.

*Illustration: Some means at least one library book is available online, possibly all.*

**Example**  Some library books are available online.

At least one library book is available online. More could be available, possibly all. It does not identify any particular book as available online.

**One sentence can fit more than one type.**  “Some visitors should register” combines quantity and normative language. Keep the amount and the recommendation.

**Try:** Can one sentence fit more than one type?

Yes. “Some library visitors are students” gives a quantity and states a fact. “Every visitor should register” combines quantity and normative language. These labels help you read; they aren't sealed boxes.

In our conditional rules, **must**  marks a logical guarantee. In a claim about duties, “must register” can mean registration is required; it doesn't prove anyone complied. Read the whole claim.

Keep asking which claims support the conclusion. A correlation alone doesn't show that one thing caused the other. “Should” doesn't mean “does,” and “some visitors” doesn't mean this particular visitor.

**A fact can activate a rule**

The rule doesn't say the library is open. A separate fact can supply that condition. Keep the rule and the fact distinct.

**Watch but, yet and however.**  Check how the ideas on either side relate. The contrast can matter, but it doesn't guarantee where the answer will come from.

Two different tasks

## Link rules. Apply a rule.

### 1. Link two rules

The same complete idea joins the rules.

**Rule 1 · If** The library is open

→

**Then · must be true** The lights are on

**Rule 2 · If** The lights are on

→

**Then · must be true** The main circuit has power

The two rules share “the lights are on.” Follow each rule forward to get the combined rule.

**The new rule** If the library is open, the main circuit must have power.

The first rule gets you to “lights are on.” The second takes that same condition to “main circuit has power.”

**Try:** What else does this chain tell you?

Its contrapositive: if the main circuit has no power, the library must not be open. Neither rule tells you whether the library actually is open or the circuit actually has power.

### 2. Apply a rule to a fact

**Rule:**  If the library is open, the lights must be on.

**Supplied fact** The library is open

→

**What follows** The lights are on

The fact meets the rule's condition, so its requirement must be true. This time you get a fact, not just another rule.

> When I tried to bait you with the outcome, you said nothing follows, and you were right. Don't switch off that answer when someone pushes. Keep using it.

Change only the fact

## Four facts. Four separate cases.

**Same rule each time:**  If the library is open, the lights must be on.

Each case starts fresh. Decide what follows from that one fact before opening the explanation.

**Use the contrapositive forward:**  If the lights aren't on, the library must not be open.

**Try:** Case 1 · Matches the left side The library is open.

**The lights must be on.**  An open library with its lights off would break the rule.

**Try:** Case 2 · Denies the right side The lights aren't on.

**The library isn't open.**  If it were open, its lights would have to be on. This is the contrapositive.

**Try:** Case 3 · Matches the right side The lights are on.

**We don't know whether the library is open.**  It could be closed with the lights on for cleaners. That possibility fits the rule.

**Try:** Case 4 · Denies the left side The library isn't open.

**We don't know whether the lights are on.**  A closed library could have its lights off or leave them on for cleaners. The rule allows either.

The arrow gives a guarantee. It doesn't itself tell you what causes the lights to be on.

Use the same pieces

## Sufficient Assumption and Must Be True

Here's a simple way to see their different jobs.

Rule · Fact · Conclusion If the library is open, the lights must be on.
The library is open.
Therefore, the lights are on.

**Sufficient Assumption** Given “the library is open,” what added rule would guarantee “the lights are on”? Our rule supplies that connection. Or, if the rule is given and the fact is missing, adding “the library is open” would complete this argument.

**Must Be True** Given both the rule and the fact, what must follow? The lights are on.

In Sufficient Assumption, the added answer works with the evidence to guarantee the conclusion. In Must Be True, follow the evidence to a guaranteed answer. These examples show the distinction; other questions can use more pieces.

Keep the whole condition

## When the left side has an “and”

**If both are true** The trail is dry and The guide is certified

→

**Then · must be true** The hike goes ahead

### Which facts have you been given?

Supply a fact to use it. A fact not supplied is unknown, not false.

**No facts supplied.** 

Whether the trail is dry and whether the guide is certified are both unknown. The rule alone leaves whether the hike goes ahead unknown.

**Given: the trail is dry.** 

The guide's certification is unknown. Only one part of the combined condition is given, so whether the hike goes ahead is unknown.

**Given: the guide is certified.** 

Whether the trail is dry is unknown. Only one part of the combined condition is given, so whether the hike goes ahead is unknown.

**Given: the trail is dry and the guide is certified.** 

Both parts of the sufficient condition are met. The hike must go ahead.

Given both conditions, the hike must go ahead. Having only a certified guide doesn't meet the whole condition.

The contrapositive If the hike doesn't go ahead, the trail isn't dry **or**  the guide isn't certified, or both. At least one required part of the combined condition must be missing.

**Try:** The guide is certified and the hike goes ahead. Must the trail be dry?

**No.**  They could hike on a wet trail. The rule guarantees a hike when both conditions hold; it doesn't say those are the only circumstances in which a hike can happen.

## Explain your answer, then check

### What kind of claim is it?

Name the type and the words that support your answer.

**Try:** Hospitals with more nurses tend to have shorter waits.

**Correlation.**  “Tend to” describes an association between staffing and waits. It doesn't by itself say extra nurses caused the shorter waits.

**Try:** The outage caused the game to be cancelled.

**Causal.**  “Caused” claims the outage produced the cancellation. It needn't mean the outage was the only contributing cause.

**Try:** The city should run more night buses.

**Normative.**  “Should” recommends what the city ought to do. It doesn't say the city already does it.

**Try:** Some of the nurses work weekends.

**Quantity and fact.**  “Some” says at least one nurse works weekends. It doesn't tell you that most do, or whether a particular nurse does.

### What follows from the whole condition?

Rule: If the soup is salted and stirred, it must be ready. Treat each case separately.

**Try:** The soup is salted and stirred.

**It's ready.**  Both parts of the sufficient condition are met.

**Try:** The soup isn't ready.

**It isn't salted or isn't stirred, or both.**  If both were true, it would have to be ready.

**Try:** The soup is salted, and it's ready.

**We don't know whether it's stirred.**  Being ready doesn't prove both conditions were met. Salted, unstirred soup that's ready would still fit the rule.

**Try:** The soup isn't salted.

**We don't know whether it's ready.**  The rule guarantees readiness when both conditions hold. It leaves other cases open.

Carry it into the next question

## When you get stuck

1. **Sort the claims.**  Track the evidence and conclusion.
2. **Look for a connection.**  Can you link rules, apply a rule to a fact, or do both?
3. **Keep complete conditions.**  Match the left side or deny the right side. Don't reverse the arrow.
4. **Check “and.”**  One part plus the result doesn't prove the other part.
5. **Write one line**  explaining why the tempting answer fails.

# Two Jobs: Linking Rules and Applying Them to Facts

Every conditional question asks you to do one of two jobs. You either link rules together, or you apply a rule to a fact. That's the whole list.

## Rules and facts

A **rule** says what must happen **if** something is true. *If a library card is valid, the holder can borrow books.*

A **fact** says what actually happened. *Dana's card is valid.*

**Job 1: link rules.** When you see rules and no facts, look for an idea that shows up in two rules. Find the start of the chain and the end of the chain, then read what runs from one to the other.

**Job 2: apply a rule to a fact.** When you see a rule and a fact, put the fact against the rule. Does it match the left side? Then the right side must happen. Does it say the right side didn't happen? Then the left side didn't happen either. Anything else, and the rule tells you nothing.

Think like a lawyer. A client walks in with a situation. You open the rulebook and ask one question: how does this rule apply to what happened?

## Sort every sentence as you read

"Read actively" only helps if you know what to do. Here's what to do: put each sentence in a bucket as you read it.

| Bucket | Sounds like |
|---|---|
| Fact | The museum closed early on Friday. |
| Rule | If the museum closes early, the guards leave at five. |
| Causal | The storm caused the museum to close. |
| Correlation | Museums near the coast are more likely to close in winter. |
| Normative | The museum should post its hours online. |
| Quantity | Some of the guards work weekends. |

Buckets tell you where an argument will go wrong before you finish reading it. A correlation followed by a causal conclusion? That's the gap. A fact isn't filler, either. Facts are what set rules off.

Watch for **but**, **yet** and **however** too. In Must Be True questions, the right answer usually brings together what comes before the pivot and what comes after it.

## Sufficient Assumption and Must Be True use the same pieces

Three pieces: a rule, a fact, a conclusion.

- **Rule:** If someone burns down a fence, they've damaged public property.
- **Fact:** Mike burned down a fence.
- **Conclusion:** Mike damaged public property.

Hide the rule or the fact, and you have a **Sufficient Assumption** question: supply the missing piece of evidence. Hide the conclusion, and you have a **Must Be True** question: supply where the evidence lands.

## When the left side has an "and"

**If the trail is dry and the guide is certified, the hike goes ahead.**

Flip it for the contrapositive and the "and" becomes "or":

**If the hike doesn't go ahead, then the trail wasn't dry or the guide wasn't certified.** "Or" means one or the other, or both.

Here's the trap to watch for:

> The guide is certified, and the hike went ahead. So the trail must have been dry.

That doesn't follow. The rule only tells you what happens when **both** left-side ideas are true. One left-side idea plus the result never proves the other left-side idea. Maybe they hiked in the mud.

## Pop Quiz

1. Rule: *If the printer is out of paper, the warning light blinks.* Fact: *The warning light is blinking.* What must be true?
   **Answer: nothing follows.** If the printer were out of paper, the light would blink. A blinking light can't tell you why it's blinking.
2. Rule: *If the soup is salted and stirred, it's ready.* What's the contrapositive?
   **Answer: If the soup isn't ready, then it wasn't salted or it wasn't stirred, or both.**
3. Rule: *If a shop is open late and has two staff on, it takes delivery orders.* Fact: *The shop has two staff on, and it took delivery orders.* Was it open late?
   **Answer: you can't tell.** One left-side idea plus the result doesn't prove the other left-side idea.

## What changed

You named the real problem yourself: details slipping away during blind review. Then you named the fix: sort each sentence into its bucket while you read. That's exactly what active reading is. You gave your mind a job.

On the book tour question you chose the correct answer and explained why it matched: two ideas on the left, one of them given, the result given, and a conclusion that claims the other one. Worth carrying: **one side of an "and" rule plus the result never proves the other side.**
