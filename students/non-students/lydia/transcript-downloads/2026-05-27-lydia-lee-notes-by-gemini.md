* **Problem Passage Categories**: Joshua Fisseha introduces a Reading Comprehension framework where passages are categorized by structure to improve efficiency and understanding. The first category, "Problem Passages," focuses on ongoing issues rather than historical events ([00:01:13](#00:01:13)). Joshua Fisseha advises that after identifying a passage as a problem passage, the student should not look for deeper understanding of the category itself, but rather focus on the specific subtype to predict the main point ([00:02:13](#00:02:13)).

* **Subtypes of Problem Passages**: Joshua Fisseha outlines three distinct subtypes of problem passages. The first is "Author's Solution," where the author presents a problem and provides their own solution ([00:02:54](#00:02:54)) ([00:04:41](#00:04:41)). The second is "Someone Else's Solution" or "Viewpoint Solution," where the author introduces a problem and details a novel solution proposed by someone else ([00:03:50](#00:03:50)). The third subtype is "Exploring a Problem," where the author elaborates on the nature of a problem without proposing a solution ([00:04:41](#00:04:41)). Joshua Fisseha suggests using a consistent annotation style, such as writing "AS" for author's solution, to help recognize these patterns quickly ([00:05:36](#00:05:36)).

* **Clash Passages and Their Subtypes**: Joshua Fisseha introduces "Clash Passages" as a dominant and increasingly common passage type that focuses on tension, critique, and debate ([00:07:16](#00:07:16)). Within this framework, Joshua Fisseha identifies several subtypes, including when the author critiques a specific viewpoint, the author presents a viewpoint and follows up by labeling it as problematic, the author criticizes a viewpoint while offering their own position, and scenarios involving multiple viewpoints. Additionally, Joshua Fisseha notes passages that merely juxtapose two differing ideas without an active attack, focusing on presenting both sides ([00:08:12](#00:08:12)) ([00:11:47](#00:11:47)).

* **Identifying Main Points by Passage Type**: Joshua Fisseha and Lydia Lee discuss how identifying the passage type dictates the focus of the main point. For problem passages, if the author provides a solution, the main point centers on that solution ([00:14:12](#00:14:12)). If the author only explores a problem, the main point should be a summary of the problem itself ([00:15:20](#00:15:20)). For clash passages, if the author critiques a viewpoint, the main point focuses on that critique; if the author provides their own position, the main point should reflect the author's stance ([00:14:12](#00:14:12)) ([00:16:11](#00:16:11)).

* **Framework for Active Reading**: Joshua Fisseha emphasizes the importance of an active reading framework to avoid getting bogged down in details ([00:17:54](#00:17:54)). Joshua Fisseha introduces the "Why" framework, which involves constantly asking why the author wrote a specific sentence, paragraph, or the passage as a whole ([00:19:59](#00:19:59)). This approach encourages students to develop a hypothesis about the author's purpose and test it against new information, treating the passage like a science experiment that they can update or confirm as they read ([00:20:54](#00:20:54)).

* **The SEAR Method**: Joshua Fisseha introduces the SEAR method as a practical tool for operationalizing active reading. The method posits that authors are always doing one of four things: Comparison (comparing different ideas or positions), Explaining (explaining something like a theory), Evaluating (telling the merits or flaws of an idea), or Recommending (asserting a position or suggesting how things ought to be) ([00:22:44](#00:22:44)) ([00:34:04](#00:34:04)). Joshua Fisseha advises applying this method per paragraph to maintain engagement and structural awareness ([00:23:35](#00:23:35)) ([00:34:04](#00:34:04)).
