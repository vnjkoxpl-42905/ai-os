### Summary

Joshua Fisseha's Presentation and About That Book discussed strategies for solving LSAT questions, focusing on prioritizing intuition over abstract diagramming for conditional statements and using meticulous reading to internalize information rather than rereading, which wastes time. Key techniques included anticipating conclusions, identifying striking structural features like "or" in parallel questions for efficient elimination, and focusing on the necessary condition as the focal point of conditional arguments. They also established a systematic, multi-step template for About That Book to formalize their approach, emphasizing the identification of structural cues, testing the bounds of claims (like the contrapositive), and addressing the categorical error that confuses subsets with the entire set in parallel flaw questions.

* **Techniques for Internalizing Conditional Statements** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation advised pausing after reading the first conditional statement to understand what is known to be true, including the contrapositive, to force understanding rather than trying to remember text ([00:13:36](#00:13:36)). By knowing what conclusion must follow from the premise and the provided counter-example (e.g., if the sculpture does not evoke intense feelings, it cannot be a work of art), one can quickly identify when a statement is technically wrong or if the argument is presenting a hypothetical outcome ([00:16:43](#00:16:43)).

* **Strategy of Not Rereading** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation recommended adopting a strict rule of not rereading what has already been read to force diligence and meticulous reading the first time, ensuring the eyes only move from left to right ([00:18:18](#00:18:18)). * **Identifying Striking Features in Parallel Questions** For parallel questions, Joshua Fisseha's Presentation suggested looking for striking features in the argument, such as the use of conditionals, the type of statement, or specific structural elements like "or" in the conclusion ([00:20:29](#00:20:29)). Identifying such a feature, like the required "or" in the conclusion of the example, can serve as a "guiding light" for efficiently eliminating answer choices ([00:21:57](#00:21:57)) ([00:28:32](#00:28:32)).

* **Testing the Bounds of Claims** The third step of the template is to "test the bounds" of the claim by articulating what is known and what is not known after identifying the type of statement, such as determining the contrapositive of a conditional or the limits of a correlation ([00:53:39](#00:53:39)). About That Book accurately likened this process to creating possible worlds in logic games ([00:55:39](#00:55:39)).

* **Stalling and Solving Logic Games Up Front** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation emphasized the importance of stalling and not letting time pressure force one's hand to move forward when solving games. * **Identifying Conditional Statements and Cues** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation instructed About That Book to practice reading a statement, understanding it, and then looking for cues, particularly after each line ([00:59:58](#00:59:58)). They explained that the flaw is a categorical error where the argument treats historians (a subset) as the entire group (the set) of those who can distinguish the significant from the insignificant ([01:12:42](#01:12:42)).
