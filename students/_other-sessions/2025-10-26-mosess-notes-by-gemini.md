* **Understanding Argument Statements** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation reiterated last week's discussion on conclusions needing to be opinions explicitly supported by another statement, not just facts or unsupported opinions. They emphasized that arguments claim their conclusion is guaranteed by the evidence, not just that it "makes sense".

* **Universal Meaning in LSAT Arguments** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation highlighted that when the LSAT presents an argument, it implies a universal meaning without exception, unlike everyday conversation. This means statements are meant to be true in every circumstance, which significantly affects how arguments are interpreted and disproven.

* **Titan Terms and Argument Strength** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation introduced "titan terms" such as "must," "cannot," "not necessarily," and "could," which are crucial to an argument's strength. These words act as the "infrastructure" of an argument, indicating whether a statement is definitive or merely possible.

* **Disproving Conclusions** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation explained that the goal in evaluating arguments is not to prove the opposite of a conclusion but to show that the argument's conclusion is not guaranteed. They emphasized that casting "some shadow of a doubt" is sufficient to disprove a claim, particularly for definitive statements.

* **Definitive Statements: Must and Cannot** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation detailed that "must" implies 100% certainty, meaning even one exception invalidates the claim, and "cannot" implies 0% certainty, requiring immense support to establish. These terms act as "parents" in their requirement for absolute support, similar to a legal claim requiring a high level of certainty.

* **Possibility Statements: Could and Not Necessarily** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation described "could" as indicating possibility (1% to 100%), only requiring proof that something is possible to be valid. "Not necessarily" is presented as the inverse of "must" (0% to 99%), meaning anything less than 100% certainty is acceptable. They are considered "flexible" or "malleable" terms, indicating possibility rather than certainty.

* **Default to Certainty in English Language** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation clarified that in the English language, unless a statement explicitly indicates possibility with terms like "not necessarily" or "could," it defaults to certainty. This means an unqualified statement like "Mary and Jane plan to go to the park" is treated as a 100% certain claim in the context of an argument, which makes many LSAT conclusions seem "ridiculous" when understood in this extreme sense.

* **Analyzing a Weakening Question Example** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation and Mosess Bryant analyzed a passage about a lawyer's comment on Raymond Burr's portrayal of Perry Mason, identifying the author's conclusion that some legal professionals are losing their ability to distinguish fiction from reality. They discussed that the author's claim, though based on an opinion, defaults to certainty because no explicit indicators of possibility are present.

* **Identifying the Flaw in the Example Argument** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation and Mosess Bryant identified the flaw in the argument: the author misinterprets the prominent lawyer's statement as literal, rather than a figure of speech. The lawyer's comment, "we feel as if we lost one of our own," indicates an appreciation for authenticity, implying they know it's not real, which the author fails to recognize.

* **Conclusion on Argument Interpretation** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation concluded that understanding the language of certainty versus possibility is paramount for approaching arguments, rather than focusing on flaw questions directly at this stage. They noted that the arguments often contain "absurd" interpretations of statements, and recognizing this is key to successfully analyzing them.
