### Details

*Notes Length: Standard*

* **Establishing Criteria for Claims** Joshua Fisseha emphasized that any argument or conclusion, especially one stating something "should be the case," must be grounded in a principle or criteria. Using the example of comparing bacon types, or saying "Canada is better than the United States," Joshua Fisseha illustrated that without defining a standard or metric (like food), the claim is too open-ended and arbitrary to engage with ([00:01:37](#00:01:37)) ([00:04:13](#00:04:13)). Fiorella Meyer's Presentation noted that to support a claim, evidence like statistics would be needed, to which Joshua Fisseha countered that a clear criterion is required first ([00:02:27](#00:02:27)).

* **Criteria and Facts in Legal Arguments** Joshua Fisseha applied the concept of criteria to legal arguments, using the example of gambling laws ([00:06:04](#00:06:04)). He explained that a claim like "there should be no laws against gambling" requires a guiding principle, which in this case was the idea that "any law that's not effective should not be a law". Fiorella Meyer's Presentation agreed that the criteria was the law's effectiveness ([00:07:44](#00:07:44)), which implies that if a law is not enforceable, it is not effective, thereby establishing a necessary bounding rule for the argument ([00:08:37](#00:08:37)).

* **Identifying Redundant Statements** Joshua Fisseha and Fiorella Meyer's Presentation discussed how a statement that merely restates an existing premise does not advance an argument. Using the example of the gambling law discussion, Joshua Fisseha highlighted that simply repeating that "no legal prohibitions against gambling or enforcement" would not contribute to the argument because it is already known ([00:10:43](#00:10:43)). He stressed that the answer choice must guide the argument to the desired conclusion ([00:27:41](#00:27:41)).

* **The Standard for Success and Assumptions in Argumentation** Joshua Fisseha reiterated that every conclusion requires a standard, using the example of defining "successful" ([00:12:10](#00:12:10)). He explained that in assumption questions, the task is often to create a criteria or "rule of law" that serves as the guiding principle for the argument ([00:13:04](#00:13:04)). This standard is necessary to connect the specifics of a case to a broader principle for evaluation ([00:29:49](#00:29:49)).

* **Analyzing an Assumption Question on Ethics** Discussing a question regarding a self-help book publisher, Fiorella Meyer's Presentation summarized that the publisher knowingly made a false claim but the argument concludes that this should not be considered unethical ([00:17:19](#00:17:19)). Joshua Fisseha guided them to determine the "line in the sand" for whether the action was unethical, which Fiorella Meyer's Presentation identified as the fact that "everyone already knows that it's not true" ([00:24:57](#00:24:57)).

* **Connecting Evidence to Conclusion in Ethics Argument** Joshua Fisseha confirmed that the evidence supported the conclusion that the publisher's action was not unethical because the premise stated that "everyone knows that no book can deliver to the many" the limited success promised ([00:16:03](#00:16:03)) ([00:25:52](#00:25:52)). They reviewed an alternative answer choice (E), which Joshua Fisseha dismissed because it established a criteria for when a false claim *is* unethical, whereas the conclusion aimed to show that the action was *not* unethical ([00:26:42](#00:26:42)). Joshua Fisseha noted that the correct answer (A) must lead to the conclusion that the action should not be considered unethical ([00:27:41](#00:27:41)).

* **Plan for Future Session and Fallacies** Joshua Fisseha concluded the session by summarizing the need for a metric or principle with every claim ([00:28:39](#00:28:39)). He then informed Fiorella Meyer's Presentation that they would send them a list of logical fallacies, which they described as "easiest free points on the LSA," and emphasized the importance of knowing them "in and out" for tackling flaw questions, which are the most common question type ([00:30:41](#00:30:41)).
