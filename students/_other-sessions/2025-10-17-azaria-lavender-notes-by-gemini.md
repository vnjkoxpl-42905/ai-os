They explained that knowing these five components helps identify why questions are difficult, as the LSAC manipulates these variables to increase or decrease complexity ([00:03:13](#00:03:13)).

* **Argument Structure Components** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation identified six key components within an argument: premise, conclusion, intermediate conclusion/subconclusion, background information, opposing viewpoint, and concession. They stressed the importance of categorizing each statement's function within the argument ([00:03:58](#00:03:58)) ([00:08:39](#00:08:39)).

* **Defining an Argument** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation clarified that an argument, in its most basic form, consists of an opinion supported by at least one other statement that attempts to justify the claim. They emphasized that the core of an argument is the relationship between the conclusion (an opinion) and the evidence ([00:06:04](#00:06:04)).

* **Argument Core vs. Peripheral Information** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation introduced the concept of the "argument core" as the essential components of the argument, akin to the bones of a dinosaur fossil ([00:07:02](#00:07:02)). Peripheral information, such as background information, opposing viewpoints, and concessions, are considered "outside the argument core" and serve to distract from the actual argument ([00:07:44](#00:07:44)).

* **The Role of Conclusion** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation highlighted the conclusion as the "North Star" of every argument, containing all the information necessary to understand what the argument is trying to convey. They advised being "razor sharp" in understanding every nuance and idea within the conclusion ([00:09:33](#00:09:33)).

* **Background Information** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation described background information as facts that introduce relevant key points or context but should not distract from the argument's core. They noted that one can generalize background information for intuitive understanding ([00:10:18](#00:10:18)).

* **Opposing Viewpoints** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation defined an opposing viewpoint as another person's perspective within the argument, which the author will always attempt to deny or push back against ([00:11:08](#00:11:08)) ([00:13:39](#00:13:39)). They indicated that pivot words like "but," "yet," and "however" often follow opposing viewpoints, signaling the introduction of the author's argument ([00:12:54](#00:12:54)).

* **Concessions** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation explained that a concession is when one admits defeat or grants points to the opposing side ([00:13:39](#00:13:39)). They stated that a concession must have an opposing viewpoint to exist and will always be followed by the author's argument, often introduced by pivot words ([00:14:33](#00:14:33)). They clarified that following a concession, either a premise or a conclusion could be introduced ([00:16:09](#00:16:09)).

* **Distinguishing Facts and Opinions** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation stated that at a basic level, every statement falls into either a fact or an opinion ([00:11:08](#00:11:08)). They clarified that facts cannot be conclusions or intermediate conclusions ([00:24:43](#00:24:43)). An opinion, especially the author's, is likely to be the conclusion or a premise, but not background information ([00:25:30](#00:25:30)).

* **Evaluating Argument Validity** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation explained that the goal is to determine if the evidence guarantees the conclusion, meaning it is impossible for the conclusion to be false if the evidence is true ([00:19:29](#00:19:29)) ([00:21:23](#00:21:23)). They emphasized thinking of "what if" scenarios or objections to test if the conclusion could fail, even if the evidence is granted as true ([00:22:14](#00:22:14)).
