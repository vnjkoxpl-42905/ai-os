### Summary

Joshua Fisseha's presentation emphasized the importance of observing and analyzing one's mental processes while tackling LSAT questions, particularly flaw and necessary assumption questions, highlighting the need to identify causation versus correlation in studies. Joshua Fisseha and Om Desai collaboratively discussed strategies for identifying specific fallacies, applying causal frameworks, and distinguishing between flaw and weakening questions, alongside a detailed analysis of answer choices and underlying assumptions in arguments. Joshua Fisseha also advised Om Desai to develop a "live" LSAT preparation template and utilize "smart drilling" for diverse question types to refine strategies and improve analytical skills.

### Details

* **Approaching LSAT Questions** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation emphasized the importance of observing and analyzing one's mental processes while tackling LSAT questions, particularly flaw questions. Om Desai identified the initial steps as logging the stimulus, looking for the conclusion, and identifying the evidence ([00:00:00](#00:00:00)).

* **Analyzing Studies and Causation** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation highlighted that when a study is presented, one should anticipate discussions of statistics, evidence supporting a hypothesis, or potential causation. They noted that the word "hypothesis" in the context of the LSAT often refers to a potential cause, prompting the immediate consideration of causation ([00:01:58](#00:01:58)).

* **Identifying Causation and Correlation** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation advised adding "causation" to one's study guide as an expected element when a study is introduced ([00:03:08](#00:03:08)). They explained that phrases like "more likely" are common traps used to suggest causation when only a correlation is present ([00:05:50](#00:05:50)).

* **Rephrasing for Clarity** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation recommended pausing after significant thoughts and rephrasing them, as this practice helps identify misunderstandings and clarifies one's thought process during review ([00:03:59](#00:03:59)). This method aids in pinpointing where mistakes were made during comprehension.

* **Understanding Flaw Question Conclusions** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation explained that the conclusion in flaw questions often provides a causal explanation, interpreting a correlation as a direct cause. They noted that a causal explanation suggests an influence or contribution, such as age influencing a person's willingness to reveal information ([00:08:50](#00:08:50)).

* **Applying the Causal Framework** When encountering a causal explanation, Joshua Fisseha's Presentation suggested applying a causal framework: identify the correlation, recognize the conclusion's causation, and then consider potential alternatives like reversing cause and effect, introducing a third factor, or scenarios where the cause was present without the effect, or vice-versa ([00:09:52](#00:09:52)).

* **Distinguishing Flaw from Weakening Questions** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation clarified that flaw questions primarily require an internal critique or descriptive analysis of what happened in the argument, rather than proposing alternate explanations. In contrast, weakening questions often necessitate identifying or coming up with new factors that could undermine the argument ([00:10:48](#00:10:48)).

* **Characteristics of Flaw Question Answer Choices** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation outlined two key criteria for flaw question answer choices: they must be descriptively accurate in representing the argument, and they must point towards a genuine problem or issue within the argument ([00:12:45](#00:12:45)). Merely stating a fact about the argument without highlighting a flaw is insufficient ([00:13:45](#00:13:45)).

* **Analyzing Answer Choices and Fallacies** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation emphasized the importance of identifying the fallacy being referenced in an answer choice, even if it's incorrect, to ensure it aligns with the fallacy in the stimulus ([00:23:01](#00:23:01)). They further stressed that answer choices claiming an assumption must be accurately described and supported by the argument's content ([00:28:02](#00:28:02)).

* **Identifying Specific Fallacies** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation and Om Desai collaboratively identified "circular reasoning" as a fallacy where the conclusion is assumed true in the evidence ([00:23:01](#00:23:01)). They also discussed "part-to-whole" as a potential fallacy in answer choices, where what is true for a group is assumed true for all individuals within that group ([00:25:00](#00:25:00)).

* **Critiquing Assumptions in Flaw Questions** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation explained that "assumes without warrant" in an answer choice means the author believes a conclusion without providing evidence ([00:27:05](#00:27:05)). They highlighted that assumptions are problematic in conclusions because evidence is generally accepted as true in LSAT arguments, therefore, the issue is never with assuming the evidence is true ([00:28:02](#00:28:02)).

* **Importance of Detailed Answer Analysis** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation advised a granular level of analysis for both correct and incorrect answer choices to understand their nuances ([00:27:05](#00:27:05)). They noted that incorrect answer choices often have multiple problems, such as mischaracterizing the argument or referring to the wrong fallacy ([00:28:56](#00:28:56)).

* **Addressing Out-of-Scope Information** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation clarified that an answer choice does not need to discuss something explicitly mentioned by the author in the stimulus to be correct. The author's failure to consider a relevant factor can itself be the flaw being highlighted ([00:30:44](#00:30:44)).

* **Analyzing Causal Arguments in Practice** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation discussed a specific question involving age and willingness to share information, where the argument mistakenly assumes age is the direct cause of refusal based solely on correlation ([00:32:48](#00:32:48)). They posed a counter-question: if age were removed, would the outcome still be the same, to highlight the flaw in assuming causation ([00:34:26](#00:34:26)).

* **Developing a Live Template for LSAT Prep** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation advised Om Desai to create a "live" template or guide for LSAT preparation, which should be continuously modified based on encountered question types and personal susceptibility to common traps ([00:36:03](#00:36:03)). This approach aims to simulate actual test conditions and refine strategies.

* **Smart Drilling for Diverse Question Types** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation recommended using the "smart drilling" feature on LSAT Demon to encounter random question types, preparing for situations where the question type is not immediately known ([00:36:55](#00:36:55)). This practice helps identify overlapping issues across different question types and builds a comprehensive template.

* **Analyzing Two-Speaker Arguments** When dealing with arguments involving two speakers, Om Desai recognized the need to identify points of agreement and disagreement ([00:37:45](#00:37:45)). Joshua Fisseha's Presentation added that the first step is to outline the conclusion, premise, and assumption of the first argument, then evaluate the second speaker's statements against each component of the first argument ([00:40:03](#00:40:03)).

* **Identifying Disagreement in "Except" Questions** For "except" questions, Joshua Fisseha's Presentation advised replacing "except" with "the least" to identify the answer choice that least aligns with the stated principles, essentially highlighting where the speakers disagree ([00:45:37](#00:45:37)). This means the correct answer must violate at least one of the principles ([00:46:30](#00:46:30)).

* **Applying Principles to Scenarios** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation guided Om Desai to identify an answer choice that contradicts at least one of the established principles, such as forcing a productive individual to work in a team when they prefer working alone ([00:47:40](#00:47:40)). This approach successfully led to identifying the correct answer in the example ([00:53:23](#00:53:23)).

* **Analyzing Argument Structure** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation reinforced the importance of looking at the question stem first to understand the task. They also highlighted the recurring theme of research studies leading to causal beliefs, encouraging Om Desai to identify correlations and causal explanations within the arguments ([00:54:52](#00:54:52)).

* **Assumption in Causal Arguments** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation and Om Desai discussed the underlying assumption in an argument stating that increased visibility reduces criminal activity. They identified the assumption that street lights increase visibility, a point not explicitly stated but necessary for the argument's conclusion ([00:57:49](#00:57:49)).

* **Analyzing Necessary Assumption Questions** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation guided Om Desai on how to approach necessary assumption questions, emphasizing that the conclusion should support the answer choice, rather than the other way around ([01:00:33](#01:00:33)). They used the "negation test," where negating an assumption should destroy the argument's conclusion, demonstrating this with a specific answer choice ([01:02:38](#01:02:38)).

* **Improving Thought Process and Practice** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation highlighted the importance of analyzing one's thought process to improve mechanics, particularly for the LR section. They suggested Om Desai use a guide for necessary assumption questions and post improvements and comments in the classroom for instant feedback, allowing for a more polished and clear communication regarding specific areas of trouble ([01:04:58](#01:04:58)).
