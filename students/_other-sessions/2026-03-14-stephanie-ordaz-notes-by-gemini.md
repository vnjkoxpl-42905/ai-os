### Summary

LSAT fundamentals training focused on defining and structurally identifying argument components, with practice application regarding rebuttal conclusions.

**Defining Argument Components**  
The foundational LSAT definition of an argument was established as solely composed of a premise and a conclusion. A conclusion is the author’s specific claim or one specific statement, while premises are the evidence accepted as true and used for support.

**Identifying Conclusion Indicators**  
The acronym FABS (for, after all, because, and since) was introduced to identify premise indicator words, which also indirectly signal the conclusion preceding them. The 2-step process for identifying a conclusion requires that it is the author’s opinion and receives explicit support from another statement.

**Analyzing Rebuttal Arguments**  
Rebuttal style conclusions occur when the author presents an opposing viewpoint only to subsequently dismantle it, with the conclusion following the rebuttal. In a practice question, the conclusion was correctly identified as the assertion that the criticism against the mayor was unreasonable.

* **Definition of "Premise"**: The premise was simply defined as the evidence used to support the author's claim. Joshua Fisseha used a lawyer analogy, stating that the premise is the evidence used to fight a case, and the conclusion is the claim being fought for ([00:05:14](#00:05:14)).

* **Requirement for a Valid Argument**: An argument must contain both a premise and a conclusion, and without both, it cannot be considered an argument. Joshua Fisseha emphasized the importance of dissecting the argument's structure like an engineer, focusing on how the parts fit together to determine what function each statement serves ([00:06:26](#00:06:26)).

* **Argument Location within the Stimulus**: Joshua Fisseha clarified that the "stimulus"—the passage itself—contains the argument but is not entirely the argument. * **Analogy for Conclusion and Premise Relationship**: Joshua Fisseha used the analogy of a wedding cake, where the conclusion is the cake and the premises are the blocks or support. This example demonstrated that the conclusion (cake) needs to be adequately supported by the premises (blocks) to avoid collapsing ([00:09:28](#00:09:28)).

* **Non-debatable Nature of Premises**: A critical rule for the LSAT is that the truth of premises is never debated; they are accepted as true, or "granted" ([00:11:07](#00:11:07)) ([00:14:55](#00:14:55)). The focus should instead be on whether the premise guarantees the conclusion's truth ([00:15:53](#00:15:53)).

* **Challenging the Conclusion**: Unlike premises, the conclusion is the part of the argument that should be questioned and fought against ([00:17:34](#00:17:34)) ([00:25:00](#00:25:00)). Joshua Fisseha advised that students should always take the opposing side and act as the "devil's advocate" against the author's claim, regardless of the question type ([00:26:03](#00:26:03)).

* **Premise Indicator Words (FABS)**: Joshua Fisseha introduced the acronym FABS (for, after all, because, and since) to help identify premise indicator words ([00:20:56](#00:20:56)). These words signify that a premise statement will immediately follow them ([00:21:57](#00:21:57)).

* **Dual Function of Indicator Words**: These premise indicator words are also useful because they indirectly signal the conclusion. Whatever statement was presented immediately before an indicator word is typically the conclusion that the following premise supports ([00:23:06](#00:23:06)).

* **Two-Step Process for Identifying the Conclusion**: To correctly identify a conclusion, a two-step process must be followed. The statement must first be the author's opinion and not a statement of fact ([00:31:12](#00:31:12)). Second, the statement must be receiving explicit support from at least one other statement ([00:32:20](#00:32:20)).
