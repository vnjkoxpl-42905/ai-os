### Summary

Joshua Fisseha's Presentation explained that flaw questions categorize into three answer types, detailing two: a weakening type ("fails to consider/overlooks X") and an assumption type ("takes for granted X"). Joshua Fisseha's Presentation and Fiorella Meyer analyzed the frameworks for both types, where "fails to consider" answer choices must weaken the conclusion if X is true, and "takes for granted" answer choices must benefit the argument if X is true and demolish the conclusion if X is negated. The participants applied these frameworks to two separate arguments—one regarding meat in the diet, and another about prehistoric plant climate—to demonstrate the distinct evaluative steps for the assumption and weakening flaw types.

### Details

* **Flaw Question Answer Types** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation explained that flaw questions typically fall into three types of answer choices, which will require multiple sessions or dozens of exposures to fully understand. The first type is similar to weakening questions, often phrased as "the author fails to consider X" or "the author overlooks X".

* **Weakening Questions and Hypotheticals (Type 1\)** For answer choices similar to weakening questions, Joshua Fisseha's Presentation advised treating X (the thing the author fails to consider or overlooks) as true and assessing its impact on the conclusion ([00:01:22](#00:01:22)). If accepting X as true severely weakens the conclusion, it is a strong candidate for the correct answer. They emphasized that X should directly attack and weaken the conclusion ([00:02:41](#00:02:41)) ([00:10:59](#00:10:59)).

* **Assumption Questions and Definitions (Type 2\)** The second type of flaw question answer choice is similar to necessary assumption questions, which Joshua Fisseha's Presentation covered in a prior session to prepare for this type of flaw question. Fiorella Meyer defined an assumption as something that hasn't been proven or something the author is assuming in the stimulus that hasn't been brought up yet ([00:03:43](#00:03:43)). Joshua Fisseha's Presentation corrected this by defining assumptions as unstated premises, or statements the author takes to be true without explicitly stating them ([00:05:01](#00:05:01)).

* **Assumption Analogy** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation used an analogy of a store transaction where someone tries to buy a $20 pack of cigarettes but only pays $18 ([00:05:50](#00:05:50)). The missing $2 is analogous to the assumption in an argument, representing the unstated or missing piece required for the conclusion to be valid ([00:07:26](#00:07:26)). They explained that the conclusion often extends beyond the scope of the evidence, and the assumption is the difference that the author is implicitly relying on for the argument to seem complete ([00:08:11](#00:08:11)).

* **False Assumption Framework (Type 2\)** The second type of flaw question is called "false assumption," typically sounding like "takes for granted X is true or false" or "fails to establish X is true or false" ([00:08:51](#00:08:51)). Joshua Fisseha's Presentation outlined a two-step process for evaluating these: Step 1, ask if X, if true, would benefit the argument ([00:11:56](#00:11:56)). Step 2, use negation: if the statement (X) is negated, it must completely undermine the conclusion for the answer choice to be correct, similar to necessary assumption questions ([00:13:23](#00:13:23)) ([00:19:26](#00:19:26)).

* **Distinction between Weakening and Assumption Flaw Types** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation highlighted the difference between the two main types: "Fails to consider" (weakening) should directly weaken the conclusion if X is true, while "takes for granted" (assumption) should benefit the argument if X is true, but its negation must demolish the conclusion. They noted that flaw questions are tricky because they may present answer choices pointing toward either weakening or assumption, which operate almost oppositely ([00:13:23](#00:13:23)) ([00:21:26](#00:21:26)).

* **Addressing "Except" Questions** Fiorella Meyer raised concern about questions with "except" (e.g., "all of these weaken the argument except"), as their brain tends to do the opposite of what is asked ([00:23:09](#00:23:09)). Joshua Fisseha's Presentation advised replacing "except" with "the least" (e.g., "which one of these would weaken the argument the least") to create a better framework for analysis ([00:24:03](#00:24:03)). They also noted that most "except" answer choices are neutral, rather than the opposite of what is being sought (e.g., strengthening instead of weakening the least) ([00:24:38](#00:24:38)).

* **Analysis of "Meat in the Diet is Healthy" Argument** The participants analyzed an argument concluding that "Meat in the diet is healthy" because "most doctors do eat meat" and "who knows more about health than doctors do" ([00:25:21](#00:25:21)). The evidence must guarantee the conclusion for the argument to be valid ([00:29:16](#00:29:16)). Joshua Fisseha's Presentation confirmed the argument is flawed because even if doctors are knowledgeable and eat meat, the evidence does not guarantee that eating meat is healthy ([00:30:22](#00:30:22)) ([00:32:44](#00:32:44)). Fiorella Meyer identified the flaw as assuming that because doctors are doing something, it means it is healthy, even though they may know better but not act on that knowledge ([00:33:35](#00:33:35)).

* **Applying the False Assumption Framework** They tested answer choice E: "takes for granted that experts do not act counter to what according to their expertise is in their best interest" ([00:34:20](#00:34:20)). Applying Step 1, if this statement (X) were true (experts/doctors do not act against their own interests), it would dramatically benefit the argument ([00:35:48](#00:35:48)). Applying Step 2, negating the statement (doctors act counter to their best interest/expertise) would demolish the conclusion, confirming it as the correct answer based on the false assumption framework ([00:36:42](#00:36:42)).

* **Analysis of the Prehistoric Plant Climate Argument** The group analyzed a second argument that determines a prehistoric plant's growth climate from the size and shape of its fossilized leaves, and concludes that this also indicates the altitude at which the plant grew, since climate depends on altitude ([00:38:09](#00:38:09)) ([00:47:02](#00:47:02)). Joshua Fisseha's Presentation noted the argument is flawed because the evidence does not guarantee the conclusion; there is an implicit assumption that the temperature change is perfectly correlated and linear with altitude ([00:48:14](#00:48:14)).

* **Applying the Weakening Framework** The participants tested answer choice B: "overlooks the possibility that locations at different altitudes can have the same climate," which is an indicator of a weakening-style answer choice ([00:50:45](#00:50:45)) ([00:54:24](#00:54:24)). Applying the framework, if it were true that different altitudes could have the same climate (X), it would be harder to tell the altitude based on leaf size and shape. This directly weakens the conclusion, confirming it as the correct answer for an "overlooks the possibility" (weakening) flaw question ([00:55:22](#00:55:22)).

* **Conclusion and Next Steps** Joshua Fisseha's Presentation acknowledged that they introduced a lot of material and would send Fiorella Meyer a note summarizing the frameworks for the weakening and assumption answer types ([00:56:01](#00:56:01)). They explained that there is one more answer type to cover and that flaw questions are key to understanding the LSAT's Logical Reasoning section, as they are multi-dimensional and test comprehension of fallacies, necessary assumptions, and weakening concepts ([00:56:45](#00:56:45)). Fiorella Meyer will practice using the provided frameworks for future sessions ([00:56:01](#00:56:01)).
