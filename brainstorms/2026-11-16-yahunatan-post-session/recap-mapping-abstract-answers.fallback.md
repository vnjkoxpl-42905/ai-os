# Mapping Abstract Answers Back to the Argument

The LSAT writes many answer choices abstractly. Say the same thing in plain English and it's usually easy to see. The last two points do that: put each abstract word in plain English, then find each keyword in the argument.

## Put the abstract word in plain English

When an answer choice sounds abstract, swap the abstract word for a plain one. Plain English and keyword mapping both work on the answer choices only. They don't apply to the stimulus.

- **Explanation.** Means causation. What's explained is the effect. The explanation is the cause.
- **Illustration.** Means example. A specific instance of a general idea.
- **Presupposes, assumes, supposes.** Think assumption: something the author takes for granted and never says.
- **Justify.** Think evidence or premise: the reason given for the conclusion.
- **Principle.** A general rule that applies to many cases, not just one.

> **What you said:** “I always see them say justify, like all the time.”
>
> Then justify goes on your list. You'll see the same words again and again.

> **Build your own list of these words.** Write each abstract word with its plain meaning beside it. Add to it every time you meet a new one.

> **An assumption is never stated.** On an assumption question, an answer that only repeats something the argument already says can't be the assumption. An assumption is a belief the author never says.

## Then find each keyword in the argument

Keyword mapping means taking the key words in an answer choice and finding them in the argument. When an answer describes the argument, as on flaw, method and role questions, and you can't find one, that part of the answer doesn't describe this argument. On strengthen, weaken and assumption questions, a right answer can add something new. Check each answer for these:

1. **Nouns.** If the answer says “a certain property,” which property? Name it, then find it in the argument.
2. **Adjectives.** Does the adjective match what the argument actually says?
3. **What the author does.** If the answer says the author claims something, does the author really claim it?
4. **A form of logic.** If the answer mentions a conditional or a cause, the argument has to contain one. A correlation isn't a cause.
5. **Abstract words.** Put each in plain English before you look for the others, using your list.

Here's one in action.

> **Evidence:** A license is required to rent a car at this shop. Dev has a license.
> **Doesn't follow:** Dev can rent a car at this shop.

Say one answer choice is: “It takes a condition that's necessary for an outcome to be sufficient for it.”

1. **Plain English.** A requirement treated as if it were enough.
2. **Find the nouns.** The outcome is renting a car. The condition is having a license.
3. **Check the form of logic.** The argument is a conditional, so this answer is a contender.

Say another is: “It concludes that every member of a group has a trait because a few do.” There's no group and no sample here, so the nouns don't map. Not this one.

> **Naming the method narrows the choices.** That's why you name the method of reasoning first. If the argument is only about a cause, an answer about necessary and sufficient conditions has nothing to match.

## Strong words are harder for the argument to prove

Words to slow down on: most, all, every.

Most means more than half. All and every mean no exceptions. The more an answer claims, the more the argument has to prove. So slow down when you see these words. It's a reason to check carefully, not a reason to cut the answer.

- **Must Be True and Most Strongly Supported.** Lean toward weaker answers. The stronger the claim, the less likely the argument guarantees it.
- **Necessary assumption.** Lean weak. It's the least the argument needs.
- **Sufficient assumption.** Strong wording is fine. It has to be enough.
- **Flaw and Method answers.** The answer has to describe the argument accurately. If it says every society, the argument has to be about every society.

Weak wording still has to be relevant, and strong wording is sometimes required. Judge the idea, not only the word.

## Check yourself

**Say it out loud, then tap to check** What does each phrase say in plain English?

- The argument offers an explanation of the trend. **Cause.** An explanation is a cause. The trend is the effect.
- The author gives an illustration of the idea. **Example.** An illustration is a specific example of a general idea.
- The author presupposes that sales were steady. **Assumption.** It's something the author takes for granted and never says.
- The author cites the survey to justify the claim. **Evidence.** The survey is the reason given for the claim.
- The author appeals to a principle. **General rule.** It applies to many cases, not just this one.

**Does the answer describe this argument?** Find the answer's keywords in the passage.

*The café's sales rose after it changed its menu. So the new menu caused the rise.*

- It concludes that one thing caused another because the first came earlier. **Matches.** There's a cause in the passage. The menu change is the cause and the rise in sales is the effect.
- It applies a rule about a group to one member of that group. **Not this one.** There's no group and no rule in the passage to find.
- It treats something required as if it were enough. **Not this one.** Nothing in the passage is required. There's no conditional to find.
- It rejects an opposing view without answering its evidence. **Not this one.** No one disagrees with the author here, so there's no opposing view to find.

**How much does each answer claim?** Which one claims the least, and which claims the most?

- Most customers liked the new menu. **Stronger.** More than half have to have liked it.
- Every customer liked the new menu. **Strongest.** One customer who didn't like it makes it false.
- Some customers liked the new menu. **Weakest.** One customer is enough to prove it.

## Before you commit to an answer

*Stimulus on the left. Choices on the right.*

1. **Run the three stimulus steps first.** Structure, method of reasoning, assumption. They tell you what a good answer has to match.
2. **Find the abstract words.** Put each in plain English.
3. **Map the keywords back.** Nouns, adjectives, what the author does, the form of logic. On flaw, method and role questions, if you can't find one in the argument, that answer isn't describing it.
4. **Watch for strong words.** Most, all and every ask a lot. Check them carefully.
