# Facts, Rules, and What Follows

Every trained person has a badge. Start with that rule. Here are two different ways to use it.

**Fact means:** Something established as true about a person, an object, or a group. “Mira is trained.”

**Rule means:** What must be true if a condition is met. “Every trained person has a badge.”

### 1 | Apply a rule to a fact

**Fact:** Mira is trained.

**Rule:** Every trained person has a badge.

**Conclusion:** Mira has a badge.

**Why:** Mira meets the rule's starting condition: she is trained. The rule tells us she has a badge.

**Result:** A conclusion about Mira.

### 2 | Link two rules

**Rule 1:** Every trained person has a badge.

**Rule 2:** Every badge holder has building access.

**Conclusion:** Every trained person has building access.

**Why:** The first rule gets us from trained to badge. The second gets us from badge to access. The shared condition, has a badge, connects them.

**Result:** A new rule. It does not establish that anyone is trained.

Treat the statements given in an inference question as true. Use them to prove the conclusion. Do not add an unstated fact or connection.

## 01 | Facts state what is true

A fact supplied by the question is your starting point. A new fact needs proof from what you were given.

“Mira is trained” establishes training for Mira. It does not establish anyone else's training, a badge for Mira, or a relationship between training and badges.

A fact can be negative: **“Niko does not have a badge.”** It can describe a group: **“Some staff members are trained.”** That last statement establishes at least one trained staff member, without establishing that every staff member is trained.

<!-- FACT-SCOPE -->

**Try it:** Some reports are missing pages. Does at least one report with missing pages exist? Must every report be missing pages?

**Worked answer:** At least one report is missing pages. The statement establishes that such a report exists, but does not establish that every report is missing pages. Other reports could be complete.

## 02 | Rules connect conditions

A conditional rule covers **every person or object that meets its starting condition**. It does not establish that anyone or anything meets that condition.

In **“Everyone who is trained has a badge,”** training is the starting condition. Having a badge is the result that must follow for that same person.

<!-- RULE-PARTS -->

Write the conditions as short phrases: **Is trained → Has a badge**. The arrow connects the condition we start with to the result that must follow. If a person is trained, that same person must have a badge.

Being trained is **sufficient** to establish that someone has a badge: it is enough to trigger this rule. Having a badge is **necessary** for being trained under this rule: a trained person cannot lack a badge.

That does not mean training is the only way to get a badge. It also does not establish that anyone is trained. The rule could hold even if no one met its starting condition.

## 03 | Apply a rule to the same person or object

**Definition:** Applying a rule means finding an established fact that satisfies the rule's starting condition, then concluding the required result for **that same person or object**.

### Apply the rule forward

**Given fact:** Mira is trained.

**Rule:** Everyone who is trained has a badge.

**Match:** “Mira is trained” satisfies the rule's condition, “is trained.”

**Therefore:** Mira has a badge.

**Why:** The rule covers every trained person, including Mira. We have proved a new fact about Mira. We have not made a claim about everyone who has a badge.

<!-- APPLY-SEQUENCE -->

**Try it:** Every file marked confidential requires restricted storage. This file is marked confidential. What follows? Does the rule alone establish that a confidential file exists?

**Worked answer:** This file requires restricted storage. The fact establishes that this particular file is confidential. The rule alone establishes a requirement for any confidential file, without establishing that any such file exists.

## 04 | Link rules to produce a new rule

**Definition:** Linking rules means matching the result of one rule to the starting condition of another, so that together they establish a **new conditional rule**.

### What two rules tell us on their own

**First rule:** Every late application requires special approval.

**Second rule:** Every application requiring special approval needs a supervisor's signature.

<!-- JOIN-RULES -->

**Match:** “Requires special approval” is the result of the first rule and the starting condition of the second. The complete condition matches, not just the word “approval.”

**Therefore:** Every late application needs a supervisor's signature.

**Why:** Consider any application that is late. The first rule requires approval; that satisfies the second rule's condition, which requires a signature. This proves the general connection **Submitted late → Needs a supervisor's signature**. It does not establish that any application was late.

## 05 | Add a fact to the chain

**Given fact:** This application was submitted late.

**Rules:** Submitted late → Requires special approval → Needs a supervisor's signature.

**Therefore:** This application requires special approval and needs a supervisor's signature.

The rules-only conclusion was a new **if-then rule**. The fact that this application was late lets us apply the chain to this application. If we have only the rules, we have not established that any application was late.

<!-- TWO-OUTPUTS -->



**Try it:** Every trained person has a badge. Every badge holder has access. No fact identifies a trained person. Can you conclude that Mira has access? What new rule can you conclude?

**Worked answer:** You cannot conclude that Mira has access. Nothing establishes that Mira meets the chain's starting condition. You can conclude that every trained person has access. That is a new rule, not a fact about Mira.

## 06 | Recognize connections the rules do not prove

### 1. Do not reverse the rule

**Given rule:** Every trained person has a badge. **Is trained → Has a badge**.

**Proposed conclusion:** Anyone with a badge is trained.

**Possible example:** An untrained visitor has a badge. The rule still holds: every trained person has a badge. But this badge holder is not trained.

**What follows:** A badge alone does not prove training. Knowing only that someone is not trained also leaves their badge status open. The rule makes no badge claim about untrained people.

### 2. A shared starting condition does not connect the results

Use only the rules given in this new example. Rules from earlier examples do not carry over.

**Given rules:** Every late application requires special approval. Every late application needs a supervisor's signature.

<!-- SHARED-TRIGGER -->

**Proposed conclusion:** Every application requiring special approval needs a supervisor's signature.

**Possible example:** An on-time application requires approval for another reason, without needing a signature. Neither rule is broken. They only tell us what late applications require.

**What follows:** If an application is late, both rules apply to it. It requires approval and needs a signature. Approval alone does not prove that it needs a signature.

<!-- POP-QUIZ-2 -->

### 3. A shared result does not connect the starting conditions

**Given rules:** Confidential files require restricted storage. Medical files require restricted storage.

<!-- SHARED-END -->

**Proposed conclusion:** Every confidential file is a medical file.

**Possible example:** A confidential financial report requires restricted storage but is not a medical file. Restricted storage can cover both kinds of files separately.

**What follows:** Neither type of file has been shown to belong to the other. Ending at the same condition does not create a link between their starting conditions.

### Check the whole condition before linking

Find the result of the first rule and the starting condition of the second. They must match, including any negation or qualifier. If they do not match, these rules do not form a chain as written. Keep them separate.

**Try it:** Every member of a review panel has training. Every member of that panel has a security badge. Must everyone with training have a security badge? What follows if Dana is a panel member?

**Worked answer:** Training alone does not guarantee a badge. A trained person could be outside the panel. Because Dana is a panel member, both rules apply to Dana: Dana has training and a security badge.

## 07 | Use the contrapositive when the result is absent

A different fact calls for a different move. Suppose **Niko does not have a badge**, and the same rule says **everyone who is trained has a badge**.

If Niko were trained, the rule would require Niko to have a badge. That would contradict the given fact. So **Niko is not trained**.

**Given fact:** Niko does not have a badge.

**Rule:** Is trained → Has a badge.

**Match:** The fact denies the required result. Restate the rule as **No badge → Not trained**.

**Therefore:** Niko is not trained.

**Why:** Training cannot be present while the rule's required result is absent. This is the **contrapositive**: reverse the arrow and negate both complete conditions.

The contrapositive states the same rule. It does not deny the original rule, and it is not the unsupported reversal **Has a badge → Trained**.

<!-- TWO-MOVES -->

### Four facts, one unchanged rule

The rule is **Is trained → Has a badge**. The rule stays the same. Change only what we know about the person.

#### Fact 1: The person is trained

**Match:** The original starting condition is met.

**What follows:** The person has a badge.

**Why:** Every trained person has a badge under this rule. Apply it to this person.

#### Fact 2: The person has no badge

**Match:** The required result is absent. This meets the contrapositive's starting condition.

**What follows:** The person is not trained.

**Why:** A trained person without a badge would contradict the rule.

#### Fact 3: The person has a badge

**Match:** We have only the original result. The rule does not say that everyone with a badge is trained.

**What follows:** Whether this person is trained remains unknown.

**Why:** An untrained visitor with a badge fits the rule. A trained person with a badge also fits it.

#### Fact 4: The person is not trained

**Match:** The original starting condition is absent. The rule only guarantees badges for trained people.

**What follows:** Whether this person has a badge remains unknown.

**Why:** An untrained person could have a badge or lack one. Both situations fit the rule.

**Trained, but no badge** contradicts the rule. The other three combinations fit.

Now explore those same moves with a different rule: **Every late application requires special approval.** First identify the supplied fact. Then see whether it triggers the original rule, triggers its contrapositive, or leaves the other condition open.

<!-- FACT-LAB -->

**Try it:** Every confidential file requires restricted storage. This file does not require restricted storage. What follows? What if all you know is that it does require restricted storage?

**Worked answer:** If it does not require restricted storage, it is not confidential. That is the contrapositive. If it does require restricted storage, its confidentiality remains unknown. A nonconfidential file could require restricted storage for another reason.

You do not have to write every contrapositive automatically. If the fact clearly meets the original starting condition, apply that rule. If the fact denies its result, or a connection is difficult to see, a contrapositive can make the supported move clear.


### Carry a negative fact through a chain

Return to the two application rules: **Submitted late → Requires special approval → Needs a supervisor's signature**.

Now add a different fact: **This application does not need a supervisor's signature.** If this application required special approval, the second rule would require a signature. So it does not require approval. If it were late, the first rule would require approval. So it was not late.

**Contrapositive chain:** Does not need signature → Does not require approval → Was not late.

**Therefore:** This application does not require special approval and was not late.

### A contrapositive can uncover the match

Suppose these two rules are given:

**Rule 1:** Is trained → Has a badge.

**Rule 2:** Not trained → Requires supervision.

“Trained” and “not trained” are opposite ideas in the same position: both are starting conditions. They do not look like a chain yet. Take the contrapositive of the first rule:

**Restated rule 1:** No badge → Not trained.

Now “not trained” is the result of that restatement and the starting condition of rule 2. We can link them:

**New chain:** No badge → Not trained → Requires supervision.

**New conditional:** Anyone without a badge requires supervision.

Again, that is a general rule. It does not prove that someone lacks a badge. If a separate fact says Niko lacks a badge, then we can apply the chain and conclude that Niko requires supervision.

<!-- HIDDEN-CHAIN -->

If the middle condition already matches, link the rules directly. You only need to restate a rule when doing so reveals a connection you could not use as written.

## 08 | Translate the complete claim

### “Only if” introduces the required condition

“A visitor may enter only if the visitor has a badge” means permission to enter requires a badge. Write **May enter → Has a badge**. Put the requirement after the arrow. A badge alone does not prove permission to enter.

“Only badge holders may enter” states that same requirement. Translate the complete sentence before shortening its conditions.

### Keep requirements separate from completed actions

“Requires special approval” and “has received special approval” are not the same condition. A requirement can remain unfulfilled. A rule ending in the first phrase cannot be linked to one starting in the second unless another statement connects them. Shared words are not enough; match the complete claim, including qualifiers and negation.

**Try it:** Every late application requires special approval. Every application that has received special approval has a supervisor's signature. Does a late application necessarily have a supervisor's signature? Explain the missing connection.

**Worked answer:** No. A late application requires approval, but it may not have received it. The second rule applies to applications that have received approval. Nothing says the late application's requirement has been fulfilled.

<!-- CHAIN-LAB -->



### Rules can connect different kinds of claims

A rule can tell us what someone **should** do or what they **may** do. That is different from telling us what they actually did.

Suppose **every damaged item should be inspected**. If this item is damaged, it should be inspected. We cannot conclude it has been inspected. But “has not been inspected” is not the same as “it is not true that it should be inspected.” An inspection could be overdue.

<!-- MODALITY -->

For **Damaged → Should be inspected**, the contrapositive starts with “it is not true that it should be inspected” and concludes “not damaged.” Negate the complete claim about what should happen. Whether the item has actually been inspected is a different claim.

Suppose **a visitor may enter the archive only if the visitor has a badge**. A badge is necessary for permission to enter. The rule is **May enter → Has a badge**. Without a badge, the visitor does not have permission to enter under this rule. Having a badge alone does not establish permission; another requirement might still apply.

The rule concerns permission. It does not prove that someone physically cannot enter without permission. Keep the same kind of claim throughout your reasoning: a requirement stays a requirement, advice stays advice, and permission stays permission.



### Negate the whole requirement

Some conditions contain more than one part. Suppose **a document may be released only if it has been reviewed AND authorized**. Permission requires both parts. If either part is missing, the combined requirement is not satisfied.

**Rule:** May be released → Reviewed AND authorized.

**Contrapositive:** Not reviewed OR not authorized → May not be released.

To negate “reviewed AND authorized,” you only need at least one failure. You do not need both parts to fail. “Not both” does not mean “neither.”

Now suppose **a visitor may enter only if the visitor has a badge OR an invitation**. This requirement allows either route, including both. To fail the entire requirement, the visitor must have neither.

**Rule:** May enter → Has badge OR has invitation.

**Contrapositive:** No badge AND no invitation → May not enter.

<!-- NEGATION -->

Negating a compound condition changes **AND to OR**, or **OR to AND**, while negating each part. For the original AND requirement, one missing part is enough. For the original OR requirement, both routes must be missing.

**Try it:** A document may be released only if it has been reviewed and authorized. It has been reviewed but not authorized. What follows? Does the contrapositive require us to conclude it was not reviewed?

**Worked answer:** The document may not be released. Missing authorization is enough to fail the combined requirement. We do not conclude that review is missing too; the fact says review occurred.

## 09 | Choose the move and test the claim

Ask what you have: a fact that can activate a rule, or two rules that can connect? The matching condition determines the move.

Keep the scope of the fact too. **Some applications were submitted late. Every late application requires special approval. Therefore, some applications require special approval.** We cannot change “some” to “all.” The rule covers every late application, but the fact establishes only at least one. Other applications could be on time and require no special approval.

| What you have | The move | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A fact meeting a rule's starting condition | Apply the rule forward | Does the whole fact match the whole condition? |
| A fact denying a rule's required result | Use the contrapositive | Did you reverse and negate both complete ideas? |
| Two rules with a matching middle condition | Link the rules | Does the first rule end with the exact condition the second starts with? |
| Only the required result, or only the absence of the starting condition | Leave the other side open | Could another explanation fit the rule? |

To disprove a proposed inference, keep every given statement true and make the proposed conclusion false. For example, an on-time application that requires approval fits **Submitted late → Requires approval**, but disproves **Requires approval → Submitted late**.

## 10 | Mix the moves

Use only the information given in each problem. For each problem, write what follows and name the move. If a proposed conclusion is not guaranteed, explain a situation that would make it false while keeping the given statements true.

**Practice 1:** Every certified instructor has a license. Every person who is not a certified instructor requires supervision. Quinn has no license. What follows? Show the connection between the rules.

**Worked answer:** Quinn is not a certified instructor and requires supervision. Contrapose the first rule: no license → not a certified instructor. That result meets the second rule's starting condition. The chain is no license → not certified → requires supervision. Quinn's fact activates it.

**Practice 2:** A visitor may enter a lab only if the visitor has a pass or an invitation. Sasha has no pass and no invitation. What follows? If Sasha had an invitation, would that alone establish permission to enter?

**Worked answer:** Sasha may not enter. Permission requires at least one of the two alternatives, and Sasha has neither. An invitation alone would not prove permission: the rule says what permission requires, not that meeting this requirement is sufficient.

**Practice 3:** Every flagged expense requires manager approval. Every expense that has received manager approval may be reimbursed. This expense is flagged. Must this expense be eligible for reimbursement?

**Worked answer:** No. This expense requires approval, but nothing establishes that it has received approval. It could still be waiting for the manager. The second rule's starting condition is not established, so the two phrases cannot serve as a matching middle condition.

**Practice 4:** Every archive worker knows the catalog. Every archive worker has a key. Sam knows the catalog. Must Sam have a key? What changes if you also learn that Sam is an archive worker?

**Worked answer:** Catalog knowledge alone does not prove Sam has a key. Sam could know the catalog without working in the archive and have no key. If Sam is an archive worker, both rules apply to Sam: Sam knows the catalog and has a key. Shared starting conditions support both results for a person who meets the starting condition, without connecting the results to each other.

## 11 | Take this into your assigned work

Do the **conditional-diagramming drills first**, then move to the **LSAT questions**. In the drills, write the complete conditions before shortening them. Check your arrow and its contrapositive. When two rules appear, look for an exact match before combining them.

In the LSAT questions, identify the rule or chain that proves each answer. Check that the answer preserves the complete conditions. **Requires approval** does not become **has received approval**. If you cannot prove an answer, try a counterexample: can the given statements be true while the answer is false?
