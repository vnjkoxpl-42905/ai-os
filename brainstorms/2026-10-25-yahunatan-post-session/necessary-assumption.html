Necessary Assumption

Use this while answering necessary assumption questions. You're looking for an unstated idea the author's reasoning requires.

Identify what the question asks

Words such as depends on, requires and assumes can ask for a necessary assumption. Read the whole question. A necessary assumption is required. A sufficient assumption, added to the evidence, is enough to prove the conclusion.

Separate the reasons from the conclusion

Accept the reasons as true. Ask whether they support the exact conclusion, including who it concerns, when it applies and how much it claims.

Suppose an author says: Lena won the race. So someone from the Riverside club won the race. We know who won. We haven't been told which club she belongs to. The author needs Lena to be a Riverside member.

Be wary of an answer that claims too much

Necessary assumption answers often use weak language such as some, may, might or at least one. They only have to supply what the reasoning requires. Every runner belongs to Riverside would be far more than the Lena argument needs. Lena could belong to Riverside while every other runner belongs to another club.

Strong wording is a warning, not an automatic rejection. Weak wording still has to address this argument. Read words such as most and primarily for what they mean: most is more than half; primary is main, not necessarily only.

A definite prediction can also be strong without an obvious word such as all. The train will arrive by six commits to an arrival time. The train will probably arrive by six leaves room for a later arrival. Keep that difference when comparing answers.

Negate an answer you're considering

Negate means treat it as not true. Keep the evidence unchanged and ask what happens to the author's reasoning.

Try Lena belongs to Riverside. Treat it as not true: Lena doesn't belong to Riverside. Now her win gives us no reason to conclude that a Riverside member won. That connection was required.

You're testing these reasons. The conclusion might happen to be true for another reason even when this argument fails to support it.

Use the Negation Station note if you need help forming the negation. Check it both ways: your answer and the original can't both be true, and they can't both be false.

An assumption can rule out a problem too

Suppose a shop moved its display to the front window, then sold more umbrellas. The author credits the new display. What else could explain the increase? Perhaps there were more rainy days.

An answer ruling out a relevant alternative can protect that reasoning. Don't choose an answer merely because it contains not. Explain what problem it rules out and why that problem matters here, then negate the answer to check whether the author needs it.

Required and sufficient are different tests

In the recap's club example, some hiking-club members live nearby was necessary. It didn't tell us whether the particular member who won lived nearby. All club members live nearby, together with the evidence that a club member won, would prove the conclusion. That stronger answer was sufficient, but more than the argument required.

Understand the argument first. Check answer strength. Then negate an answer you're still considering and see whether the reasons can support the conclusion without it.
