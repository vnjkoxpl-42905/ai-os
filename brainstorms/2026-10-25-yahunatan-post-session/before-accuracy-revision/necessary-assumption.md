# Necessary Assumption

|  |  |
|---|---|
| **How to use it** | Keep it open beside a necessary assumption set. |

## What you're looking for

A necessary assumption is something the author has to believe for the argument to work, but never says. Pull it out and the argument falls, the way a tower falls when you pull the wrong block in Jenga.

It doesn't have to prove the conclusion. It just has to be true for the conclusion to have a chance.

**Question stems:** *assumes*, *depends on*, *relies on*, *requires*, *presupposes*.

## The steps

**1 · Split the argument.**
Conclusion on one side, evidence on the other. Accept every word of the evidence. Accept nothing in the conclusion yet.

**2 · Grant nothing that isn't written.**
Read the evidence exactly. *Primary* means main, not only. *Most* means more than half, not all. A plain sentence with no qualifier is certain.

**3 · Find the gap.**
What does the conclusion talk about that the evidence never mentions? That jump is where the assumption lives. Say it in one sentence before you look at the answers.

**4 · Read the reasoning type.**

| The argument runs on | The right answer usually |
|---|---|
| Conditionals, a causal chain, or ideas that repeat and link up | Reads like a plain positive claim that fills the link |
| Causation, comparison, or anything else | Has a "not" in it, ruling out something that would wreck the argument |

**5 · Cut the strong answers.**
*Most*, *primarily*, *always*, *only*, or a flat certain claim. A necessary assumption is the bare minimum, so the stronger the answer, the less likely the argument needed it.

**6 · Run the two tests on what's left.**

| Test | Ask | Keep it if |
|---|---|---|
| **Helps** | If this were true, does the argument get stronger? | Yes |
| **Negation** | Negate it. Does the conclusion fall apart, or only wobble? | It falls apart |

## Two kinds of assumption

**Linking.** Connects an idea in the evidence to a new idea in the conclusion.
*Evidence: Maya finished the advanced course. Conclusion: Maya can teach the beginner class.*
Assumption: Maya can teach the beginner class. The argument needs this to be true about Maya. It doesn't need the stronger claim that everyone who finishes the advanced course can teach it.

**Defending.** Rules out something that would wreck the argument. These often have a "not."
*Evidence: The bakery switched to cheaper flour, and then sales dropped. Conclusion: the flour drove customers away.*
Assumption: the drop was not caused entirely by something else, like a new bakery next door.

## How to negate

A negation is the weakest claim that contradicts the original. Not the opposite.

| Original | Negation |
|---|---|
| All of the guests arrived. | Not all of the guests arrived. |
| Some of the guests arrived. | None of the guests arrived. |
| The shop is open on Monday. | The shop is not open on Monday. |
| If the café is busy, Sam works late. | The café is busy, and Sam doesn't work late. |
| At least one block was finished. | No block was finished. |

**Negation is not the contrapositive.** The contrapositive flips a rule and negates both sides, and it says the same thing as the rule. Negation never flips anything. It contradicts.

## Things that will fool you

**Common sense.** The argument will lean on something that sounds obvious. *No repairs this month, so they broke the promise.* Sounds fine. But nobody said how long the promise was supposed to take. If it isn't on the page, it isn't given.

**Time and scope.** Watch for a time limit, a group, or a place the conclusion quietly assumes. A plan with no deadline can't have failed by a deadline.

**Strong language in the right-looking answer.** An answer can name the exact gap and still be wrong because it says *always* or *only*.

**Mixing it up with Sufficient Assumption.** Sufficient Assumption asks what would prove the conclusion, so strong answers are fine there. Necessary Assumption asks what the argument can't do without, so they aren't.
