**Session Manual · October 20**

# Necessary Assumptions and Negation

An assumption isn't something you invent out of thin air. It's the piece the author needs between the evidence and the conclusion, and it's built from what's already on the page. When you miss one, it's almost always because you granted the author something he never wrote.

**Step zero**

## Split the argument in two

Conclusion on one side, evidence on the other. Then treat them completely differently.

- **The evidence** Accept every word as true. You don't fight it.
- **The conclusion** Accept nothing yet. The author has to earn it.

**Grant nothing that isn't written** Common sense will fill in the gaps for you, and that's exactly what the test is counting on. **Primary doesn't mean only.** Most doesn't mean all. If it isn't on the page, the author doesn't get it.

**Finding the gap**

## What the author never said

The town promised to repave Elm Street. Not one block has been finished this month. So the town has broken its promise.

No block finished this month → promise broken

Those aren't the same thing. To get from one to the other, the author has to believe something he never wrote down.

**The assumption** **If the town were keeping its promise, at least one block would be finished within a month.** Nobody said how long the promise was supposed to take. Maybe a block takes six weeks.

> You caught this on the tax question before I pointed it out: nobody said the plan had to be done in a year. That's the kind of detail that decides these questions. Keep looking for it.

**The two tools**

## Negation is not the contrapositive

Both use a "not," so they get mixed up. They do different jobs.

**Contrapositive**

The same rule said a different way. Flip the sides and put a "not" on both.

**Looks like**

- If you're in New York, you're in the US.
- If you're not in the US, you're not in New York.

**Use it** To read a rule from the other end. It says exactly what the original says.

**Negation**

The weakest claim that contradicts the original. Nothing gets flipped.

**Looks like**

- If the café is busy, Sam works late.
- The café is busy, and Sam doesn't work late.

**Use it** To test an answer. Negate it. If the argument falls apart, the argument needed it.

- **A contrapositive, if true** Says the same thing as the rule.
- **A negation, if true** Breaks the rule. The argument falls.

For Elm Street, the negation is: **the town is keeping its promise, and still no block was finished this month.** If that's true, the conclusion is dead. That's how you know the assumption was necessary.

> You put it in your own words: you don't flip it, you just put a "not" on it. That's the whole difference.

**Claim strength**

## Be scared of strong answers

A necessary assumption is the bare minimum the argument needs. It's the boring, conservative claim. The stronger an answer is, the less likely the argument needed it, and one exception is enough to sink these:

only · always · most · primarily · must

**A plain sentence is a certain sentence** *Alex will go to Tokyo next summer* sounds relaxed. On the LSAT it means Alex goes no matter what, the same as *Alex must go*. Only words like *likely* or *probably* leave room for it not to happen. **A flat statement is as strong as "must."**

Sufficient Assumption is the opposite. There, strong answers are often exactly right, because the answer, together with the evidence, has to prove the conclusion.

**A preview**

## What the right answer looks like

Read the reasoning before you read the answers. It tells you what shape to expect.

- **Conditionals, a causal chain, linked ideas** The right answer usually reads like a plain positive claim.
- **Causation, comparison, anything else** The right answer usually has a "not" in it.

We'll go deeper on this next session.

**Recognition drill**

## Negate it

### Say the negation out loud, then tap to check

The weakest claim that contradicts the original. Not the opposite.

- All of the guests arrived.
  **Answer:** Not all of the guests arrived

- Some of the guests arrived.
  **Answer:** None of the guests arrived

- The shop is open on Monday.
  **Answer:** The shop is not open on Monday

- If it rains, the game is off.
  **Answer:** It rains, and the game is still on

### Safe or too strong?

Argument: the bakery switched to cheaper flour, then sales dropped, so the flour drove customers away. Which answers could the argument need?

- The drop was not caused entirely by a new bakery next door.
  **Answer:** Safe. Negate it and the argument falls

- Cheaper flour always makes bread taste worse.
  **Answer:** Too strong

- The flour was the only reason sales dropped.
  **Answer:** Too strong

**The finalist**

## On every necessary assumption question

1. **Split it.** Accept the evidence, accept nothing in the conclusion.
2. **Find the gap.** What does the conclusion need that the evidence never said?
3. **Read the reasoning.** Expect a positive answer or a "not" answer.
4. **Cut the strong answers.** Only, always, most, primarily, or a flat certain claim.
5. **Negate what's left.** If the argument falls apart, that's your answer.

# Necessary Assumptions: Find the Gap, Then Negate It

A necessary assumption is something the author has to believe for the argument to work, but never says. You don't invent it out of thin air. You find it in the space between what the evidence says and what the conclusion claims.

## Split the argument in two

Put the conclusion on one side and the evidence on the other.

- **Evidence:** you accept all of it as true. Every word.
- **Conclusion:** you don't accept any of it yet. The author has to earn it.

Then grant the author nothing that isn't written down. If it's not on the page, you don't give it to him.

Here's an argument:

> The town promised to repave Elm Street. Not one block has been finished this month. So the town has broken its promise.

The evidence says no block was finished this month. The conclusion says the promise is broken. Those aren't the same thing. To get from one to the other, the author has to believe something he never said:

**If the town were keeping its promise, at least one block would be finished within a month.**

That's the assumption. Nobody said how long the promise was supposed to take. Maybe a block takes six weeks.

## Negation is not the contrapositive

These two get mixed up because both use "not." They do different things.

**The contrapositive flips the rule and negates both sides.** It says the same thing as the original.
*If you're in New York, you're in the US.* becomes *If you're not in the US, you're not in New York.*

**Negation doesn't flip anything.** You put a "not" on the whole idea, and you get the weakest claim that contradicts it.

To negate an if-then assumption, keep the first part and deny the result:

| Assumption | Negation |
|---|---|
| If the town were keeping its promise, a block would be finished within a month. | The town is keeping its promise, and still no block was finished within a month. |

If the negation is true, the argument falls apart. That's how you know the assumption was necessary.

## How strong is the claim?

**"Primary" doesn't mean "only."** The main tool for something is not the only tool for it. Same with "most": most is not all.

**A plain sentence is a certain sentence.** *Alex will go to Tokyo next summer* sounds relaxed. On the LSAT it means Alex goes no matter what, the same as *Alex must go.* Only words like *likely* or *probably* leave room for it not to happen.

Necessary assumptions are conservative. They're the bare minimum the argument needs. So be scared of strong answers: *most*, *primarily*, *always*, or a flat certain claim. The stronger an answer is, the less likely the argument needed it. (Sufficient Assumption questions are the opposite. Strong answers are often right there.)

## What the right answer tends to look like

Look at the reasoning before you look at the answers.

- **Conditionals, a causal chain, or ideas that repeat and link up:** the right answer usually reads like a plain positive claim.
- **Causation, comparison, or anything else:** the right answer usually has a "not" in it.

This is a preview. We'll go deeper on it next time.

## Pop Quiz

1. Negate: *If the café is busy, Sam works late.*
   **Answer: The café is busy, and Sam doesn't work late.** One case where the first part happens without the result is enough.
2. *Email is the primary way the office contacts clients.* Does the office ever call clients?
   **Answer: you can't tell.** Primary means main, not only.
3. Argument: *The bakery switched to cheaper flour, and then its sales dropped. So the cheaper flour drove customers away.* Which does the argument need? (a) *Cheaper flour always makes bread taste worse.* (b) *The drop in sales was not caused entirely by a new bakery opening next door.*
   **Answer: (b).** Negate it: the drop was caused entirely by the new bakery. Then the flour had nothing to do with it, and the argument falls apart. (a) is far stronger than the argument needs. Notice (b) has a "not" in it: this is a causal argument.
