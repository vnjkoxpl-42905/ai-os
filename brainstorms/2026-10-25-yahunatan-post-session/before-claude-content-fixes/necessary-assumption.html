Necessary Assumption

A necessary assumption is something the author's reasoning needs, even though the author has not said it. The answer must be required for that reasoning. It does not have to prove the conclusion by itself.

First, understand what the author is claiming

Separate the reasons from the conclusion. Accept the reasons as true, then ask what they leave unsaid.

The prize went to a member of the hiking club. So the prize went to someone who lives nearby.

Being a club member and living nearby are different things. We need information connecting them. Do not supply that information just because the conclusion sounds reasonable.

Prefer what is needed over what would prove too much

Necessary assumption answers often use some, may, might or at least one. These make smaller claims, which is useful when we are looking for only what the argument requires.

Answer Does the argument need this much?
Some hiking-club members live nearby. Yes. If none lived nearby, the prize could not have gone to a club member who lives nearby.
Most hiking-club members live nearby. No. Most means more than half. The winner could live nearby even if almost everyone else lived far away.
All hiking-club members live nearby. No. We do not need every member to live nearby for this winner to do so.

The first answer is necessary, but it does not prove that the winner lives nearby. Perhaps a different club member does. That is the distinction: required is not the same as enough to prove the conclusion.

Treat strong language as a warning. With all, always, most or primarily, ask whether a smaller claim would be enough for what the reasoning needs. Often the stronger answer adds something unnecessary.

Do not choose by one word alone. A weak answer can be about the wrong thing. A strong answer can be required when the argument genuinely needs that much. Preserve the actual meaning: primary means main, not necessarily only; most means more than half and allows all.

Then negate an answer you are considering

Negate means treat it as not true. Keep the author's reasons as given.

Our answer says some hiking-club members live nearby. If that is not true, none lives nearby. Now a prize given to a club member cannot be a prize given to someone nearby. The author's reasoning fails. That tells us the answer was necessary.

You are checking the reasons the author gave. You do not have to show that the conclusion could never be true for any other reason.

Original What makes it not true
All guests arrived. At least one guest did not arrive.
Some guests arrived. No guests arrived.
The shop is open on Monday. The shop is not open on Monday.
If the café is busy, Sam works late. The café is busy and Sam does not work late.

For all, one exception is enough. For some, there must be none. With the café sentence, a quiet café does not break the rule. The café has to be busy while Sam does not work late.

Keep negation separate from the contrapositive

For if the café is busy, Sam works late, the contrapositive is if Sam does not work late, the café is not busy. A busy café would require Sam to work late, so if he does not, it cannot be busy. That is the same rule another way.

The negation is the café is busy and Sam does not work late. That makes the original false. Use that kind of test when checking an assumption.

Ask what the answer contributes

Some answers connect the evidence to the conclusion, as the club example does. Others rule out an explanation that would undermine the author's reasoning. Work out what the answer means rather than selecting it because it contains not.

A sufficient assumption, added to the evidence, makes the conclusion follow. A necessary assumption only has to be required. Understand the argument, check how much each answer claims, then treat a remaining answer as not true and see what happens to the author's reasoning.
