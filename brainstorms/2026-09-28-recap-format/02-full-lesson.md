# Quantity Statements: Follow the Group Being Counted

“Some,” “most,” and “all” describe how much of one group has a trait. The key question is **which group is being counted?** In “most apples are green,” apples are the starting group. The sentence says nothing precise about the much larger group of all green things.

## The quantities at a glance

| Wording | Guaranteed amount of A that is B | Could all A be B? |
|---|---:|---|
| Some A are B | At least one | Yes |
| Most A are B | More than half | Yes |
| All A are B | Every one | Yes, by definition |

“Most” is **more than 50%**. It is not always exactly 51%. Three of five is most; fifty-one of one hundred is most. “Some” can describe the same group as “most” or “all,” but it does not prove either stronger claim.

For a starting group that exists, the guaranteed claims run in one direction:

```text
all A are B  →  most A are B  →  some A are B
```

The reverse is possible, but not guaranteed. “Some apples are green” leaves open whether all apples are green. It does not establish that they are.

## Switching groups changes the question

“Some apples are green” means at least one thing is both an apple and green. That same thing proves “some green things are apples.” A **some** relationship is reversible.

“Most apples are green” also proves “some green things are apples.” It does **not** prove “most green things are apples.” There may be millions of other green things. The denominator has changed from all apples to all green things.

Nor does “most apples are green” prove that any apples are not green. “Most” may include all. A sentence that says “most” gives you a lower bound, not an exception.

When an answer switches the order of the groups, stop and name the new starting group. Ask what the original sentence actually guarantees about it.

## Two majorities of the same group must overlap

Suppose most students in a class take history and most students in that **same class** take biology. Some students must take both. If the two groups were separate, each would contain more than half of the class, which cannot fit within one class.

```text
Most A are B.
Most A are C.
Therefore, some B are C.
```

The repeated A is essential. For example, “most history students take biology” plus “most chemistry students take physics” gives no required overlap between biology and physics. Each “most” counts a different group.

## Carry a group through a rule that covers it

Suppose some apples are green, and all green apples are edible. The green apples already identified are covered by the rule. Therefore, some apples are edible.

```text
Some A are B. + All A that are B are C. → Some A are C.
Most A are B. + All A that are B are C. → Most A are C.
```

The second statement must cover **every** B reached by the first. “Some apples are green” plus “most green things are edible” does not establish that any apples are edible. The green apples could all be among the green things that are not edible. Do not carry a group through a “most” claim as though it were an “all” rule.

## Use a shared overlap to compare total group sizes

Consider these two claims:

1. Most banjo players also play guitar.
2. Most guitar players do **not** play banjo.

Call the people who play both instruments the **overlap**. From the first claim, this overlap is more than half of all banjo players. From the second, it is less than half of all guitar players. So the guitar group must be larger than the banjo group.

For a concrete arrangement, imagine 10 banjo players and 20 guitar players, with 6 people in both groups. Six is more than half of 10 and less than half of 20. The numbers are an illustration; the conclusion follows from the two proportions, whatever the actual counts are.

This is different from saying “most guitar players play banjo.” That reversal is not licensed. Track the group named immediately after each “most,” then compare what each claim says about the **same overlap**.

## Translating the conditional language from the drills

“No Canadians are angry” can be translated as **Canadian → not angry**. The word “no” is the instruction to make that translation. Once the rule is written, do not keep “no Canadians” on one side and negate it again. The equivalent form is **angry → not Canadian**.

For “A guarantees B,” having A is enough to establish B: **A → B**. For “A requires B,” A cannot occur without B: **A → B**. The words emphasize different sides of the same relationship. When a sentence contains multiple indicator words, identify the actual claim about what guarantees what before drawing an arrow.

## A process for the next question

1. Underline each quantity or conditional indicator.
2. Name the starting group for each “some,” “most,” or “all.”
3. Write the minimum the statement guarantees. Keep possible stronger claims separate.
4. Look for a shared group or an all-rule that lets you connect the statements.
5. Test the proposed conclusion. Can the premises be true while the conclusion is false? If so, reject it.
