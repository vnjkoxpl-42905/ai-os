# Some, Most, and All: Learn the Move, Then Test It

Quantity statements tell you how much of a **starting group** has a trait. Each time you read “most,” ask: **most of which group?** That is the group you must keep fixed while you reason.

| Statement | Minimum guarantee | What remains possible |
|---|---|---|
| Some A are B | At least one A is B | Most or all A might be B |
| Most A are B | More than half of A are B | All A might be B |
| All A are B | Every A is B | B might contain things outside A |

If A exists, all A are B proves most A are B, and most A are B proves some A are B. A weaker statement does not prove a stronger one.

### Try it: “Most of five apples are green.” How many green apples must there be?

**Check:** At least three. “Most” means more than half, not a fixed count of 51%. All five could be green.

## Change direction carefully

If some apples are green, some green things are apples. The same apple is counted in both sentences.

If most apples are green, some green things are apples. You cannot say **most** green things are apples, because the group being counted has changed. There could be many other green things.

### Try it: “Most apples are green.” Must at least one apple be *not* green?

**Check:** No. All apples might be green. “Most” establishes a minimum, not an exception.

## Find overlap between two majorities

If most students in one class take history and most students in that **same class** take biology, some take both. Two separate groups each containing more than half the class cannot fit inside the class without overlapping.

```text
Most A are B.
Most A are C.
Therefore, some B are C.
```

### Try it: What word in that pattern makes the inference work?

**Check:** The repeated **A**. Both majorities are measured against the same starting group. If the starting groups differ, the overlap is not guaranteed.

## Carry a group through an all-rule

If some apples are green and all green apples are edible, some apples are edible. The identified green apples are inside the all-rule. If most apples are green, then most apples are edible under the same rule.

### Try it: Some apples are green. Most green things are edible. Must some apples be edible?

**Check:** No. The green apples could all be among the green things that are not edible. The second premise would need to cover **all** green apples to carry them forward.

## Compare totals using the same overlap

Most banjo players also play guitar, but most guitar players do not play banjo. Call the people who play both the overlap. That overlap is more than half of the banjo group but less than half of the guitar group. Therefore, there are more guitar players than banjo players.

### Try it: Does “most banjo players play guitar” alone prove “most guitar players play banjo”?

**Check:** No. The first percentage is measured against banjo players. The second would be measured against guitar players. You need more information to make that reversal.

## A reminder from the conditional drills

“No A are B” becomes **A → not B**. The word “no” has already been used in the translation. Do not negate “no A” again. “A guarantees B” means A is enough for B. “A requires B” means B is necessary for A. Both produce **A → B**.

### Try it: “No runners are seated.” What follows if someone is a runner?

**Check:** That person is not seated. Write **runner → not seated**. Do not keep “no runner” as the starting condition.
