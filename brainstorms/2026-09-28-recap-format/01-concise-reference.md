# Some, Most, and All: A Quick Reference

Quantity words tell you how much of a **starting group** has a trait. Keep that starting group fixed. If a sentence says “most apples are green,” the percentage is measured against apples, not against all green things.

| Statement | What you know | What you do not know |
|---|---|---|
| Some A are B | At least one A is B. | Whether a minority, a majority, or all A are B. |
| Most A are B | More than half of A are B. | Whether all A are B, or what share of B are A. |
| All A are B | Every A is B. | Whether anything outside A is also B. |

“Some” and “most” can each include all. If the starting group has members, **all → most → some**. The arrows do not run backward. “Most” means more than half, which is three of five or fifty-one of one hundred.

## What you can carry across

**Some reverses as some.** If some apples are green, some green things are apples. The same apples establish both statements.

**Most reverses only as some.** If most apples are green, some green things are apples. You cannot conclude that most green things are apples. You also cannot conclude that any apples are not green, because all of them might be green.

**Two majorities of the same group overlap.** If most students in a class take history and most students in that class take biology, some students take both. Two separate groups of more than half the class cannot fit without meeting.

**A universal rule can carry a group forward.** If some apples are green and all green apples are edible, some apples are edible. If most apples are green, then most apples are edible. A claim that *most* green things are edible is too weak to do this: the green apples could all be among the exceptions.

## Keep the two groups straight

Suppose most banjo players also play guitar, but most guitar players do not play banjo. The people who play both are more than half of the banjo group and less than half of the guitar group. The guitar group must be larger. This conclusion compares the shared overlap against two totals; it does not reverse either “most” statement.

## A conditional reminder

When you translate “No A are B” into an if–then rule, the word “no” has already done its job: **A → not B**. Do not keep “no A” as a separate category and negate it again. For “A guarantees B,” A is sufficient. For “A requires B,” B is necessary. Read the relationship when a sentence has multiple indicators rather than applying two translation rules to the same words.
