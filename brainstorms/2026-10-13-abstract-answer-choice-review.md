## Choice 1

Source exercise 1; walkthrough and meaning pending approval.

> It is used to illustrate the general principle that the argument presupposes

**Plain English:** The statement gives an example of a general rule that the argument requires.

**Grading requirements (draft):**

- The statement is an example.
- The example illustrates a general principle.
- The argument requires that principle.

1. Pull out “illustrate”, “general principle”, “presupposes”.
2. The statement gives an example of a general rule that the argument requires.
3. The statement must be an example, and the rule behind it must apply beyond that one case. John’s unhappiness despite his income is the source example.
4. Does the statement illustrate the rule? Does the argument require that rule? John’s situation illustrates why money alone does not bring happiness.
5. General means the principle covers multiple cases. Presupposes means required, not merely helpful.
6. The example illustrates the principle that money alone is not enough for happiness; that principle is needed for the argument that we must pursue intangible things.
7. Go back and forth between the choice and the argument. Check every requirement before accepting the choice.

**Polished source breakdown:**

<p>Whenever we are faced with an answer choice that looks super abstract, the first thing to do is to always <strong>extract what keywords we can</strong> to try to make sense of the whole thing. Three words stand out in this answer choice: “illustrate”, “general principle”, and “presuppose”.</p>

<p>Now certain words have specific meanings within the LSAT. All three of these words appear repeatedly in LR questions and we need to memorize them:</p>

<div class="definition"><strong>Presuppose:</strong> in daily usage, a presupposition can mean either an assumption or something that is necessary. On the LSAT presuppose simply means “require”. It is talking about a necessary condition.</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Illustrate:</strong> an illustration, as it appears in a LR question, is simply referring to an example/examples</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>General Principle:</strong>  a principle is a rule on what we should/shouldn’t do; and that it applies to multiple conditions and scenarios.</div>

<p>For example, “<em>John is always tired in the morning, he should go to bed earlier</em>” is not a principle, we are just talking about one person here. “<em>Those who are tired in the morning should strive to get to bed earlier</em>” is a principle. It’s no longer just about John anymore, it is a recommendation for a certain category of people.</p>

<p>So now there are three requirements that must be met if we were to select this hypothetical answer choice as the correct answer. First, the statement in question must be an example; second, behind this example is a principle on what we should or shouldn’t do; and lastly, this principle is required in order for the author to advance their argument.</p>

<p>Take a look at this example:</p>

<p class="example"><span class="underlined">Despite a high paying job, John was extremely stressed out and depressed</span>, so there are intangible things in life that we must pursue to find true happiness.</p>

<p>John’s situation is the illustration of a general principle; namely that having a lot of money isn’t enough to make you happy. Is this principle required in order for the conclusion to stand? Yes, the conclusion says factors other than money must be considered in order to be happy. The underlined statement satisfies all three requirements and if this was a Role question, this would be the correct answer.</p>
