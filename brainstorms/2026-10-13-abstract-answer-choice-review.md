### Module 4 draft: match every requirement to the argument

Purpose: move from describing what an answer needs to pointing to the actual parts of one full source argument. Unlike Module 3’s learner-created examples, this lesson supplies the argument and asks the learner to locate the relationship.

#### A description has to fit this argument

An answer choice can sound familiar and still describe the wrong relationship. Now we’ll use an argument that is already written. For every requirement in the choice, we need to point to the part of that argument that fulfills it.

Read this original exchange:

<p class="example"><strong>John:</strong> <span class="underlined">Celebrities have the biggest smiles in front of the camera</span>, so they must be the happiest people alive.</p>

<p class="example"><strong>David:</strong> That is not true. Many celebrities suffer from depression and substance abuse. Smiling in front of the camera doesn’t mean a thing, they could simply be putting on a show for the audience.</p>

The statement we are examining is John’s observation that celebrities have the biggest smiles in front of the camera. David is responding to John’s argument. Keep those two speakers separate as we read the choice.

Here is the original answer choice:

> It makes an observation that, according to the argument, is insufficient to justify the claim that the argument concludes is false

#### Find the claim the author rejects

The choice says that the argument concludes a claim is false. Which claim?

John says celebrities must be the happiest people alive. David opens with “That is not true,” then points to depression and substance abuse. So the rejected claim is John’s conclusion about their happiness. The statement under examination is not David’s objection. It is the observation John used to reach the claim David rejects.

#### Find the observation and check whether it is enough

John’s reason is the smiles he sees in front of the camera. David says smiling “doesn’t mean a thing” and suggests celebrities could be putting on a show. David is challenging what those smiles establish. They do not justify John’s conclusion, according to David’s argument.

We can now match the description completely:

| Requirement in the choice | Where it appears in the original argument |
|---|---|
| An observation is made | John: celebrities have the biggest smiles in front of the camera |
| The observation is used to justify a claim | John uses the smiles to conclude celebrities are the happiest people alive |
| The author treats the observation as insufficient | David says the smiles do not establish happiness and could be a performance |
| The argument concludes the supported claim is false | David rejects John’s claim and cites depression and substance abuse |

**False describes the claim David rejects. It does not describe the observation about the smiles.** That difference matters. If we moved “false” onto the observation, we would be reading a different choice.

Before accepting a description, say which statement it refers to and point to every relationship it requires. A passage containing all the right topics is not enough. The right statements must be doing the right jobs.

Practice instruction draft: reread the original exchange and choice. Identify the observation, the claim it supports, and David’s response in separate written lines. Compare each line with the matching table. This is a source comparison, not an invented multiple-choice question.

### Module 5 draft: see the actual move, not just the topic

Purpose: identify how the author uses a reason to challenge a conclusion. This adds an actual worked method question, rather than a methods-label list.

#### Follow the rule to the case the economist chooses

The argument below is about taxation. That is its subject. The question asks how the economist responds to the politician, so we need to follow what the economist does with the politician’s reasoning.

**Original question PT103-S1-Q18**

Politician: A government that taxes incomes at a rate of 100 percent will generate no revenue because all economic activity will cease. So it follows that the lower the rate of income tax, the more revenue the government will generate by that tax.

Economist: Your conclusion cannot be correct, since it would mean that an income tax of 0 percent would generate the maximum revenue.

**Original question stem:** Which one of the following argumentative strategies is used by the economist in responding to the politician?

**A.** stating a general principle that is incompatible with the conclusion the politician derives

**B.** providing evidence that where the politician’s advice has been adopted, the results have been disappointing

**C.** arguing that the principle derived by the politician, if applied in the limiting case, leads to an absurdly false conclusion

**D.** undermining the credibility of the politician by openly questioning the politician’s understanding of economics

**E.** attacking the politician’s argument by giving reason to doubt the truth of a premise



Start with the politician’s conclusion: the lower the income-tax rate, the more revenue the tax generates. The politician takes the result at a 100-percent rate and draws a broad relationship between lower rates and higher revenue.

The economist does not cite a country where a tax cut failed. She does not dispute that economic activity would cease at the 100-percent rate. She takes the politician’s conclusion to the other end of the range: a rate of zero percent.

If lowering the rate always raises revenue, a zero-percent rate would produce the maximum. But a tax charged at zero percent collects no income-tax revenue. The economist uses that consequence to challenge the politician’s conclusion.

**That is the reasoning move: apply the politician’s rule at its lower limit and show that the resulting conclusion cannot be right.**

#### Connect that move to the answer’s language

Choice C calls the politician’s conclusion a **principle**. Here it is the general relationship the politician claims: lower rate, more revenue. **If applied in the limiting case** refers to applying that relationship at the zero-percent rate. **Leads to an absurdly false conclusion** refers to the claim that a zero-percent income tax would generate maximum revenue.

Each part names something we can point to. The answer is C in the captured source key.

Notice how other choices change the action. B describes real results from places that adopted the advice; no such evidence is given. D describes an open attack on the politician’s understanding of economics; the economist instead points to a consequence of the politician’s conclusion. E says a premise is challenged; the response challenges the conclusion drawn from the premise.

Before selecting a methods label, explain the actual move in ordinary words. Here, “the economist uses a zero-percent tax to show what goes wrong with the politician’s rule” connects the reason to the response. “They disagree about taxes” does not.

Practice draft: identify the politician’s rule, the case the economist applies it to, and the result that makes the rule untenable. Then match those three parts to C. This is a worked source question; no new seventeen-category bank is added.

### Module 6 draft: a possible explanation is not a proven explanation

Purpose: diagnose the precise word or relationship that makes a close description go beyond the argument.

#### Keep the author’s degree of commitment

A description can correctly identify the topic and still say the author did more than the passage shows. Read this full original question. Pay attention to **unwarranted**, **might instead**, and **might then** in the stimulus.

**Original question PT139-S1-Q23**

Anthropologist: Every human culture has taboos against eating certain animals. Some researchers have argued that such taboos originated solely for practical reasons, pointing out, for example, that in many cultures it is taboo to eat domestic animals that provide labor and that are therefore worth more alive than dead. But that conclusion is unwarranted; taboos against eating certain animals might instead have arisen for symbolic, ritualistic reasons, and the presence of the taboos might then have led people to find other uses for those animals.

**Original question stem:** In the argument, the anthropologist

**A.** calls an explanation of a phenomenon into question by pointing out that observations cited as evidence supporting it are also compatible with an alternative explanation of the phenomenon

**B.** establishes that an explanation of a phenomenon is false by demonstrating that the evidence that had been cited in support of that explanation was inadequate

**C.** rejects the reasoning used to justify a hypothesis about the origins of a phenomenon, on the grounds that there exists another, more plausible hypothesis about the origins of that phenomenon

**D.** argues in support of one explanation of a phenomenon by citing evidence incompatible with a rival explanation

**E.** describes a hypothesis about the sequence of events involved in the origins of a phenomenon, and then argues that those events occurred in a different sequence



The researchers conclude that the taboos originated solely for practical reasons. They point to useful domestic animals that are worth more alive than dead.

The anthropologist says that conclusion is unwarranted. Why? There is another possible explanation: symbolic or ritualistic taboos could have come first, and people could then have found practical uses for the protected animals.

The observation about useful animals fits that alternative too. So the anthropologist challenges whether the cited observation settles the origin of the taboo. The argument does not establish which origin actually occurred.

#### Check the correct description completely

Choice A says the author **calls an explanation into question**. That fits “that conclusion is unwarranted.” It then says the observations cited as evidence are **also compatible with an alternative explanation**. That fits the possibility that taboos came first and practical uses were found afterward.

The source key is A. Both parts match. The alternative can account for the observation without contradicting the fact that those animals have practical value.

#### Find the exact point where each nearby choice changes the argument

B says the author **establishes ... is false**. The author challenges whether the researchers have established their explanation; she does not establish that it is false. “Unwarranted” does not do the same work as “false.”

C calls the alternative **more plausible**. The author raises it as a possibility. She does not compare the explanations and establish that hers is more plausible.

D describes evidence **incompatible with a rival explanation**. The point is that the same observation can fit another explanation. Compatibility is the reason the original explanation has not been settled.

E says the author **argues that those events occurred in a different sequence**. The source presents another sequence with “might.” It does not conclude that sequence actually occurred.

This is how we diagnose a close answer. Quote the word that adds strength or changes the relationship, then compare it with what the author actually commits to. Do not replace a careful comparison with “it sounds too strong.” Explain what is too strong and why.

Practice draft: for B through E, identify the exact phrase that does not fit and point to the stimulus wording that settles the mismatch. Then explain how both parts of A fit. All five choices are original source text, not manufactured distractors.

### Module 7 draft: put the whole procedure to work on a fresh question

Purpose: combine structure, decoding, full requirement mapping, and precise rejection of alternatives. This full question is outside Module 3’s fifteen choices and outside the worked examples in Modules 4–6.

#### Make your own account before opening the explanation

Read the original argument and all five choices. Identify the columnist’s conclusion and the reason for it. Explain the author’s move in ordinary language. Then choose a description and show where each part of it appears in the argument.

**Original question PT118-S4-Q12**

Columnist: If you received an unsigned letter, you would likely have some doubts about the truth of its contents. But news stories often include statements from anonymous sources, and these are usually quoted with the utmost respect. It makes sense to be skeptical of these sources, for, as in the case of the writer of an unsigned letter, their anonymity makes it possible for them to plant inaccurate or slanted statements without ever having to answer for them.

**Original question stem:** The columnist's argument proceeds by

**A.** pointing out that a certain attitude would presumably be adopted in one situation, in order to support the claim that a similar attitude would be justified in an analogous situation

**B.** drawing an analogy between an attitude commonly adopted in one situation and a different attitude commonly adopted in another situation, and establishing that the latter attitude is better justified than the former

**C.** inferring that an attitude would be justified in all situations of a given type on the grounds that this attitude is justified in a hypothetical situation of that type

**D.** calling into question a certain type of evidence by drawing an analogy between that evidence and other evidence that the argument shows is usually false

**E.** calling into question the motives of those presenting certain information, and concluding for this reason that the information is likely to be false



#### First, follow the argument

The conclusion is that it makes sense to be skeptical of anonymous sources in news stories. The columnist compares those sources with the writer of an unsigned letter.

What connects the cases? In both, anonymity allows inaccurate or slanted statements without the speaker or writer having to answer for them. The columnist uses the doubt we would likely have about an unsigned letter to support skepticism toward anonymous news sources.

That gives us an account of the move before we rely on a label: **use our likely response to one anonymous source to justify a similar response to another, where the same anonymity problem applies**.

#### Decode the full answer and map every part

The captured source key is A. Break its description into requirements:

| Part of A | Match in the original argument |
|---|---|
| A certain attitude would presumably be adopted | You would likely have doubts about the truth of an unsigned letter |
| In one situation | Receiving an unsigned letter |
| To support the claim that a similar attitude would be justified | The columnist concludes skepticism makes sense |
| In an analogous situation | Reading news stories that include anonymous sources |

The final sentence explains why the situations are compared: in each, anonymity allows inaccurate or slanted claims without accountability. The analogy is doing support work, rather than merely mentioning two different kinds of writing.

#### Check the alternatives without changing the conclusion

B says the **latter attitude is better justified**. That would defend the respect given to anonymous sources over the doubt directed toward unsigned letters. The conclusion instead supports skepticism toward anonymous sources.

C describes an inference about **all situations of a given type** from a hypothetical situation **of that type**. That is not the reasoning the columnist gives. The columnist uses the likely doubt about an unsigned letter to support a similar cautious attitude toward anonymous news sources, explaining the feature the cases share. The passage does not establish a universal rule for every situation in a defined category merely from one hypothetical case. Check the whole description of the inference: A captures the comparison and the justified similar attitude. Do not reject C just because the examples have different names; analogous cases can share a broader category. The mismatch is C’s description of a same-type general inference to all situations, rather than the analogy the author actually uses.

D says the analogous evidence is **usually false**. The source says we would likely have doubts about an unsigned letter. Doubts about truth do not establish that such letters are usually false.

E says the conclusion is that the information is **likely to be false**. The columnist concludes that skepticism makes sense. The possibility of inaccurate or slanted statements supports that cautious attitude; it does not establish the stronger conclusion E states.

#### Explain your choice fully

If your answer matched A, check whether you matched every part or only recognized “analogous.” If you chose another answer, identify the exact phrase that changed the attitude, the situation, or the strength of the conclusion. Then revise your written account of the argument.

You have now used the whole procedure on a fresh full source question: understand the structure, decode the description, list its requirements, match them to the argument, and check each qualification. The same procedure gives you a reason to accept the answer and a precise reason to reject a close alternative.

## Draft opening and guided source example

### An answer choice describes a relationship

An abstract answer choice tells us what a statement does in an argument. To understand it, we need more than the name of a role. We need to see the relationship the choice describes.

A premise is a reason for accepting a conclusion. An example can illustrate that premise. Those are related jobs, but they are different jobs. The answer choice can ask us to follow both.

Consider this choice:

> It is an illustration of a premise that is used to support the argument’s conclusion

**Illustration** means an example. **Premise** tells us that the idea being illustrated is a reason the author uses. **Support the argument’s conclusion** tells us what that reason is used for.

So there are two relationships to check: the statement must be an example of the premise, and the premise must support the conclusion. Calling the statement “an example” gets us started. It does not finish the work.

### Follow the example

The underlined statement is the one the choice describes.

<p class="example"><span class="underlined">John has been smoking two packs a day since he was sixteen, last week he was diagnosed with lung cancer.</span> Smoking is a direct cause of lung cancer. We must avoid cigarettes at all costs if we want to stay healthy.</p>

First, find the point the author wants us to accept. Here, the author says we must avoid cigarettes if we want to stay healthy.

What reason does the author give? **Smoking is a direct cause of lung cancer.** That is the premise used to support the recommendation.

Now look at the underlined statement. John smoked two packs a day and was diagnosed with lung cancer. John’s case is an example of the causal relationship stated in the premise.

We can now follow the whole description. **John’s case illustrates the premise. The premise supports the conclusion.** The choice describes what the underlined statement does, including the connection to the reason and the reason’s connection to the final point.

If we only noticed that the paragraph discusses smoking, we would miss the structure. If we only noticed that John’s case is an example, we would leave part of the choice unchecked. We need to know what it is an example of and how that idea is used in the argument.

This explains the statement’s role. It does not ask us to decide whether the argument has proved its causal claim. The task here is to understand the description and identify the relationship the source is showing.

### Make each choice concrete

For each of the fifteen choices, there are two pieces of written work you can use to test your understanding.

**State the requirements.** What would have to appear in an argument for the choice to describe it correctly? Keep every part of the description. A claim receiving support is different from a claim receiving no justification. A claim being required is different from a claim establishing the conclusion.

**Create an example argument.** Write reasons and a conclusion, then identify the particular statement the choice would describe. Check where each requirement appears in your example. If one part is missing, you know what still needs work.

The original breakdown is available whenever you need it. You can try the written work first, open the explanation to learn the relationship, or return to revise your example after reading. Reading the breakdown and completing the written work are recorded separately.

As you continue, keep comparing the wording of the choice with what the statement actually does. The explanation should make the language more concrete. Your example gives you a chance to use that meaning yourself.

### Check the whole description

The fifteen choices use different words, but the task stays the same: turn the description into requirements you can check.

Start with the idea the choice names. Is it an example, a principle, an observation, a hypothesis, or a conclusion? Then look at what happens to that idea. Is it used as support, challenged, denied, or offered as an explanation?

Keep the strength of the wording. **Required** and **establishes** do different work. **Attempts** does not tell us that the attempt succeeds. **Explicitly stated** asks for something the argument actually says. Leaving out one of these details changes the description you are trying to satisfy.

- **insufficient**: Not enough to establish the claim.

- **false**: Untrue.
- **phenomenon**: The event, effect, or situation being explained.
- **validity**: Whether the principle is true or acceptable in this choice.
- **questions**: Challenges or calls into doubt.
- **denies**: Says a claim is not true.
- **assumed**: Taken for granted without being established.

## Choice 1

Source exercise 1; walkthrough and meaning pending approval.

> It is used to illustrate the general principle that the argument presupposes

**Plain English:** The statement gives an example of a general rule that the argument requires.

**Grading requirements (draft):**

- The statement is an example.
- The example illustrates a general principle.
- The argument requires that principle.

1. Pull out “illustrate”, “general principle”, “presupposes”.
2. The statement gives an example of a general rule that the argument requires.
3. The statement must be an example, and the rule behind it must apply beyond that one case. John’s unhappiness despite his income is the source example.
4. Does the statement illustrate the rule? Does the argument require that rule? John’s situation illustrates why money alone does not bring happiness.
5. General means the principle covers multiple cases. Presupposes means required, not merely helpful.
6. The example illustrates the principle that money alone is not enough for happiness; that principle is needed for the argument that we must pursue intangible things.
7. Go back and forth between the choice and the argument. Check every requirement before accepting the choice.

**Polished source breakdown:**

<p>Whenever we are faced with an answer choice that looks super abstract, the first thing to do is to always <strong>extract what keywords we can</strong> to try to make sense of the whole thing. Three words stand out in this answer choice: “illustrate”, “general principle”, and “presuppose”.</p>

<p>Now certain words have specific meanings within the LSAT. All three of these words appear repeatedly in LR questions and we need to memorize them:</p>

<div class="definition"><strong>Presuppose:</strong> in daily usage, a presupposition can mean either an assumption or something that is necessary. On the LSAT presuppose simply means “require”. It is talking about a necessary condition.</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>Illustrate:</strong> an illustration, as it appears in a LR question, is simply referring to an example/examples</div>

<div class="definition"><strong>General Principle:</strong>  a principle is a rule on what we should/shouldn’t do; and that it applies to multiple conditions and scenarios.</div>

<p>For example, “<em>John is always tired in the morning, he should go to bed earlier</em>” is not a principle, we are just talking about one person here. “<em>Those who are tired in the morning should strive to get to bed earlier</em>” is a principle. It’s no longer just about John anymore, it is a recommendation for a certain category of people.</p>

<p>So now there are three requirements that must be met if we were to select this hypothetical answer choice as the correct answer. First, the statement in question must be an example; second, behind this example is a principle on what we should or shouldn’t do; and lastly, this principle is required in order for the author to advance their argument.</p>

<p>Take a look at this example:</p>

<p class="example"><span class="underlined">Despite a high paying job, John was extremely stressed out and depressed</span>, so there are intangible things in life that we must pursue to find true happiness.</p>

<p>John’s situation is the illustration of a general principle; namely that having a lot of money isn’t enough to make you happy. Is this principle required in order for the conclusion to stand? Yes, the conclusion says factors other than money must be considered in order to be happy. The underlined statement satisfies all three requirements and if this was a Role question, this would be the correct answer.</p>

<p>So right away, we should realize that “<em>a consideration that might be taken to undermine the argument’s conclusion</em>” is referring to a real or potential counter argument. A counter argument runs contrary to the author’s conclusion. If I say “going to college is a great way to enjoy a higher standard of living down the road,” but you respond with “but many college graduates have to take years to pay back debt”, you are making a counter argument in opposition to me.</p>

<p>Continuing with our example, if I had anticipated your potential objection/counter argument, I can say something along the lines of “college graduates enjoy higher income than their non college counterparts even with the cost of debt factored in.” What I’m doing, essentially, is anticipating your potential objection and shutting you down in advance. <p>Here, the underlined statement is both a premise as well as a pre-emptive strike against a potential objection, namely that graduates end up with so much debt that going to college doesn’t make sense.</p>

## Choice 4

Source exercise 4; walkthrough and meaning pending approval.

> It makes an observation that, according to the argument, is insufficient to justify the claim that the argument concludes is false

**Plain English:** The statement gives an observation that the author says is not enough to support a claim the author rejects.

**Grading requirements (draft):**

- The statement makes an observation used to support a claim.
- The author says that observation is not enough to justify the claim.
- The argument concludes that claim is false.

1. Pull out “observation”, “insufficient”, “justify”, “claim”, “false”.
2. The statement gives an observation that the author says is not enough to support a claim the author rejects.
3. Identify the observation and the opposing claim. John points to celebrities’ smiles and concludes they are the happiest people.
4. Does the author reject that claim and say the observation does not justify it? David says smiles may be a performance and cites depression and substance abuse.
5. Insufficient means not enough. False describes the claim the author rejects, not the observation itself.
6. The smiles are John’s support; David says they do not establish happiness and argues that John’s conclusion is false.
7. Go back and forth between the choice and the argument. Check every requirement before accepting the choice.

**Polished source breakdown:**

<p>A key piece of information we can extract from this answer choice is that there is a <em>claim that the argument concludes is false</em>. In other words, the author of this argument is arguing AGAINST a claim. Someone has made a claim, and the author is saying that claim is false.</p>

<p>So we have two viewpoints in this potential stimulus. The author’s argument is directed against an adversary.</p>

<p>Further, we know that the opponent made an observation in support of their view, but the author is saying that observation is not enough to prove anything, and then goes on to argue that the opponent’s claim is false.</p>

<p>So in order for this answer choice to be correct, the stimulus must satisfy the following requirements: there must be an opposing view, the author must be arguing against this opposing view and declaring it false. The opponent must have provided an observation as a basis of support for their view, and the author must have made the claim that this observation is not enough to demonstrate the truth of the opponent’s view.</p>

<p>Let’s go a step further with a concrete example:</p>

<p class="example"><strong>John:</strong> <span class="underlined">Celebrities have the biggest smiles in front of the camera</span>, so they must be the happiest people alive.</p>

<p class="example"><strong>David:</strong> That is not true. Many celebrities suffer from depression and substance abuse. Smiling in front of the camera doesn’t mean a thing, they could simply be putting on a show for the audience.</p>

<p>Does the underlined statement fit the requirement for this answer choice? Yes, David is arguing that John’s claim is false. David also suggests that the observation John makes in support of his claim is insufficient to justify his claim.</p>

## Choice 8

Source exercise 8; walkthrough and meaning pending approval.

> It is a claim for which no justification is provided but that is required to establish the argument’s main conclusion

**Plain English:** The statement is an unsupported premise that the argument needs to reach its main conclusion.

**Grading requirements (draft):**

- The statement is a claim with no justification supplied in the argument.
- It supports the main conclusion.
- It is required for this argument to establish its main conclusion; being required does not mean it is enough by itself.

1. Pull out “claim”, “no justification”, “required”, “establish”, “main conclusion”.
2. The statement is an unsupported premise that the argument needs to reach its main conclusion.
3. Find a claim supporting the main conclusion without receiving support itself. John’s Olympic participation is the source’s premise.
4. Does the claim help establish the main conclusion, and is it required by this argument? The source relies on Olympic participation to identify John as the medal’s original owner.
5. No justification means no support is supplied for this claim in the argument. Required means necessary; it does not mean enough by itself.
6. The Olympic-participation statement is taken without further support and is treated as required for this argument’s claim about the original owner.
7. Go back and forth between the choice and the argument. Check every requirement before accepting the choice.

**Polished source breakdown:**

<p>There are two important pieces of information in this answer choice that can help us determine whether it’s the correct answer. First, it is “required” to establish the argument’s main conclusion. That means it’s necessary in order for the main conclusion to be valid. So there’s no way this answer choice is talking about the conclusion itself, it’s therefore either a premise or an intermediate conclusion.</p>

<p>Second, the answer choice says it’s a claim for which no justification is provided. Premises support the conclusion but do not have any other statements supporting them, whereas intermediate conclusions both support the conclusion and are supported by premises. So when we have a statement which supports the conclusion but is not supported by others, it can only mean one thing: we are talking about a premise here.</p>

<p>So we are essentially talking about a premise that is required to establish the truth of the conclusion. In other words, if the premise was untrue, the conclusion would not stand. Take a look at the following example for more clarity:</p>

<p class="example">I found an Olympic gold medal in the swim team locker room. It must have originally belonged to John. John is always losing things and <span class="underlined">he participated in the Olympics.</span></p>

<p>John’s participation in the Olympics is a requirement for the example’s conclusion to stand. In order for John to be the original owner of the medal, he MUST have been a participant at least.</p>

<p>John’s participation in the Olympics is also a premise that supports the example argument’s conclusion.</p>

## Choice 11

Source exercise 11; walkthrough and meaning pending approval.

> It is a hypothesis that the argument attempts to undermine by calling into question the sufficiency of the evidence

**Plain English:** The statement proposes a possible cause, and the author challenges it by saying the supporting evidence is not enough.

**Grading requirements (draft):**

- The statement is a hypothesis, a possible causal explanation.
- The author attempts to undermine that hypothesis.
- The challenge specifically concerns whether the evidence is sufficient to support the hypothesis.

1. Pull out “hypothesis”, “attempts”, “undermine”, “sufficiency”, “evidence”.
2. The statement proposes a possible cause, and the author challenges it by saying the supporting evidence is not enough.
3. Find a hypothesis and the evidence offered for it. A hypothesis proposes why a phenomenon occurs.
4. Does the author undermine that hypothesis specifically by questioning whether its evidence is enough? An unrelated objection would not satisfy the choice.
5. Attempts does not require successful refutation. Sufficiency asks whether there is enough evidence, not whether there is no evidence.
6. The target must be the proposed causal explanation; the author’s objection must concern the adequacy of its support.
7. Go back and forth between the choice and the argument. Check every requirement before accepting the choice.

**Polished source breakdown:**

<p><strong>What is a hypothesis?</strong> In science, a hypothesis is offered by scientists to try to explain a phenomenon in the physical world or universe. Why did different types of birds have physical features that are adapted to their physical environments? Darwin hypothesized that it is due to the fact that species can change over time, adapting to their environments via physical modifications generation after generation. Darwin came up with a hypothesis, which today we know as The Theory of Evolution, that has been supported by evidence gathered both by him and subsequent scientists. In other words, a hypothesis is simply a posited causal explanation of a phenomenon, explaining WHY something is the way it is.</p>

<div class="definition"><strong>On the LSAT, the word “hypothesis” can be understood as a “potential explanation/potential causation”; it usually appears in science/medicine themed LR stimuli.</strong></div>

<p>So what is this answer choice saying? The argument is attempting to undermine this hypothesis. It does so by saying that the evidence out there is not enough to support such a hypothesis. So when we are going through answer choices and come to this, we must make sure that there is a hypothesis present in the stimulus (the quoted statement being that hypothesis), the author is challenging this hypothesis, and the author does so by suggesting that the available evidence is not enough to support this hypothesis.</p>

### Draft opening: what are you being asked to recognize?

**New wording for review.** Place after the four untouched source introduction paragraphs, before the first choice. The opening prepares the learning task without supplying item 1’s answer.

An abstract answer choice describes what a statement does in an argument. The wording can be difficult even when the relationship it describes is familiar. Our job is to make that relationship clear.

There are two questions to answer for each choice. **What would have to be present for this description to fit? Can you build an argument where a statement actually does that?**

The first question keeps us from settling for a label. “It’s a premise” may tell us part of the story, but the choice can say much more. It can describe what the premise supports, whether the premise receives support itself, or how strong its relationship to the conclusion must be. Each of those details gives us something to check.

The second question makes us use the meaning. An example argument needs reasons and a conclusion. Identify the particular statement the answer choice would describe. Then check your own argument against every requirement you listed. If you can only explain the choice by repeating its words, go back to the part that is still unclear.

You can write your requirements and your example before opening the breakdown. You can also open the original breakdown when you need help, then return to the written work. The breakdown is there to teach the relationship. Reading it is useful; creating a matching example asks you to perform the skill yourself.

We’ll work through the original fifteen choices in order. As you read each explanation, keep comparing three things: the choice’s wording, the requirements it describes, and the role played by the statement in the example.

### Draft transition after choice 3: keep all the relationships

**New wording for review.** This is a short teaching pause after the original first three breakdowns, not another question or a replacement explanation.

The first three choices show why recognizing a familiar word is only the beginning. An illustration is an example. But an example of what? And what does that example contribute to the argument?

Look at the original examples again.

**Exact source example, choice 1:**

<p class="example"><span class="underlined">Despite a high paying job, John was extremely stressed out and depressed</span>, so there are intangible things in life that we must pursue to find true happiness.</p>

**Exact source example, choice 2:**

<p class="example"><span class="underlined">John has been smoking two packs a day since he was sixteen, last week he was diagnosed with lung cancer.</span> Smoking is a direct cause of lung cancer. We must avoid cigarettes at all costs if we want to stay healthy.</p>

In the first choice, the source asks us to find an example, a general principle behind it, and a principle the argument requires. In the second, the example illustrates a premise that supports the conclusion. Both contain examples, but the descriptions ask us to check different relationships. Just spotting John’s situation would leave part of the choice unchecked.

The third choice adds another relationship. A statement can support the author’s conclusion while answering a possible objection. Keep the word **might**: the objection can be anticipated. It does not have to be spoken by another person in the argument.

This is why we list requirements before creating an example. Each requirement tells us something the example must actually contain.

### Draft transition after choice 9: required and enough do different work

**New wording for review.** This follows both exact breakdowns, so it consolidates the distinction after the source has taught it.

Choices 8 and 9 both describe a claim used to establish the main conclusion. The details separate them.

Choice 8 says **no justification is provided**. The claim gives support without receiving support in the argument. It also says the claim is **required**. This argument needs it, but needing it does not tell us it is enough on its own.

Choice 9 says **justification is provided**. The claim receives support and then supports the main conclusion. The choice also says that, **if true**, it establishes the conclusion. Preserve both parts: the condition and the strength of the relationship.

Here is the original example for choice 9:

**Exact source example, choice 9:**

<p class="example">All practicing lawyers must have passed the bar. John is a partner at a prestigious law firm and therefore <span class="underlined">he is a practicing lawyer</span>, so he must have passed the bar.</p>

John’s position at the law firm supports the statement that he is a practicing lawyer. That statement then supports the conclusion that he passed the bar, using the rule stated at the start. The source is showing a claim that receives support and then gives support. We need both relationships to call it an intermediate conclusion.

When you build your own example, check the strength as carefully as the direction. A reason that makes a conclusion more believable does not automatically establish it. And a claim the argument needs does not automatically guarantee the conclusion.

### Draft closing after choice 15: the lecture ends here

**New wording for review.** The proposed closing follows the fifteenth exact breakdown. It does not introduce another methods list, quiz, or module.

You’ve now seen fifteen ways an answer choice can describe a statement’s role. The useful habit is the same across them: translate the words into a relationship you can check.

Start with what the choice names. Is it describing an example, a general principle, an observation, a hypothesis, or a conclusion? Then ask what happens to that idea. Is it used to support something, challenged, denied, or offered as an explanation?

Keep the qualifications. **Required** and **establishes** ask for different relationships. **Attempts** does not tell us the attempt succeeds. **Explicitly stated** asks for something actually said. These words are part of the description you must satisfy.

The last four choices make the direction of support especially clear. A statement can receive support without being the argument’s final point. It can receive support and then support the main conclusion. Or it can be the main conclusion, supported by another statement that itself receives support. Trace each link instead of treating every supported statement as the same kind of conclusion.

For any choice you want to revisit, use the written work to check your understanding. State what must be present. Build an example argument. Identify the statement the choice describes. Then compare that statement with every requirement and with the original breakdown.

That completes this lecture. Applying a choice to a full LSAT stimulus is a further task: you would need to find each required part and relationship in that particular argument. That application belongs in a separate practice sequence if we choose to add one.

#### See what the description requires

Consider the first original choice:

> It is used to illustrate the general principle that the argument presupposes

Let’s separate what that description asks us to find. **Illustrate** means give an example. A **general principle** is a rule that applies beyond one particular person or situation. **Presupposes** means the argument requires it.

That gives us three requirements. There must be an example. There must be a general principle behind the example. And that principle must be needed for the author’s argument. Recognizing just the example would leave the last two requirements unchecked.

Here is the original source example, with the described statement underlined:

<p class="example"><span class="underlined">Despite a high paying job, John was extremely stressed out and depressed</span>, so there are intangible things in life that we must pursue to find true happiness.</p>

What is underlined? John’s situation. That is the particular case: he has a high-paying job, but he is stressed and depressed.

Now look at the conclusion. The author says there are intangible things we must pursue to find true happiness. The source identifies the principle behind John’s case as **having a lot of money isn’t enough to make you happy**. That goes beyond John. It is the idea the author needs when arguing that we must consider factors other than money.

So we can check the description piece by piece. John’s case supplies the example. The source gives the general principle it illustrates. That principle is required for the argument as the source explains it. All three parts matter.

When you make your own example, do the same work. Write the argument and identify the particular statement the choice describes. Then ask where each requirement appears. If you cannot point to one of them, you have found the part of your example that still needs work.

The original breakdown remains available in full. Compare your requirements and example with it whenever you need to clarify the relationship.
