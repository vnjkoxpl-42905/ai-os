    Finding the Conclusion
    by Asking Why

    Most students find the conclusion by hunting for a word like but or therefore. Those words help when they show up, but plenty of conclusions don't have one. A check that works on every argument is two questions. Is this sentence an opinion? And does another sentence tell me why to believe it?

    Read it first

    Say each sentence in plain words

    The LSAT is written awkwardly on purpose. Reading isn't sounding out the words. It's understanding them. If you can't say a sentence the way you'd explain it to a friend, you haven't read it yet.

    1. Say it in your own words, like a short story. Who did what, and why? "Local shops typically run promotions to attract customers" becomes "Shops usually run sales to get people in." Don't hunt for fancier synonyms. Question it like a lawyer would: what are they really telling me?
    2. Break long sentences down. Start with the smallest piece you understand, even one word. Shorten long names too: "Dr. Okonkwo-Reyes's lab" can just be R.
    3. Swap pointer words for what they point to. This, that, they and such stand in for something said earlier. Find the closest noun that fits and put it back. "The school added a later bus. This was meant to help students in clubs" becomes "Adding a later bus was meant to help students in clubs."
    4. Guess where it's going. Reading actively means predicting where the stimulus is headed, not which answer is right. "Normally the bakery closes at six" tells you what's usual, and an author who points that out is typically setting up a twist. A wrong guess is fine. The next sentence shows you, and you update.
    The process is what you're practicing Getting one practice question right matters less than how you got there. On test day you can't repeat a result. You can repeat a process.
    Two kinds of sentences

    Fact or opinion?

    Sort every sentence first. On the LSAT these two words mean something narrower than they do in conversation.

    Fact

    Something you can measure or check. It describes the world as it is, so nobody argues with it.

    Looks like The painting sold for $20,000.
    TestCould I measure it or look it up?
    Opinion

    Someone's interpretation. You can't put it under a microscope.

    Looks like The painting is beautiful.
    TestIs someone interpreting?

    A fact isn't something you prove. It's something you use as proof. So this test is really asking what the author is using as proof and what the author is trying to convince you of. Occasionally an author argues for something checkable, like a disputed claim about the past. That claim still needs a reason, so the second check below decides.

    Why someone did something is never a fact Maya moved her desk to the window. That's a fact. Maya evidently moved it for the daylight. That's an interpretation, because you can't measure a motive. The root of evidently is evidence: the author is reading it, not just reporting it.
    Words like "effectively" can hide a number "The new bus route carries too few riders to run effectively" sounds like a judgment. Look for the number behind it. There's a count of riders it needs, and the route either reached it or didn't. You can check that, so it's a fact. "The new bus route is a waste of money" has no number behind it. That's an opinion.
    The check

    What makes a sentence the conclusion

    An argument is a conclusion plus the premises that support it. The conclusion isn't a summary of the topic, like in English class. It's one specific claim you can point to.

    1. Is it an opinion? Is the author interpreting something? If you could simply measure it or look it up, and the author is only reporting it, it's a fact.
    2. Does another sentence answer "Why should I believe this?" The reason has to be stated outright. It doesn't have to be a good one. The author has to be trying.

    Try the two checks

    Flip each switch and see what the sentence is.

    Neither check passes. Not the conclusion. Only an opinion. It still needs a reason. Not the conclusion yet. It has to pass both. It passes both. This is the conclusion.
    One check isn't enough An opinion with no reason behind it is only an opinion. It has to pass both.

    Link each sentence to what you've already read and ask why the author put it there. "The café tried three menus in March." Then: "It tracked sales of each one." The tracking explains the first sentence: the café was testing the menus. If the sentence you support also supports something further, keep going.

    Keywords

    Pivot words turn the direction, nothing more

    Keywords are friends when they're there, but not every conclusion has one. You need a process that works without them.

    butyethowever

    These tell you the author is about to push back. They don't tell you the next sentence is the conclusion. Often it is. Not always. The LSAT is never black and white.

    The trap that costs the most Seeing but and picking the next sentence without checking it. The sentence after a pivot can be a plain fact the author uses as proof. The practice below has one.

    Not every sentence is a premise or a conclusion. Some are background. Some are the other side's view. A sentence that gives a reason for the conclusion is a premise, even after a but.

    You said it yourself: pivot words don't always lead to the conclusion. And you asked whether the sentence with the but was a premise. That's the right question to ask about every sentence, and you were right.

    Which sentence is the conclusion?

    Run both checks on each sentence, then tap.

    The council plans to widen Mill Road. But the road carried 9,000 cars a day five years ago and carries 6,000 now. Widening it would spend money on a problem that's going away.

    The council plans to widen Mill Road.Not this oneNot the conclusion. It's a fact about the plan, and it's what the author is answering.

    On every argument

    Before you read the answer choices

    A vague sense that you know argument parts can give you false confidence. Pinning down the conclusion and its support exactly is the hard part, and most of what comes later gets easier once it's solid. On a main conclusion question, do this work in the stimulus, on the left side of your screen, before you look right.

    1. Say each sentence in your own words. Put real names in for pointer words.
    2. Guess where the argument is headed. Change the guess as you read.
    3. Ask fact or opinion, then "Why should I believe this?"
    4. Label every sentence. Conclusion, premise, or something else, like background or the other side's view.
    5. Then read the choices. On this question type you aren't searching them for the answer. You already have it. You're looking for the choice that matches the sentence you marked.
