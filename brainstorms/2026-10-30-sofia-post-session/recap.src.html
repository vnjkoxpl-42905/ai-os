Session recap · October 29

Find the Rule
Behind the Verdict

Principle questions ask you to find the standard that connects the evidence to the conclusion. Name that standard in your own words before you read the answers. Then test each answer on its own, and say out loud what is wrong with it.

Start here

Where is the principle?

Principle questions come in a few forms. What changes between them is where the principle sits, and so what you are looking for.

The principle is in the answers

Two forms. One asks which principle most justifies the conclusion. The other asks which principle the reasoning most conforms to.

Your job: pick the principle. Prephrase it before you read the choices.

The principle is in the stimulus

The principle is given to you, along with a situation. The answers say which fact would make the principle apply to that situation.

Your job: hold the given principle fixed. Find the answer that fits the situation to it.

The first form is the most common, and it is the one that has been slowing you down. We started there. Read the question stem carefully so you know which form you have before you start.

Before you read the answers

Build the bridge: if the premise, then the conclusion

When the answers are principles, prephrase one. The evidence is the "if." The conclusion is the "then." The right answer is a rule that carries you from one to the other.

IfDana trained every day this month
→
ThenDana deserves a spot on the team

The usual trap is a flipped order. An answer says, "If someone deserves a spot on the team, they trained every day this month." It uses the right ideas. It has them backwards. It starts from the conclusion and ends at the evidence, so it never gets Dana to the spot.

The trapRight ideas in the wrong order. Before you move on from an answer, check which idea is the "if" and which is the "then."
Judgments need a standard

Facts alone never reach a should

"Unethical," "wrong," "should" and "permissible" are judgments. A judgment needs a measuring stick: a standard that says what counts as unethical, what should be done, or what is allowed. A list of facts has no way to produce a verdict by itself.

Suppose the facts are these: a town lot sits empty most days, and the town has little green space. The conclusion is that the town should turn the lot into a garden. Nothing in those facts says what a town should do with an empty lot. The missing piece is the standard.

What is trueThe lot is empty. Green space is scarce.
→
The standardUnused public land should be put to community use.
→
The verdictTurn the lot into a garden.

The standard can be broad or narrow. It only has to reach at least one of the facts and carry it to the verdict. "If land is public and unused, it should be put to community use" works. So does "If a town lacks green space, it should add some." Either one connects what is true to what is claimed.

Use it every timeWhen the conclusion says "should," "unethical" or "permissible," ask: where is the standard? If the stimulus does not give one, the answer has to.
Check who is being judged

The answer has to judge the same thing the conclusion judges

Say the conclusion is that a theatre acted wrongly by overbooking its seats. An answer says, "It is wrong for ticket holders to arrive late." That answer judges ticket holders. The conclusion is about the theatre.

Proving that customers did something wrong does not prove the business did. When a standard sounds right, check what it is judging. If it judges a different person or thing, it cannot carry you to this conclusion.

You said it out loud: the answer was calling the wrong thing unethical. The conclusion judged the business practice, and that answer judged the people. That settled it.
When you are torn between two answers

Do not ask which looks better

You told us the pull toward comparing is strongest when you have two left. The two answers use similar language, and you end up weighing the wording side by side. Switch the question. Ask what is wrong with each one.

  1. Take one answer alone. Do not look at the other one.
  2. Say out loud every problem you can find with it: it judges the wrong thing, it flips the order, it adds a condition the argument never mentions, it is too narrow.
  3. If you can say what is wrong, it is out. If you cannot say anything is wrong with it, keep that in mind.
  4. Now do the same with the other answer, on its own.

Think of two suspects questioned in separate rooms. Compare them directly and both can sound believable. Question them one at a time and the weaker story starts to give way. One answer cracks. If instead you find you were wrong, you now know exactly what you did not understand, and that is worth more than a lucky guess.

Look for the wrong answerFour answers can be removed by naming what is wrong with them. You do not have to feel sure about the right one first.
Keep the "if" broad

The wider the starting condition, the more cases it reaches

A principle helps you only if the facts actually fall under it. The easier its starting condition is to meet, the more likely your facts qualify.

Broad starting condition

A student may borrow a lab laptop if they have a project due. Many students meet this.

Narrow starting condition

A student may borrow a lab laptop if they have a project due, a signed form, and a supervisor's approval. Far fewer students meet this.

Watch for "only if." It introduces a requirement, the thing that must be true. It does not say that meeting the requirement is enough. We saw an answer built on "only if" with one extra feature that made the principle too narrow to carry the case.

Stay out of arrow modeFor these questions, name the standard in plain words: what counts, and what follows. Diagramming every sentence in arrows tends to make the question more complicated, and you can lose the point of the standard.
Say the answer first

Three checks before you reveal

A bakery is accused of acting wrongly by selling day-old bread as fresh. One answer says, "It is wrong for customers to shop without reading labels." Does it support the conclusion?

No. It judges customers. The conclusion is about the bakery. Customers being careless would not make the bakery's practice wrong.

The roof of a public library leaks, and the budget has money left over. The conclusion is that the library should fix the roof now. What kind of idea must the right answer supply?

A standard that connects those facts to the verdict, such as: if a public building is damaged and funds are available, it should be repaired. The facts alone do not reach "should."

Maya has a project due. Answer X: "A student may borrow a lab laptop if they have a project due." Answer Y: "A student may borrow a lab laptop only if they have a project due and a signed form." Which one gets you to "Maya may borrow a laptop"?

X. Its starting condition is broad, and Maya meets it. Y lists what is required, so it tells you what must be true. It does not tell you that meeting the requirement, or Maya's project alone, makes her allowed.

The order to follow

On every principle question

  1. Read the stem. Is the principle in the answers, or in the stimulus?
  2. Find the conclusion. If it is a judgment, ask what standard is missing.
  3. Prephrase the standard as "if the premise, then the conclusion." Keep the order straight.
  4. Check what each answer judges. It has to match what the conclusion judges.
  5. Take each answer alone. Say what is wrong with it out loud, and notice which ones you cannot answer.
