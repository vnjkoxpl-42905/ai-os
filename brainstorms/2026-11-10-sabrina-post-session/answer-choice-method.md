# Give the Answer a Job

Start by finding what the author wants you to believe and why the author thinks you should believe it. Then use the question stem to decide what the correct answer has to do. That decision tells you what to test when two choices sound plausible.

## Find the gap

1. **Say what the author concludes.** If one seller charges $20 and another charges $25, the author might conclude that the first is cheaper overall.
2. **Name the evidence.** All you have so far is the sticker price. That evidence gives you a $5 lead before fees.
3. **Ask what could break the link.** If the first seller charges $8 more in fees, the cheaper sticker price gives you the higher total. Now you know where to look in the choices.

## Match the answer to the question

| If the stem asks for | Test the answer this way |
| --- | --- |
| A necessary assumption | Suppose the choice is false. Could the argument still work the way the author needs it to? If the first seller's fees erase the $5 lead, the cheaper sticker price won't make it cheaper overall. |
| A sufficient assumption | Suppose the choice is true. Does the conclusion have to follow from the choice and the evidence together? If neither seller charges fees, the first is cheaper. |
| A weakener | Suppose the choice is true. Does the evidence give you less reason to believe the conclusion? If the first seller charges more than $5 extra in fees, that's enough to hurt this particular argument. A weakener can also hurt the reasoning without settling the conclusion. |
| A flaw | Describe what the author did with the evidence. In this case, the author treated a comparison of sticker prices as a comparison of total costs. |

## Break a tie with a test

When two answers mention the same issue, put each one into the argument. A choice can sound right because it mentions fees and still leave you without a comparison. For example, “one seller charges for shipping” doesn't tell you which seller, how much they charge, or whether shipping cancels the sticker-price difference.
