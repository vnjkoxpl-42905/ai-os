    Make Your First Pass
    Count Under Time

    It's easy to spend another minute on a question because you feel close to the answer. That minute comes out of the questions you haven't seen yet. Pick a pace before you start. Then give every question a clear job by asking what the correct answer has to do.

    First pass

    Choose the pace before you start

    Give your timed section a job. You can use one section to find out what happens when you give the first ten questions 15 minutes. In a different section, you might try 12 or 17 minutes. The point is to learn which pace lets you get through the section while keeping the answers you usually get right.

    1. Pick one timing target. Write down when you want to finish the first ten questions. Treat 15 minutes as a starting experiment.
    2. Give yourself a way out. When a question stops moving, select your best answer, flag it and move on. You can come back with time left.
    3. Compare what happened. Record how far you got on the first pass, which questions you returned to and which misses you fixed on review.
    Question types

    Know what the answer has to do

    You get to the choices faster when you can say what would count as a good answer. We worked through several question types in the session. The same price example shows why two questions about assumptions can demand very different answers.

    Suppose a seller charges $20 for an item and a second seller charges $25. The author says the first seller is cheaper overall. A necessary assumption is something the argument needs. It needs the first seller's added fees to be less than $5 higher than the second seller's. If the difference is $5 or more, the lower sticker price no longer gives the lower total.

    A sufficient assumption gives the argument enough to prove the conclusion. If you learn that neither seller charges any fees, $20 really is cheaper than $25. That's more information than the author needs. The first seller could charge $2 in shipping and still be cheaper.

    Ask what the stem wantsFor necessary assumption, ask what the author needs. For sufficient assumption, ask what would make the conclusion follow.
    Test the link

    A good weakener gives the evidence another explanation

    Suppose students who study in the library earn better grades. The author concludes that the library causes the difference. Maybe students who choose the library also put in more study hours. If those hours account for the difference, the original observation can be true even if the location made no difference.

    When the question asks what weakens the argument, take each answer as true and ask what changes. An answer about a library adding new shelves is related to the topic. To weaken the argument, it needs to make the grade comparison less convincing as evidence for the author's explanation.

    You brought up lifestyle and demographic differences when we looked at studies. That's the right place to look. Ask whether the people in the two groups differ in some other way that could account for the result.
    Common traps

    Check the leap the author actually made

    When a flaw question asks what went wrong, name the move in the argument. If a review says there is no evidence that a program saves money and then calls the program a waste, ask what the review proved. A lack of proof that the program saves money leaves the question open.

    The trap that costs the mostIf you're rereading the same choice without a new question in mind, flag it. Get to the questions you haven't seen yet. Then come back and decide what evidence would change your mind.

    When you review a question, write the specific move that mattered. “The author left out shipping” tells you what to look for on the next price comparison. “Necessary assumption is hard” doesn't give you a next step.

    After the clock

    Review the process that cost you time

    Your untimed review is already telling you something. When you get a question right after time ends, compare the two attempts. What did you do with the extra time? Maybe you found the conclusion faster on a second read. Maybe you finally asked what the answer had to do. That difference gives you a habit to practise.

    1. Find the first place you got stuck. Was it the question stem, the argument, or two choices you couldn't separate?
    2. Write one thing to do next time. For a weaken question, try “Name the author's explanation before reading the choices.” That gives you something you can repeat.
    3. Keep the useful rules on one sheet. Take the notes you already have and save the line you actually need for each question type.
    Your work

    Try the pace on a real section

    1. Condense your study notes. Make a short sheet that says what the correct answer has to do for each question type. Start with the types that take the most time.
    2. Test one timing plan. Set your target before starting the section. Keep track of what you flagged and what you got back to. Try a different target next time if the first one costs you questions you can do.
    3. Try a brief warm-up. I said I'd share timing guidelines, warm-ups and how to use the platform. The goal of a warm-up is to start the first question already thinking through the steps you know.
    4. Use a practice test as a check-in. We talked about roughly one a week as an option. Focus most of your practice on sections with a specific goal and review what each section taught you.
