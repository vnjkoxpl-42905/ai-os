# Naming the Flaw Before the Choices

  A flaw question asks what is wrong with an argument. Most students read the answer choices and wait for one to sound right. The choices are written in abstract language, so two of them often sound right at once. There is a shorter path: name the mistake in the stimulus first, then look for the choice that says it. More than half of flaw questions are built on one of four mistakes, and this note gives you all four.

## Say the flaw before you look at A

  On the practice question from our session, a counselor argued that harsh criticism is necessary for people to change. You named the flaw correctly: sufficient treated as necessary. That is the hardest part. The next step is to hold that flaw in your head as one plain sentence, so the choices become a matching job.

1. **Find the conclusion and the evidence.** The conclusion is what the author wants you to accept. The evidence is the reason. "If you win the raffle, you get a bicycle. So winning is the only way to get a bicycle." The second sentence is the conclusion.

1. **Say exactly what the evidence gives.** Not what it sounds like. This evidence says winning is enough. It does not say winning is required.

1. **Say exactly what the conclusion adds.** It says winning is the only way. That is a bigger claim than "enough."

1. **Say the flaw in your own words.** "The evidence shows one route works. The author says it is the only route." Now read A to E looking for the choice that says that.

  **The lesson is the order.** Name the flaw first, then read the choices. If you read the choices first, you are deciding between sentences you only half understand.

## Four mistakes cover most flaw questions

  Learn these four names and the question each one asks. The next four sections take them one at a time, in the same order.

1Sufficient versus necessaryTreats *enough* as *the only way*Is this the only route, or just one that works?

## Enough is not the only way

  If you win the raffle, you get a bicycle. That rule says winning is enough. It does not say winning is the only way, because someone can buy a bicycle. A rule that says *enough* can be misused in two ways, and both show up on the test. Take this rule: anyone who has seen a certain documentary understands the prison system well.

  Saw the documentary → understands the prison system well

  **Illegal reversal**Jane understands the prison system really well, so she must have seen the documentary.
**Illegal negation**Megan has not seen the documentary, so she must have a poor understanding of the prison system.

  cause, because, leads to, only,

  **The trap that costs the most.** Seeing the word *cause* and choosing correlation versus causation. In the counselor question the evidence showed that harsh criticism gives a person a motive to change, and the conclusion said only harsh criticism will do. The word was a decoy. The flaw was the word *only*. Ask what the conclusion adds to the evidence.

  You said it yourself. You saw the word cause and thought causation. Then you read the conclusion again and asked, *"Why is it only harsh criticism? ... It could be gentle, it could be constructive ... it could be other things."* That second read is the method. It is the sufficient-versus-necessary question in your own words.

## Together is not the same as because

  On the test the evidence usually reports a study: two things happen together. The conclusion then says one of them is why the other happens. A correlation is easy to show. A cause is very hard to prove.

  Evidence: readers drop out less → Conclusion: reading keeps students in school

  **The evidence gives**People who read a lot are less likely to drop out of high school. The two go together.
**The conclusion claims**Reading is the reason students stay in school.

  **Three stories fit the same fact.** Reading could keep students in school. Staying in school could give students more time to read. Or richer parents could give a child more books and more help, and that drives both. A correlation does not choose between them. When the author picks one story, ask which others are still standing.

## A group is not each of its members

  What is true of a whole is not necessarily true of each part. What is true of one part is not necessarily true of the whole. The flaw runs in both directions, and the clue is a noun that changes size.

  Team → player, or player → team

  **Whole to part**The team is a star team, so every player on it is a superstar.
**Part to whole**She is the best guard in the country, so her team must be great.

  **You already do this.** You told me you had seen questions like this and it felt like common sense. Your words were, *"Just because the team does well overall doesn't mean every single player on the team does well."* The name is new. The reasoning is yours. Watch for a team and a player, a campus and a building.

## No proof is not proof of the opposite

  A lack of evidence is not evidence of lacking. If nobody has shown a claim is true, all you know is that nobody has shown it. You do not know it is false. The newer version of this flaw: someone knocks down one bad argument for a claim and says the claim itself is false. A bad argument for X is not proof of not-X.

  Nobody has shown it is true → so it is false

  **Unproven**No one has shown the claim is true. All we know is that it has not been shown.
**Proven false**Someone has shown the claim is false. The records put you in another city that night.

  **It fails in both directions.** *"Because I can't prove you're innocent, you're guilty."* And the reverse: because I can't prove you're guilty, you're innocent. You put it that way yourself, and I called it the best example. The same move shows up whenever an author says nobody has shown X, so not X.

## Name the flaw, then tap to check

    Say it out loud firstWhich of the four is it? Answer in your own words before you tap.

- A clear syllabus will cause students to start, because it gives them a plan. Students need a plan before they can start. So a clear syllabus is the only thing that gets students started. Answer: Sufficient treated as necessary. The word cause is a decoy. A syllabus is enough to get students started. The conclusion says it is the only way.

- People who walk to work report better moods. So walking to work improves your mood. Answer: Correlation versus causation. The evidence says walking and good mood go together. People in good moods may be the ones who walk, or something like a free morning could drive both.

- The restaurant has a great reputation, so every dish on its menu must be great. Answer: Whole to part. A restaurant can have a great reputation while one dish on the menu is poor.

- The lead engineer on the project is brilliant, so the project must be going well. Answer: Part to whole. One brilliant member does not make the whole project go well.

- Nobody has shown the old footbridge is unsafe, so it must be safe. Answer: Unproven versus proven false. No one showing it is unsafe is not the same as someone showing it is safe. All we know is that nothing has been shown.

## Turn each answer choice into a plain question

  Flaw answer choices are written in abstract language. They sound like a foreign language until you have seen them a few times. Your job is to translate them into plain questions about the stimulus.

1. **Name the flaw first.** One plain sentence about what the author did.

1. **Translate each choice into a question.** A choice that says the author refutes an opposing argument becomes: did the author attack anyone's argument? In the counselor question nothing was refuted, so that choice described something that never happened.

1. **Cut a choice for a reason you can say.** You told me you sometimes just know a choice is wrong. Write the reason, even a short one: "The stimulus never talks about avoiding anything." That reason is the skill.

1. **Read the wrong choices after the question, not only your pick.** Look up what each one means. A wrong choice today can be the right answer on the next question. After you have seen the same language a few times, it stops sounding abstract.
