# Sufficient Assumption

Use this beside a question that asks which added assumption allows the conclusion to follow logically. The answer is treated as true along with the premises. Together, they must prove the conclusion.

## Read the task

“Most helps to justify” asks for support. “Allows the conclusion to follow logically” asks for enough support to complete the inference. The word “justify” or “assumption” by itself isn't a reliable shortcut.

## Find the gap

1. Identify the exact conclusion and the evidence offered for it.
2. Separate the conclusion's naming part from its telling part. What do you know about the named person or group? What must you prove about them?
3. Track any new idea, change of group, quantity, time or certainty.
4. Predict a connection using the actual evidence, rather than adding a vaguely related fact.

## Common ways an answer finishes the argument

| Pattern | Given | What the answer supplies |
|---|---|---|
| Missing link | Rina is licensed. Conclusion: Rina is eligible. | Anyone licensed is eligible. |
| Missing fact | All licensed applicants are eligible. Conclusion: Rina is eligible. | Rina is a licensed applicant. |
| Longer chain | Licensed → registered. Registered → reviewed. Conclusion: licensed → eligible. | Reviewed → eligible. |
| Two exhaustive cases | The parcel goes by courier or customer pickup. Conclusion: its recipient isn'tified. | Courier delivery requires notifying the recipient, and customer pickup requires notifying the recipient. |

The last pattern isn't permission to assume both alternatives happened. Check precisely what the premises and answer say in each case.

## Check the direction of the rule

If the evidence says Rina is licensed and you need to prove she is eligible, licensed → eligible gets you there. Eligible → licensed only tells you something about people already eligible. It doesn't prove Rina is one of them.

A contrapositive says the same rule another way. If every archive volunteer has completed training, someone who hasn't completed training can't be an archive volunteer. You can use either version. With a longer rule, check what each and or or applies to. When you negate a whole and condition, you need or; when you negate a whole or condition, you need and.

Don't turn *some* or *most* into a universal conditional. Their exceptions can matter. An answer must be strong enough for the actual conclusion, but the strongest-sounding choice isn't automatically correct.

## Check the full argument

Add the answer as a premise. Can every premise and that answer be true while the conclusion is false? If yes, reject it. If no, explain the complete connection that forces the conclusion.

Check meaning, not identical words. A definition, synonym or contrapositive may supply the needed idea. Repeating the conclusion's vocabulary without linking it to the evidence doesn't work.

## Frequent traps

- A reversed rule: the result leads to the evidence rather than the evidence leading to the result.
- A partial connection: one condition is missing, or one part of an *and* condition is dropped.
- A scope mismatch: evidence about one member or one account is used to conclude something about an entire group.
- Mere strengthening: the answer makes the conclusion more plausible but leaves an exception that would make it false.
- A different question: an answer is required by the reasoning but is too little to complete it. That is a necessary-assumption test, not this test.
