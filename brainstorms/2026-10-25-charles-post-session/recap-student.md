# Build the missing connection

A sufficient assumption is an added premise that makes the conclusion follow from the evidence. It has to finish the reasoning. Think of our payment example: the bill is $20, but the customer has paid $18.50. An extra dollar helps; it still leaves fifty cents unpaid. A sufficient assumption has to cover the whole gap. An answer that sounds relevant, repeats the conclusion's vocabulary, or makes the conclusion more likely has not necessarily done that job.

## Start with the exact conclusion

Say who or what the conclusion is about, then say what the author claims about it. These are the naming part and the telling part. “Leah qualifies for the workshop” names Leah and tells us that she qualifies.

If the conclusion says “that view is wrong,” write out the view it rejects. You can't test whether an answer proves the conclusion while leaving the conclusion vague.

Then collect what the premises actually tell you. Keep names, groups, quantities and certainty intact. Evidence about one version of a theory doesn't automatically cover the entire field.

## Are you missing a rule or a fact?

Sometimes you have the facts but need a rule. Other times the rule is there, but you need a fact that lets you apply it. Check which part you already have before deciding what to add.

### Missing rule

Given: Leah completed the safety course.

Conclusion: Leah qualifies for the workshop.

We have a fact about Leah, but no rule connecting that fact to qualification. Add: **Anyone who completed the safety course qualifies for the workshop.**

Now the reasoning works: Leah completed the course; everyone who did qualifies; therefore Leah qualifies.

### Missing fact

Given: Anyone who completed the safety course qualifies for the workshop.

Conclusion: Leah qualifies for the workshop.

This time the rule is present. We need a fact that puts Leah within it. Add: **Leah completed the safety course.**

Think of our circuit example: both the fact and the rule have to connect. A rule about people who completed the course is no use if we don't know whether Leah completed it. A fact about Leah is no use if we don't have a rule taking us to qualification. The answer closes that gap.

## Make the arrow reach the conclusion

For the missing-rule example, the needed connection is:

**Completed the safety course → qualifies for the workshop**

Put the supplied evidence on the left side of the arrow and the result you need on the right. The wrong direction would be:

**Qualifies for the workshop → completed the safety course**

That rule tells us what qualified people must have done. It doesn't tell us that everyone who completed the course qualifies. Leah might have completed it and still lack another qualification. All the premises and that reversed answer could be true while the conclusion is false.

That's the test. Don't ask whether the words look right. Ask whether the conclusion now has to follow.

You identified the conclusion's subject and predicate in the sound-change question. That gave us a specific question to answer: how does the evidence about one account reach the broader conclusion?

## Predict the connection, then read the answers

Before reading choices, finish this sentence: **I know \_\_\_, and I need to prove \_\_\_.**

For Leah, you know she completed the course and need to prove she qualifies. That gives you a direction to check. It also explains why a rule about an unrelated person doesn'thing for this conclusion unless it actually connects back to Leah.

Watch for a genuinely new idea in the conclusion. If the evidence has no route to “qualifies,” the added premise must supply one. Look for that idea or its equivalent meaning. Matching an exact word is not the final test: a definition or a contrapositive can express the needed connection differently.

Use all the relevant evidence. The sentence nearest the conclusion may help you find the gap, but its position doesn't make the other premises disappear.

## A strong answer can finish the argument

Suppose an answer says: “Most people who completed the course qualify.” That leaves room for Leah to be an exception. It helps, but doesn't finish this argument.

“Anyone who completed the course qualifies” does finish it. That is why strong language can be useful on sufficient assumption questions: everyone leaves no exception for Leah. You need enough to guarantee this conclusion. Check what the strong claim actually connects; a rule about the wrong group will not do the job.

Nor must a sufficient assumption be necessary. We could prove Leah qualifies by adding a different fact, such as that she meets another qualification rule already supplied. The question asks whether the proposed addition works, not whether it is the only possible addition.

## Keep the question tasks separate

| Task | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| Must Be True | Use the supplied information to find what follows. Don't add a premise. |
| Sufficient Assumption | Add the answer as a premise. The conclusion must follow from the combined information. |
| Necessary Assumption | Find a premise the argument requires. Something required may still be too little to prove the conclusion by itself. |

Read the whole question stem. The word “assumption” alone doesn't tell you which test to run.

## Try the connection

Say what is missing before checking each explanation.

Every item on the approved list can be ordered. The spare battery is on the approved list. Conclusion: the spare battery can be ordered. Is another premise needed?

No. The supplied fact already meets the supplied rule. The conclusion follows without another assumption.

Nia submitted the application before noon. Conclusion: Nia met the deadline. What rule would be enough?

Everyone who submitted the application before noon met the deadline. Reversing this rule would not prove that Nia met it.

Every archive volunteer has completed training. Conclusion: Eli has completed training. What fact would be enough?

Eli is an archive volunteer. The rule then applies to Eli. “Everyone who completed training is a volunteer” would not supply the needed fact about Eli.

## When two answers remain

1. Put one answer into the argument as an additional premise.
2. Trace the actual route from the evidence to the exact conclusion, including its scope and strength.
3. Try to keep every premise and the answer true while making the conclusion false. If you can, the answer is not enough.
4. Check the other answer the same way. Write one sentence explaining the failure of the rejected answer.
