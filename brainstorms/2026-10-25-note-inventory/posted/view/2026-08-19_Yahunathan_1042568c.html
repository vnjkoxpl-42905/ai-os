Eleven famous flaws. Only five are common.

Every flaw answer on this test is one of about a dozen moves. Five of them are the answer. Six are there to waste your time. Knowing which is which is most of the work.

Tier one

The five that are actually the answer

Ranked by how often each one is the answer. Learn them in this order. If you can name the flaw before you read the answer choices, you are done in under a minute.

01 Necessary vs. Sufficient Most common

The author gives you a conditional rule, then runs it backwards or negates it to reach the conclusion. An illegal reversal, or an illegal negation.

People who have seen that documentary have a great understanding of the prison system. Since Megan still has not seen it, she must have a poor understanding of the prison system.

People who have seen that documentary have a great understanding of the prison system. Jane understands the prison system really well, so clearly she has seen it.

TellThe stimulus leans on "must," "only," "unless," "requires." If there is no conditional language anywhere, there is no conditional answer.

02 Causal Very common

Two things move together, and the author picks one causal story and commits to it while the other stories are still standing. Reverse causation and a shared third cause never got ruled out.

People enlisted in the military are more likely than those who are not to have excellent physiques. Evidently, recruitment officers select their future soldiers in large part on fitness and strength.

TellA study, a survey, a statistic, or a correlation in the premises, and a "because of" in the conclusion.

03 Unproven vs. Proven False Common

The author treats a lack of evidence as evidence of the opposite, or knocks down a bad argument for a claim and acts like that settles the claim itself.

Billy says his uncle was related to a famous singer, but he has not been able to connect them on a genealogy site. Thus, Billy's uncle was not related to him.

Some claim that a team should change its name because it is the only major sports team with that kind of mascot. However, another team still has one too. Thus, the first team should not change its name.

Tell"No evidence that," "has failed to show," "cannot prove." A bad argument for X is not proof of not-X.

04 Part vs. Whole Common

A trait that is true of each piece gets asserted of the group, or a trait of the group gets pushed down onto every piece.

The military is part of the government, and the military is conducted with lots of honor and discipline. Thus, the government overall is conducted with lots of honor and discipline.

Since rat poison is very harmful if ingested, each ingredient in rat poison would be very harmful if ingested.

TellWatch the nouns change scale between premise and conclusion: one member to the organization, one ingredient to the product, one year to the decade.

05 Sampling Real, learn last

The author generalizes from a group that may be biased, too small, or not representative of the population the conclusion is about.

Charlie is from that city and works for a theater company. Since Sheila is also from that city and also works for a theater company, she probably votes the same way he does.

Today's youth prefer comics to literature. After all, in a survey conducted at last year's comics convention, over 85% of the young people surveyed reported liking comics much more than novels.

TellTreat this as a check you run on a study question, not your first guess. No study samples an entire population, so "the sample was imperfect" alone is not enough. The sample has to be skewed toward the very thing being concluded.

Tier two

The six that are there to distract you

These show up as answer choices constantly and are almost never the answer. You are not learning them to find them in a stimulus. You are learning them so you can read the choice, name it, and cross it out without thinking.

Ad Hominem

Dismissing a view because the source is biased, has something to gain, or once said the opposite.

Attacks the arguer, not the argument

Inappropriate Appeal

A plea for sympathy, an "authority" whose credibility was never established, or picking one source's opinion over another's for no stated reason.

Same family as ad hominem

Circular Reasoning

The premise restates or quietly assumes the conclusion. The support is the thing it was supposed to support.

Almost always offered, almost never right

Equivocation

One term doing two different jobs. Also dressed up as "fails to adequately define the key phrase."

Rare enough that it stands out when real

Internal Contradiction

Something the author says undercuts something else the author says.

Check both claims before you take it

Straw Man

Beating a distorted, easier version of the other side's position and calling the real one defeated.

Compare what was said to what got answered

"Almost never" is not "never." These are the answer once in a while, and when one is, it usually stands out, because it is so rare. Suspicion is the rule, not exclusion. Read the choice, name the flaw, then decide.

How to actually drill this

01

Name it before the choices

Read the stimulus, say the flaw out loud in your own words, then go to the answers looking for your prediction. Reasoning each question from scratch is the habit this list exists to break.

02

Work Tier 1 in order

Conditional and causal are the biggest chunk of the pie. Get those two automatic before you spend time anywhere else.

03

Drill Tier 2 backwards

Do not hunt for these in stimuli. Practice naming them in the wrong answers you already eliminated, so the recognition gets fast.

04

Log the ones you miss

Every flaw you get wrong belongs to one of these eleven. Write down which one, and the pattern in your misses shows up within a week.
