How much does the answer actually move

Three checks, and they kill most of the tempting wrong answers on Weaken and Strengthen.

Judge the answer on its smallest reading. Some means at least one. So does at least one. An answer saying some of the drivers did the opposite is telling you about one driver, and you have to price it that way, because that is all it guarantees. Some US presidents have been men is a true sentence, and it is true because of one. You never get to judge an answer by the amount it might mean.

That gives you a fast cut: an answer that moves a single case can rarely dent a claim built on a whole comparison. Weigh it against how much the conclusion covers.

You cannot beat a hedged claim by finding an exception to it. If the author says most of the gardeners who used the new compost got bigger yields, they have already allowed that some did not. Pointing at one who did not is not an objection, it is something the author agreed to in advance. Someone claiming men are generally taller than women is not refuted by naming a tall woman. They said generally. Causal stimuli are written in this soft language on purpose, and answers offering a single non-conforming case are the bait built to match it.

On an if-true question, strong wording in the answer is a good sign, not a bad one. You are being handed the answer as true, so you want it to do as much work as possible once you accept it. Almost none of the shoppers read the sign hits harder than some shoppers may not have read the sign, and both are equally free.

An additional explanation is not an alternative one. An alternative replaces the author's cause. An additional one works alongside it, and it leaves the argument standing. If a slow month is partly down to bad weather, and the shop is also hard to find, the owner can still say the weather was part of it and worth planning around. Only the answer that takes the author's cause off the table weakens.

Match the answer to how hard the author actually committed. An author who writes one possible explanation has not claimed theirs is the only one. An answer that requires them to have ruled out every other explanation is then too strong, however much it looks like the defender you were hunting for. Read the hedge before you accept the answer.

Two answers that look right

An answer that restates the conclusion is a trap. They will not write an answer that simply repeats what the author already said, so an answer that sounds like the conclusion has almost always been altered on the way past, usually by flipping it. A conclusion saying you need Y in order to get X becomes an answer saying Y would guarantee X. Those are different claims, and only one of them is on the page.

Conditional conclusions hide inside causal arguments constantly. Need, must and require are the giveaway, and once you see one, check the direction of any answer that echoes it.

An author changing what people believe does not have to share the belief. A shop owner who thinks customers read a low price as poor quality can raise her prices to change that impression, without believing for a moment that price and quality are actually connected. She is working on the impression, not the goods. So an answer saying quality really does track price is not something she needs to assume, however closely it follows her reasoning. Ask whose head the belief has to sit in.

What each question wants from the same argument

One causal argument sits in the middle and the stem decides your job.

Stem
Your job
Flaw
Name what went wrong
Weaken
Raise one of the four, or break the evidence
Strengthen
Support the cause, or rule out one of the four
Necessary Assumption
Defend against one. Negate it and put it back in
Resolve or Explain
Supply what lets both facts be true at once
Most Supported
Go forward only as far as the stated chain allows

On Necessary Assumption, expect a defender: an answer with a not in it, protecting the cause from an attack the author never raised.

Flaw and Necessary Assumption both live mostly on ruling out alternatives, so read them expecting an answer about reverse causation or a third cause.

Anti-causal arguments sit mostly in Weaken, and they announce themselves: the fact that X does not show that Y. Supporting one takes the same two moves as attacking an ordinary causal claim, which is why they feel upside down. To help an author say do not jump to that conclusion, either supply a different explanation or make theirs hard to believe.

On Most Supported, when two answers survive, flip one and see whether the passage still makes sense. If milk always soured on exactly the seventh day, nobody would ever need to smell it first. The answer whose opposite breaks the passage is the one the passage supports.

Write your own attack before you read the choices. The test will have written one of your predictions almost word for word, and that only happens if you predict first.

When the argument proposes doing something

Some causal stimuli recommend an action to get a result. Ask four things:

1. Is it feasible? 2. Will people cooperate? 3. Could it backfire? 4. Does it treat the root issue?

Run all four. Which one bites changes from question to question, and backfire is the one people skip. A shop raising its prices to look more desirable may simply lose the customers it had, and that is the whole argument.

Say the plan's story out loud, every step of it. Put the new price on the sign, people walking past read it, they take the higher price for better goods, more of them come in. That is three separate links, not one, and a necessary assumption question will pick one of them and ask whether you noticed it was there. Spelling the story out is how the missing link becomes visible.

Late in a section, one stimulus will be doing several jobs at once, and the answer might rule out an alternative, or attack plausibility, or defend the plan, or supply a missing link. When a stimulus is doing more than one thing, hold all of the jobs open instead of committing to one and reading past the answer.

Before-and-after arguments belong here too. They look like comparisons and they are usually causal. Ask whether the two sides were fair to compare, and whether the one thing that changed is really what made the difference.

How to practise it

Working causal questions as one undifferentiated pile teaches less than it should. The arguments vary so much from one to the next that nothing accumulates. Group them instead: hold one stem fixed, and do three or four causal ones in a row so they sit close enough together to compare.

Then change the stem and run another few. The same causal argument asks for something different depending on what is being asked of it, and running those variations back to back is what makes the difference visible. That is worth more than volume.
