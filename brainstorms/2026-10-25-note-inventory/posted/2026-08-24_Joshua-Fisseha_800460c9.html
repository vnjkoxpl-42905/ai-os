Read every rule as the left requires the right, and the ones that link get easy to spot.

1. The word tells you where it goes

There are only four moves. Every word on this page does one of them.

  • Left. The word’s idea is the trigger. It goes on the left, exactly as written.
  • Right. The word’s idea is what is required. It goes on the right, exactly as written.
  • Negate left. The word’s idea goes right, and the other side picks up a NOT.
  • Negate right. The word’s idea goes left, and the second half flips.
The Left move

Whatever the word attaches to is the trigger. It goes on the left, exactly as written, and nothing gets negated.

It shows up like this
  • If A, then B.
  • If A, B.
  • B, if A.
  • B whenever A.
  • Every A is B.
  • All A are B.
Ask yourself

“If what?” or “Every who or what?” Whatever answers it goes on the left.

Which gives you
If left, right.
right if left.
If it rains, practice is cancelled.
rain → practice cancelled
The Right move

Whatever the word attaches to is what is required. It goes on the right, exactly as written. Same sentence as the one above, one word different, and the arrow points the other way.

It shows up like this
  • A only if B.
  • Only A are B.
  • A requires B.
  • B is necessary for A.
  • A must B.
Ask yourself

“What is this word naming as required?” Whatever answers it goes on the right.

Which gives you
left only if right.
right is required for left.
You pass only if you attend.
pass → attend
The Negate-left move

The attached idea is what is required, so it goes right, and the other side picks up a NOT. If you would rather not negate anything, swap the word for if not and put that on the left instead. Both routes give the same rule.

It shows up like this
  • A unless B.
  • No A without B.
  • Not A until B.
Ask yourself

Read the word as if not, then put that on the left and negate it.

Which gives you
left unless right.
Or read it as: if not right, then not left.
You cannot enter unless you have a pass.
enter → pass
The Negate-right move

The attached idea is the trigger and goes left, but the negative belongs to the second half of the sentence. Rewrite it as a positive before you draw and it stops being confusing: no A are B is all A are not B.

It shows up like this
  • No A is B.
  • None of the A are B.
  • A is never B.
  • A cannot be B.
Ask yourself

Rewrite it as a positive first: no A are B is all A are not B.

Which gives you
No left is right.
Which is left → NOT right.
No freshman may apply.
freshman → NOT apply

When the word is on no list

Some of these have a home in the table above. Others are ones you will meet cold. Either way the same question settles it. Ask what is guaranteed and what is required. Whatever sets the rule off goes left. Whatever has to be there goes right. That works whether the word made a list or not.

  • prerequisite and depends on are requirements, so they go right.
  • will is an outcome, so it goes right too.
  • the moment that is a trigger, so it goes left.
  • never is a negative, so treat it like the negate-right move.

Spotting the gap is the requirement, so it goes on the right, even though it happens first.

2. And, or, and the flip

Flipping a rule is what the test is really checking. Three steps, and the third is where the points are.

  • Swap. What was on the right goes on the left.
  • Negate. Positive becomes negative, negative becomes positive.
  • Switch the connector. Every and becomes an or, every or becomes an and.
A→B
flips to
NOT B→NOT A

Swap the sides, negate both. Then switch every and to an or.

Flip this. A and B → C

Check it

NOT C → NOT A or NOT B

If C did not happen, at least one of A or B is missing, and you cannot say which. Writing and there claims both are missing, which is more than the rule ever gave you.

Going the other way, A → B or C becomes NOT B and NOT C → NOT A.

3. Sentences that are not one plain arrow

Two shapes break the one-arrow pattern. One hands you the rule in both directions. The other is not a rule at all.

Both directions at once

Some sentences hand you the rule going both ways. Two words do it, and they do not work the same way.

  • If and only if (also then and only then, when and only when). The if puts it left and the only if puts it right, so it is telling you to do both.
  • Otherwise. It hands you a second rule covering the other case. Write both down, flip both, and the arrow runs both ways.
Why otherwise runs both ways

Take Jeff takes a vacation if he wins the lottery. Otherwise, he does not. That is two rules, not one: lottery → vacation and NOT lottery → NOT vacation. Flip both and they collapse into a double arrow. Either both happen or neither does.

The trap: one rule plus its own flip is not this. The deposit clears, otherwise the seat is released gives you only NOT deposit → released. Flipping that is the same rule said backwards, not a second direction. The sentence has to state both cases.

Not conditionals at all

These sit next to no and behave nothing like it. They count. They do not guarantee, so do not draw an arrow.

  • Few A are B means most A are not B.
  • Not all A are B means some A are not B.
Why an arrow would be wrong here

Not all the essays were graded tells you at least one was not graded. An arrow would say every essay behaves the same way, which is more than the sentence gave you. Flip these to the positive and leave them as statements.

4. What proves what, and what proves nothing

Given A → B, four things can happen and only two of them prove anything.

A is trueB has to be true.
B is falseA cannot be true.
A is falseNothing follows. Saying B is false is the illegal move.
B is trueNothing follows. Saying A is true is reading the arrow backwards.

Two of the four prove something. The other two prove nothing at all.

Try it. The rule is if the alarm sounds, the doors lock. You are told the doors are locked. What follows?

Check it

Nothing follows. The doors could be locked for any number of reasons. You were handed the right side and asked to claim the left.

When a flaw answer says the author confuses a necessary condition with a sufficient one, this is what it is describing. Go back and check whether the author had the right side and claimed the left.

5. What you do with a rule

Everything above gets the arrow onto the page. From there the test only ever asks for two things. Chain it to another rule and you get a new rule. Trigger it with a fact and you get a new fact.

rule + ruleA new rule. This is chaining.
rule + factA new fact. This is triggering.

Chaining

Whenever you have more than one conditional, ask one question: do these connect? If the right side of one rule is the left side of another, they link, and the link is the inference.

A→B→C→D
flips whole
NOT D→NOT C→NOT B→NOT A

A chain flips the same way a single rule does. Start at the far end and walk it backwards.

The test usually wants the front of the chain joined to the back, so A → D is the headline answer. But every shorter run is an inference too. That same chain also gives you A → C, B → D, NOT C → NOT A, and NOT D → NOT B. Any two links, read in order, is fair game.

Requirements chain by ear, and that is faster than drawing. Take you cannot get into Yale without a great score and you must study hard to get a great score. In requirement form: getting into Yale requires a great score, and a great score requires studying hard. So getting into Yale requires studying hard. Nothing was drawn. This is the payoff for reading every rule as the left requires the right.

Triggering

A rule sits there doing nothing until a fact sets it off. Hand it the left side and the right side has to follow. Hand it a missing piece of the right side and the rest of the right side has to carry the load.

ruleA → B or C
factA
factNOT C
soB

A fires the rule, so at least one of B or C is there. C is ruled out. That leaves B, and the answer is a flat statement, not another arrow.

Try it. You have two rules, A and B → C and C → D, and you are told as a fact that A is true. What can you infer?

Check it

B → D

The two rules chain: A and B → C → D. A is already in hand as a fact, so B is the only piece still missing from that trigger. Add B and the whole chain fires.

B → C is also true, but it stops one link short of what the chain gives you, and the test rewards the far end.

Which one is being asked for? Look at the shape of the answers. If they read if this, then that, you are chaining and the answer is a rule. If they read as a flat statement, you are triggering and the answer is a fact.

And whether to draw at all

Diagramming is setup, not the answer. Draw only when there is a reason to.

  • Two or more conditionals that could link. Draw. Chain them and look for what connects the two ends.
  • The conclusion is itself a conditional. Draw. The author is claiming one thing guarantees another.
  • One conditional in an ordinary argument. Do not draw. Hear it as a requirement and keep reading.

The missing link. An argument gives you A → B and concludes A → C. There is a hole in the chain, and the piece that fills it is B → C, or its flip, and that piece is the answer. Find the hole and you have found the answer.

All five, in five steps
  1. Find the word. One word tells you which side is the trigger and which side is required.
  2. Do what it says. Left, right, negate left, or negate right.
  3. Flip it when the question wants the other direction. Swap, negate, and switch every and to an or.
  4. Chain it or trigger it. Rule plus rule gives you a new rule. Rule plus fact gives you a new fact. Those are the only two things the test asks you to do with an arrow.
  5. Only draw when the rules could link. A single conditional inside an ordinary argument is something you hear, not something you write down.
