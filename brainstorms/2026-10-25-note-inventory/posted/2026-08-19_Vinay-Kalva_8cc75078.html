The words the test hides behind

The test almost never says cause. Learn what it says instead, because you cannot attack a causal claim you did not notice.

Explanation means cause. Every time. Not elaboration, not detail, not why in the ordinary sense. Swap the words and the sentence still works. Same for explains, accounts for, the reason for.

Phenomenon means effect. Hypothesis means proposed cause.

A study means a cause is coming. What a study reports is usually a correlation: what happened, how often, which groups differed. The moment you read researchers found, wait for the author to turn that into a claim about why.

Factor means causation, not correlation. So do reason, result, consequence, impact, influence, driver. One of those in the conclusion puts you in causal territory however flat the verb looked.

Then look at the verb joining the two things. Some verbs sit still. Some move.

Sits still
Moves
is, are, tend to be
makes, causes, produces

Same facts, one word apart:

Reports
Owes you proof

Sleeping eight hours raises scores
Neighborhoods with more police have less crime
More police reduce crime
Exercise is associated with longer life
Exercise extends life

The bait is more likely. On its own it is correlation. Makes more likely is causation. One word of motion moves the whole burden onto the author.

The quiet ones do it without looking like it: explains, is behind, is due to, stems from, is why.

Three ways a causal claim arrives

Only two of them say anything out loud.

1 · A causal verb. The claim sits inside one sentence.

Singing to your plants makes them grow.

2 · A connector between two claims. Two sentences, joined by something that does the causal work.

Joan sings to her plants. Due to this, they grow faster than average.

3 · Two differences, side by side. Nothing causal is stated at all.

Joan is the only one in her botany class who sings to her plants, and Joan's plants grow faster than everyone else's.

The first two tell you. The third only suggests it, and that is the one people read straight past, because there is no word to catch. Two differences lined up next to each other is an argument that one produced the other, whether or not anyone says so.

When the stem is Most Supported, check for the third kind first. That is where it lives.

What a correlation is

A correlation is when two things occur together. Once, or ten thousand times. Either way, correlation.

That's all it says. Two things showed up together. It does not say why.

Frequency does not settle it. Two things can occur together every single time and still be unrelated. How often they travel together is not why they do.

There are two kinds, and which one you have tells you where the answer lives.

1 · Across a group. Two things move together in a population. You spot it by rates, studies, surveys, associated with, or any comparison between two groups.

2 · In sequence. One thing happens, then the other. You spot it by after, since, ever since, following, once, then.

Sequence matters more than it looks. If X happened first, Y did not cause X, so a sequence argument arrives already defended against the reversal. That reads as strength and it is not. A hidden third cause is still live, and so is coincidence, and that is where the credited answer will be.

The rooster crows every morning before the sun rises. Perfect correlation, right order, all of recorded history. The rooster does not raise the sun. Approaching dawn wakes the bird, and that third thing was never ruled out by the sequence.

Find the conclusion before you attack anything

Three things settle it quickly.

Only a main clause can be the conclusion. When a sentence opens with although or while, that opening clause is a concession, and a concession is never what the author is really selling. Wait for the main clause. If a sentence has three clauses, you still have to pick one.

Watch for a claim being handed evidence. The results of a recent experiment establish that X. Everything before establish that is the support. X is the conclusion.

Then test it: if you were trying to convince a room of that claim, what would the evidence sound like? Say the candidate out loud, imagine what would back it up, and compare that to what is actually on the page. Say the candidate is the town has a parking problem. To argue that, you would need evidence that drivers cannot find spaces and that it costs the town something. If the page instead gives you a survey of which shops people visit, that was never the conclusion. The claim the evidence actually fits is the conclusion.

And find the thing being explained. It is almost always a comparison or a change: one group against another, or now against a few years ago. It comes in many forms and it is easy to read past, so name it explicitly. Once you have it, the author's explanation of it is what you are arguing with.

Two ways the argument itself will trip you

The cause is not the premise, and the effect is not the conclusion. Either one can sit in either slot. The same trap runs through conditional questions, where people map sufficient onto cause and necessary onto effect. Check which claim is doing the supporting and which is being supported, separately from which is the cause.

When you suspect an answer has flipped the direction, write the claim both ways and build the same sentence out of something you already have an opinion about. You need a great LSAT score to get into a great law school. Does a great LSAT score guarantee you get in? Obviously not, and the two sentences are not the same claim. If the answer has quietly turned a requirement into a guarantee, that test finds it in seconds.

A chain runs forward only. Reading a long time gives Jorge a headache, and headaches make him lie down. So reading a long time leads to lying down. That direction is fair. The reverse is not: Jorge is lying down, so he was reading. That treats one possible cause as the only cause. The joints are where the flaws get built, usually by swapping a phrase, reading for a long time quietly becoming reading anything.

Four ways to attack a causal claim

Say the claim out loud before you attack it. Name what X is, name what Y is, and name which way the arrow points. Most of the difficulty here is arguing with a claim you never stated.

Then there are four moves, and only four:

1 · Reverse it. Y caused X.

2 · A third cause. Something else produced both halves.

3 · Cause without effect. X showed up and Y did not.

4 · Effect without cause. Y showed up and X did not.

You can also go at the evidence rather than the cause: a comparison that was not fair, a sample that was not representative, totals measured against rates.

When the evidence compares two groups, your alternative has to explain both sides. One office switches to standing desks and reports fewer sore backs. The office that did not switch reports no change. Backs get sore in winter explains nothing here, because winter reached both offices. You need something that separates them. Any alternative that would have been just as true of the group that did not show the effect is not an alternative at all.

You are not proving your alternative. This works the way a criminal case works, and you are on the defense. You do not have to show your story is true. You have to put a story on the table the author never ruled out. The standard is whether the author's version is more likely than not, so one live alternative is enough.

Say which of the four you are running. Offering a reversal and calling it cause-without-effect is what makes answer choices start to feel arbitrary.

An argument that something did not cause something owes you exactly as much. Claiming there is no link is as strong a claim as claiming there is one, and the same four attacks apply. Debunking arguments read as automatically reasonable. They are not.
