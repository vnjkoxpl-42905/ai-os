Reading Flaw
Answer Choices

A flaw question is not one task. The answer choices come in three kinds, and each kind is checked a different way. When you get down to two and stall, it is almost always because you are running the wrong test for the kind of answer you are holding.

Step zero

Mark the conclusion first

Highlight it before you read a single choice. You are not testing choices against the argument in general, you are testing them against one claim, and holding that claim in your head while you read five options is exactly where it slips.

The taxonomy

The three kinds

Name the kind before you evaluate it. Everything downstream depends on getting this right.

Assumption

Something the author was already standing on, but never said.

Sounds like takes for granted presumes without justification fails to establish assumes
TestDoes the author have to agree? Negate it and see if the argument falls.
Objection

An attack from outside the argument. A weakener in different clothing.

Sounds like fails to consider overlooks the possibility disregards ignores that
TestAssume it is true. Does it damage the conclusion?
Description

A report of what the argument did, rather than a judgment of it.

Sounds like infers X on the grounds of Y bases the conclusion on Y treats X as Y
TestSwap the generic words for the real subject matter. Is that what happened?
An assumption, if trueHelps the argument. The author needed it standing.
An objection, if trueHurts the argument. The author never saw it coming.

The first two pull in opposite directions, and that is the whole difficulty. Test an assumption answer by asking whether it hurts the conclusion, and you will throw out the right answer every time.

Kind one

Assumption answers

Two steps, and it has to pass both.

  1. If this were true, does the argument get stronger? If yes, keep going. Passing this alone is not enough, because plenty of choices help without being required.
  2. Negate it. Does the conclusion collapse? If it only wobbles, the author did not need it. It was useful, not necessary.
Negating well is its own skill Negation means stepping outside the category, not flipping to the opposite. The negation of hot is not cold. It is anything that is not hot, and room temperature counts. When the choice already contains a not, the fastest move is to delete the not and read it straight through.

One exception. Do not negate a conditional answer choice. It gets messy fast. Ask instead whether it links the evidence to the conclusion.

Prefer the weaker answer. Soft language is easy for an author to be committed to, so it is easy for an argument to require. A sweeping claim is far more than the argument needs. A single conceivable exception is enough to sink these:

onlyneveralwaysallmust
You did this on the technology question when you leaned toward the choice that said “unlikely,” and you got there before finishing the analysis. That instinct is correct. Keep using it.
Kind two

Objection answers

Assume the choice is true, point it at the conclusion, and ask whether real damage is done. Sounding relevant is not damage.

Watch for net and total An author claiming an overall result has taken on the entire accounting, benefits included, so an upside they never weighed is a genuine hole. That was the moral socialization argument: the conclusion was about total suffering, which put the possible upside squarely in play.
Kind three

Descriptive answers

The abstract wording is the difficulty, so make it concrete. Substitute the argument's real terms for the generic ones and reread. Then check the choice word by word.

  1. Noun. Does this concept actually appear in the argument?
  2. Verb. Does the author really do this?
  3. Adjective or adverb. Does it describe the strength correctly?

Any single word that does not match the stimulus kills the choice.

The trap that costs the most A nearly perfect answer with one wrong word, sitting next to a correct answer worded so loosely it feels underwhelming. The vague but accurate choice beats the precise but wrong one.

The finalist

When you are down to two

  1. Name the kind. Assumption, objection, or description?
  2. Assumption: does it help, and does negating it bring the argument down?
  3. Objection: assume it true, does the conclusion take real damage?
  4. Description: substitute the real terms, then check every word against the stimulus.
  5. On an assumption question, is one of the two simply the safer claim?
  6. Write one line on why the loser lost.
