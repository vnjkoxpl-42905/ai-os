# Conditional Logic: What the Rule Gives You

Draft for Joshua's review · 2026-10-02

These six lessons form the opening chapter. Each teaches one idea, works through an example, and gives the student a small task before the attached practice. Visual and drill directions for our review are at the end, separate from the student copy. All new practice examples below are original draft examples.

## 1. Start with a guarantee

Take this statement:

> All dogs are mammals.

Now suppose I tell you that Luna is a dog. You know something else about Luna: she's a mammal.

You didn't need another fact about her. The rule already covers every dog, and Luna falls inside that group.

We can say the same rule another way:

> If something is a dog, then it is a mammal.

That's a conditional. It tells you that **when one condition is met, another must be met too.**

Notice the guarantee. The statement doesn't say dogs are usually mammals. It leaves no room for a dog that isn't a mammal.

We can write the relationship like this:

**Dog → Mammal**

Read the arrow as “if this is true, then that must be true.”

The rule doesn't tell us that a particular dog exists. It tells us what must be true of anything that is a dog. When we add the fact about Luna, we can apply the rule to her.

### See the relationship

Imagine a box containing every mammal. Every dog has to fit inside that box. Mammals can sit outside the dog box, but dogs can't sit outside the mammal box.

That's why knowing “dog” gives us “mammal.”

### Your turn

> All volunteers wear badges. Ari is a volunteer.

What does the rule guarantee about Ari?

<details>
<summary>See the reasoning</summary>

Ari wears a badge. The rule covers every volunteer, and Ari is a volunteer. **Volunteer → Wears a badge.**

</details>

**Keep this:** A conditional tells you what must follow when its condition is met.

## 2. Why the direction matters

Let's stay with the same rule:

**Dog → Mammal**

If I tell you Luna is a dog, you know she's a mammal.

But if I tell you Luna is a mammal, do you know she's a dog? No. She could be a cat. The rule has room for mammals that aren't dogs.

The arrow gives us a direction. Switching that direction changes the claim.

That's a major part of conditional reasoning on the LSAT. You need to know what a rule gives you, and where someone has gone beyond it.

In a Must Be True question, you may apply a rule to a fact. In a Flaw question, someone may reverse the rule and claim more than the premises establish. In a Sufficient Assumption question, you may need a missing rule that connects the evidence to the conclusion.

The question changes. The relationship still matters.

### Compare the two moves

| What we know | What we can conclude | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Luna is a dog. | Luna is a mammal. | The rule covers every dog. |
| Luna is a mammal. | We don't know whether Luna is a dog. | Other mammals are allowed. |

### Your turn

> Every volunteer wears a badge. Ari wears a badge.

Does that establish that Ari is a volunteer? Explain your answer using the rule.

<details>
<summary>See the reasoning</summary>

No. The rule requires volunteers to wear badges. It doesn't say that only volunteers wear them. Ari could be a staff member who also wears a badge.

</details>

**Keep this:** Follow the direction the rule gives you. Before accepting a conclusion, check that the rule actually reaches it.

## 3. Sufficient means enough

We have names for the two sides of a conditional. Start with the left:

**Dog → Mammal**

Being a dog is **sufficient** for being a mammal. It's enough. Once we know something is a dog, we have what we need to conclude that it's a mammal.

You can think of the sufficient condition as the trigger. When it is met, the rule gives you the other side.

> If a student earns full credit, that student passes the assignment.

**Earns full credit → Passes**

Earning full credit is sufficient for passing. Suppose Maya earned full credit. Put yourself in her situation: under this rule, what do you know? She passes.

But full credit might not be the only way to pass. A student could earn less than full credit and still pass. “Enough” doesn't mean “the only way.”

### Your turn

> If a person wins the race, that person receives a medal.

Which condition is enough to guarantee the other? Then write the arrow.

<details>
<summary>See the reasoning</summary>

Winning is enough to guarantee receiving a medal. **Wins → Receives a medal.** The rule doesn't tell us that winning is the only way to receive one.

</details>

**Keep this:** The sufficient condition goes on the left. Meeting it is enough to guarantee the right.

## 4. Necessary means required

Now look at the other side:

**Dog → Mammal**

Being a mammal is **necessary** for being a dog. A dog has to be a mammal.

Necessary means required. It doesn't mean enough.

Return to the assignment:

**Earns full credit → Passes**

Under this rule, passing is necessary for earning full credit. You can't earn full credit and fail. But knowing someone passed doesn't tell you they earned full credit.

Here's a different example:

> Entering the archive requires a pass.

**Enters the archive → Has a pass**

The pass is required. That's why it goes on the right. Having a pass doesn't, by itself, establish that someone enters the archive.

### Put the two sides together

| Sufficient | Necessary |
|---|---|
| Enough to guarantee the other condition. | Required whenever the sufficient condition is met. |
| Goes on the left. | Goes on the right. |
| If I have this, what must follow? | What must I have in order for the left side to be true? |

These are two roles in the same relationship. You don't decide that a condition is always sufficient or always necessary. You read the particular rule.

### Your turn

> Every finalist receives an invitation.

Identify the sufficient condition and the necessary condition. Does receiving an invitation establish that someone is a finalist?

<details>
<summary>See the reasoning</summary>

Being a finalist is sufficient. Receiving an invitation is necessary. **Finalist → Receives an invitation.** Receiving one doesn't establish finalist status; the rule permits other people to receive invitations too.

</details>

**Keep this:** Sufficient is enough. Necessary is required. Required means right.

## 5. Group 1: Find the sufficient idea

You already understand the relationship. Now we're going to recognize the different ways a sentence can express it.

Our Group 1 indicators are:

**if · when · where · all · the only · every · any**

These introduce the sufficient idea. That complete idea goes on the left of the arrow.

The important question is: **“What is the indicator referring to?”**

### Start with “if”

> If a student revises the essay, the student receives feedback.

If what? If the student revises the essay.

That's the sufficient idea:

**Revises the essay → Receives feedback**

Now move the same clause:

> A student receives feedback if the student revises the essay.

If what? Still, if the student revises the essay. The arrow stays the same. Sentence order changed; the relationship didn't.

### The same relationship in other words

| Sentence | What the indicator refers to | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| Every volunteer wears a badge. | Every volunteer. | Volunteer → Wears a badge |
| Any applicant who submits late pays a fee. | Any applicant who submits late. | Applicant submits late → Pays a fee |
| When the alarm sounds, the staff evacuate. | When the alarm sounds. | Alarm sounds → Staff evacuate |
| Where there is smoke, there is fire. | Where there is smoke. | Smoke → Fire |

Keep the complete idea. In the applicant sentence, “applicant” alone isn't enough. The rule applies to an applicant **who submits late**.

### Read “the only” carefully

> The only people admitted are members.

The only what? The only people admitted.

**Admitted → Member**

Everyone admitted must be a member. It doesn't tell us that every member is admitted.

Don't skip “the.” We'll compare “the only” with “only” in the next lesson.

### Your turn

For each sentence, identify the indicator, ask what it refers to, and write the complete rule.

1. All registered guests receive a wristband.
2. A report is reviewed if it is submitted before noon.
3. The only vehicles permitted inside are electric vehicles.

<details>
<summary>See the reasoning</summary>

1. **All** refers to registered guests. Registered guest → Receives a wristband.
2. **If** refers to submission before noon. Submitted before noon → Reviewed.
3. **The only** refers to vehicles permitted inside. Vehicle permitted inside → Electric vehicle.

</details>

**Keep this:** Find the indicator's target. For Group 1, that complete idea goes left.

## 6. Group 2: Find what's required

Group 2 introduces the necessary idea. That complete idea goes on the right.

Our Group 2 indicators are:

**only · only if · only when · only where · always · requires · must**

Use the same question: **“What is the indicator referring to?”**

### Start with “only if”

> A student enters the archive only if the student has a pass.

Only if what? Only if the student has a pass.

The pass is required:

**Enters the archive → Has a pass**

Compare that with “if”:

| Sentence | Rule |
|---|---|
| A student enters if the student has a pass. | Has a pass → Enters |
| A student enters only if the student has a pass. | Enters → Has a pass |

The first makes a pass enough. The second makes a pass required. One word changes what the sentence guarantees.

### Required things can appear first

> A pass is required for entry.

What is required? A pass.

**Enters → Has a pass**

The necessary idea appears before “required.” That's why we ask what the indicator refers to instead of automatically taking the words after it.

### Compare “only” and “the only”

> Only members are admitted.

Only what? Only members. Being a member is required for admission.

**Admitted → Member**

> The only people admitted are members.

The only what? The only people admitted. That is the sufficient idea.

**Admitted → Member**

These two sentences express the same rule. The indicator points to a different part of each sentence. Read the whole statement before deciding where each idea belongs.

### More ways to state a requirement

| Sentence | Required idea | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| A finalist must attend the interview. | Must attend the interview. | Finalist → Must attend interview |
| Approval requires a signature. | Has a signature. | Approved → Has signature |
| A review occurs only when a complaint is filed. | A complaint is filed. | Review occurs → Complaint filed |
| The service operates only where a station is available. | A station is available. | Service operates → Station available |
| A finalist always receives an invitation. | Receives an invitation. | Finalist → Receives invitation |

Here, “always” expresses a rule without exceptions. “Must attend” sets a requirement; it doesn't prove the finalist actually attended. Keep that obligation in the diagram. Read the sentence in context; a word by itself doesn't give you the complete relationship.

### Your turn

Identify what's required, then write each rule.

1. Only registered guests receive wristbands.
2. A permit is required for construction.
3. A student graduates only if the student completes the project.

<details>
<summary>See the reasoning</summary>

1. Registration is required. Receives wristband → Registered guest.
2. A permit is required. Construction → Has permit.
3. Completing the project is required. Graduates → Completes project.

</details>

**Keep this:** For Group 2, the required idea goes right. Ask what the indicator refers to, then check the meaning of your arrow against the sentence.

## For our review: How the teaching and practice fit together

This section is editorial planning, not student-facing copy. Each lesson ends with its own drill before the student continues. Joshua can assign a whole chapter or an individual lesson and its practice. Students see their assigned work and its next step.

| Lesson | Visual or guided interaction | Attached drill | What it establishes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Guarantee | Reuse the nested-box activity shown in the supplied screenshot if its existing implementation correctly validates containment. Place dogs inside mammals; then place Luna inside dogs. No strict size claim: sets may be equal. | Distinguish guaranteed rules from “usually” claims; apply one explicit rule to a matching fact. | Student understands a guarantee and rule versus fact. |
| 2. Direction | Place a cat inside mammals but outside dogs. Compare what the two starting facts allow. | Rule + fact: choose follows or undetermined, then explain. | Student applies the rule without reversing it. |
| 3. Sufficient | Reveal “passes” when “earns full credit” is selected. Show a separate permitted example of passing without full credit. | Identify what is enough in plain if/then rules; apply it. | Student understands enough without assuming only way. |
| 4. Necessary | Highlight the required pass in an entry example; compare a person with a pass who stays outside. | Identify both roles; distinguish required from enough. | Student can explain both sides of one rule. |
| 5. Group 1 | Highlight indicator → select complete target → place target left → supply right. Move the if-clause without changing the rule. | Short blocks by indicator, then mixed Group 1 translations; include complete modifiers and “the only.” | Student translates every Group 1 form, beyond keyword recognition. |
| 6. Group 2 | Same interaction with target placed right; place “a pass” before “required”; compare if/only if and only/the only. | Short blocks by indicator, then mixed Group 1/2 translations in both sentence orders. | Student interprets requirements without positional shortcuts. |

Drill scaffolding fades: first show a worked example, then give a target-selection prompt, then require an independent translation. Feedback explains the student's specific directional or target error. Keep initial learning untimed. Timing can be added to teacher-assigned medium and advanced practice later, as previously agreed.

These are six opening lessons, not six difficulty levels. Contraposition, Groups 3 and 4, compound conditions, chaining, and LSAT application belong in subsequent lessons. Quantities remain the next phase after the conditional curriculum.
