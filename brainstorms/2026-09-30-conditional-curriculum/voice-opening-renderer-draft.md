## Orientation: What are conditionals?

Hero subtitle: A conditional tells us that if one thing is true, something else has to be true too. Let's see what that connection lets you conclude, and what it leaves open.

### One condition tells you something else

A condition is something that can be true or false of the situation we're considering. A door is open. A person has a ticket. An order has been packed. Each gives us an idea whose truth we can ask about.

A conditional statement connects two conditions. It says that if the first is true, the other has to be true too. It doesn't tell us that the first condition actually occurred. It tells us what would follow if it did.

That distinction matters. You can understand a rule about people who have tickets without knowing whether anyone has a ticket. Later, a fact about one person may give you a way to use that rule.

Visual: two ordinary-language panels, “If this condition is met...” and “...the other condition has to be true.” No arrow or sufficient/necessary labels yet.

### Why learn this?

On the LSAT, you're often deciding whether a conclusion follows from what you've been told. A conditional may supply the connection you need. It can also help you see exactly where an argument asks you to accept more than its information supports.

The important part is the direction. If one condition guarantees another, can we start with the other condition and go back? Sometimes another statement allows that. But the first rule alone may not.

We'll work with one everyday rule and change the fact we know about a particular order. You'll see a conclusion the rule supports and another that would need more information. That's the distinction you'll use when checking what must be true or deciding whether an argument has gone too far.

### A guarantee is stronger than a tendency

Compare these statements:

**Orders marked ready are usually packed.**

**Every order marked ready has been packed.**

Usually leaves room for exceptions. You could find an order marked ready that hasn't been packed and still accept the first statement. The second statement doesn't leave that possibility open. Every order with that label has been packed.

For an LSAT question, take the stated rule as given. A real shop could make a mistake, but we're asking what follows from this particular statement. The conditional gives us a guarantee within the information we're considering.

### Read one rule in ordinary English

**Every order marked ready has been packed.**

There are two complete ideas here: an order is marked ready, and an order has been packed. The sentence tells us something about every order that meets the first condition.

Read it this way: if an order is marked ready, then it has been packed. We don't need to know why the shop uses that label. We need to know what the sentence tells us about an order that has it.

Prediction pause: **Order A is marked ready. What do we now know about it?**

Reveal: **Order A has been packed.** The rule covers every order marked ready, and the fact tells us that Order A is one of those orders. That's the connection. The rule tells us what has to be true of the group; the fact lets us apply it to this case.

We haven't learned that every order in the shop is packed. Our conclusion concerns Order A because that's the order the fact tells us about.

### The arrow is a shorter way to write it

Now that we understand the sentence, we can give the two conditions shorter labels. The arrow records the connection between them:

**Marked ready → Packed**

Read it from left to right: if the order is marked ready, then it has been packed. The left condition gives us enough information to establish the right condition under this rule.

The arrow doesn't mean “and then.” Packing could happen before the label is added. It also doesn't claim that the label causes the packing. We're recording what follows logically, so the same diagram can preserve a relationship whose real-world events happen in a different order.

### Change the fact, keep the rule

Keep the rule exactly the same. This time, the fact tells us about packing:

**Every order marked ready has been packed.**

Prediction pause: **Order B has been packed. Do we know whether it is marked ready?**

Reveal: **We don't know yet.** Order B could be marked ready. It could also be packed and waiting for a ready label. Both possibilities fit the statement.

The rule tells us about every ready order. It hasn't told us that every packed order gets that label. To conclude that Order B is ready, we'd need that additional connection.

The picture shows allowed arrangements; the rule doesn't require the two groups to have different sizes.

### Try the same reasoning with a new rule

You've used the rule in its stated direction and checked what happens when you start from its result. Try that distinction with a different sentence.

## Sufficient and necessary: What is enough, and what is required?

Hero subtitle: The same rule gives its two conditions different jobs. Let's take each one seriously before giving it a place in the diagram.

### Sufficient means enough

Return to our order:

**Every order marked ready has been packed.**

If you know an order is marked ready, you have enough information to conclude it has been packed. You don't need a second fact about its contents. The rule already tells you what must be true of every order with that label.

We call being marked ready the sufficient condition. Sufficient means enough. Here, enough to establish that the order has been packed under this particular rule.

Prediction pause: **Order C is marked ready. Do you need another fact about C to know it has been packed?**

Reveal: No. The ready label meets the condition the rule tells us about. The rule and that fact are enough to establish packing. This doesn't make the label the only possible way to find out that an order is packed.

Existing GateExplorer follows the reveal. Keep meaningful labels and readable roles.

### Enough does not mean the only way

Suppose Order D isn't marked ready. We no longer have the fact that allowed us to use the rule in its stated direction. But that doesn't settle the packing question.

Prediction pause: **Order D isn't marked ready. Could it still have been packed?**

Reveal: Yes. D could be packed and waiting for its label. It could also be unpacked. Neither possibility creates a ready order that hasn't been packed, so both fit the rule.

This is why “enough” needs care. The sentence gives us one condition that settles packing. It hasn't said packing happens only for those orders. When that sufficient condition isn't present, the other condition may still be true.

Keep the existing collected/receipt check. Its fresh rule checks whether the learner recognizes the sufficient role in a new situation.

### Necessary means required

Now look at the other condition in the same rule. An order marked ready has to have been packed. Packing is required for an order to meet the ready condition without breaking the rule.

We call being packed the necessary condition. Necessary means required. It names something that has to be true whenever the sufficient condition is true.

Ask “required for what?” Here, required for an order marked ready under this rule. The relationship gives the word its meaning. Being packed isn't permanently a necessary condition in every sentence about orders.

Prediction pause: **Which condition does a ready order have to meet under this rule?**

Reveal: It has to be packed. Otherwise we'd have a ready order that wasn't packed, which the sentence rules out. The ready label is enough to establish packing, and packing is required for every ready order. Those are two views of the same relationship.

Then show the existing two-role visual, with “Enough to establish packing” beside ready and “Required for every ready order” beside packed.

### Having a requirement does not guarantee the result

Let's use a different requirement:

**Entering the archive requires a pass.**

The sentence tells us what someone needs for entry: a pass. If someone enters the archive, they have that pass. That's why “has a pass” belongs on the required side.

**Enters the archive → Has a pass**

Prediction pause: **Mina has a pass. Does this sentence establish that she entered the archive?**

Reveal: No. Mina could have a pass and remain outside. The rule tells us what entry requires; it doesn't tell us that every person with a pass enters. To make that conclusion, we'd need a rule giving the pass that additional job.

Necessary doesn't mean enough. A requirement can be present while the other condition remains unknown. Keep the existing finalist/invitation check to test the distinction with a new rule.

### Read the roles in the relationship

When you see **Marked ready → Packed**, the left condition is sufficient for the right: it's enough to establish it. The right condition is necessary for the left: it's required whenever the left holds.

The names describe roles in this rule. A different sentence could give the same idea a different role. So don't ask whether “a pass” is sufficient or necessary by itself. Ask what the complete sentence says having the pass does.

The next two groups give us ways to recognize these roles in varied wording. We'll keep returning to the meaning because a familiar word can only help if you know what complete idea it refers to.

## Group 1: Find what is enough

Hero subtitle: These expressions can point to the sufficient idea. Read what each one says before deciding which complete condition belongs on the left.

Opening explanation: You already know what a sufficient condition does. When it holds, the rule gives you the other condition. Group 1 helps you recognize that job in several kinds of sentence. We'll work through the meaning of each expression, then practice finding its complete target.

### If: the starting condition can come last

**A report is reviewed if it is submitted before noon.**

Read the if-clause first: the report is submitted before noon. The sentence makes that enough for review. A report meeting that condition has to be reviewed under this rule.

The target of if is the complete idea “submitted before noon.” It appears second in the sentence, but it still has the sufficient role. The order of the clauses doesn't change what guarantees what.

Prediction pause: **Which fact lets you use this rule to establish review: submission before noon, or review itself?**

Reveal: Submission before noon. Write **Submitted before noon → Reviewed**. Read it in ordinary English and compare it with the original. Starting from review instead would make a new promise about submission time that this sentence hasn't given us.

### When: an occasion can supply enough information

**When the alarm sounds, the staff evacuate.**

In this stated rule, the alarm sounding is enough for the staff to evacuate. When refers to that complete event. We're treating the sentence as an exceptionless relationship, rather than making a prediction about what staff usually do.

Prediction pause: **The staff are evacuating. Does this rule establish that the alarm sounded?**

Reveal: No. It tells us what follows when the alarm sounds. It hasn't said an alarm is the only possible reason for evacuation. Write **Alarm sounds → Staff evacuate**. “The staff evacuate when the alarm sounds” expresses the same direction even though the event comes last.

### Where: keep the location attached

**Where a station is available, the service operates.**

The sentence tells us about a location where a station is available. At that location, station availability is enough for the service to operate. Where refers to the station being available, not to service in some unrelated place.

Prediction pause: **A station is available at North Square. What does the sentence establish about North Square?**

Reveal: The service operates there. Write **Station available at a location → Service operates there**. Keep the location the same on both sides. The statement hasn't given us a conclusion about another square whose station status we don't know.

### All: name the complete group

**All registered guests receive a wristband.**

The statement covers each registered guest. Once you know someone is a registered guest, you can conclude that person receives a wristband. Registered matters: the rule doesn't tell us about every guest regardless of registration.

Prediction pause: **Nora is a guest, but we haven't been told whether she's registered. Does the rule settle her wristband status?**

Reveal: No. The fact doesn't yet put Nora in the complete group the rule covers. Write **Registered guest → Receives wristband**. Keep the modifier when you shorten the condition; leaving out registered changes the rule.

### The only: read the whole expression

**The only vehicles permitted inside are electric vehicles.**

The sentence limits the vehicles permitted inside. Every vehicle in that group has to be electric. Start by asking “the only what?” The target is vehicles permitted inside, not electric vehicles.

Prediction pause: **You learn a vehicle was permitted inside. What does the sentence tell you about it?**

Reveal: It is electric. Write **Vehicle permitted inside → Electric vehicle**. The sentence hasn't promised that every electric vehicle is admitted. An electric vehicle that remains outside would fit the rule.

This is why the only belongs with our sufficient expressions: the complete group it introduces gives us the starting condition. We'll compare it with only in Group 2. Read the complete expression now so that the missing or added word doesn't quietly change your interpretation.

### Every: each qualifying case gets the result

**Every finalist receives an invitation.**

This gives finalist status the same sufficient job that all gave registered-guest status. Choose any person who is a finalist and the statement settles the invitation question for that person.

Prediction pause: **Someone receives an invitation. Have we established that they are a finalist?**

Reveal: No. Every finalist receives one, but the sentence hasn't restricted invitations to finalists. Write **Finalist → Receives invitation**. The direction comes from which people the statement covers, not from which condition appears most important.

### Any: keep the restriction with the person

**Any applicant who submits late pays a fee.**

Any covers each applicant who meets the stated restriction. That whole idea is enough for the fee: the person is an applicant and submits late. Applicant alone doesn't capture it.

Prediction pause: **Which label preserves the sufficient condition: “Applicant” or “Applicant submits late”?**

Reveal: Applicant submits late. Write **Applicant submits late → Pays fee**. “A fee is paid by any applicant who submits late” moves the target later without changing it. Locate what any refers to rather than treating sentence position as a placement instruction.

### Find the target, then place it

Now use the existing TranslationLab. Keep its two-stage decision: identify the complete target, then place it left. Explain: “Add what to the indicator when that gives you a sensible question. If what? All what? The only what? Your answer should be the whole idea the expression refers to. Then check whether that idea really is enough to establish the other condition.”

Keep the existing limiting-modifier check, the only comparison, fresh DiagramBuilder, seven fresh recall sentences, and final meaning check. Strengthen the practical diagram instruction: “If you know the meaning but hesitate over the arrow, imagine the left condition is true. Does the sentence then require the right condition? Read the whole relationship in English. If it makes a promise the original didn't make, change the direction.”

## Group 2: Find what is required

Hero subtitle: These expressions can identify a requirement. Find the complete idea the sentence says you need, then check what its presence does and does not prove.

Opening explanation: Group 1 helped us find a condition that is enough. Group 2 turns our attention to what must be true when the other condition holds. A requirement belongs on the right because the left condition cannot hold under the rule without it. Having the requirement, though, may leave the left condition unknown.

### Only: find the people the result is limited to

**Only members are admitted.**

Admission is limited to members. So if someone is admitted, the sentence tells us they're a member. Only refers to members, and membership has the necessary role.

Prediction pause: **Leon is a member. Does this sentence promise that Leon is admitted?**

Reveal: No. Membership is required, but a member could remain outside. Write **Admitted → Member**. This preserves the restriction on admission without adding a promise about every member.

Compare “The only people admitted are members.” That sentence also tells us every admitted person is a member. Its expression points to a different target, admitted people, while the full relationship remains the same. The only gives us the sufficient target; only gives us the necessary target. Read the whole sentence in both cases.

### Only if: a condition that must be met

**A student graduates only if the student completes the project.**

The student needs to complete the project for graduation. The sentence makes project completion a requirement; it hasn't made it a promise of graduation.

Prediction pause: **A student completed the project. Could the student still fail to graduate without contradicting this sentence?**

Reveal: Yes. The sentence hasn't told us that completion is the only requirement or that every student who completes the project graduates. Write **Graduates → Completes project**. The target of only if is the complete condition “completes the project,” which goes right.

Keep DirectionSwitch here. Introduce it with the meaning: “Watch what changes when only is added to if. A pass can be enough in one sentence and required in the other. Compare what each sentence lets you conclude from having a pass.” The visual remains an exploration after the explanation, not the first definition.

### Only when: an occasion that is required

**A review occurs only when a complaint is filed.**

The sentence restricts reviews to occasions with a filed complaint. Filing a complaint is necessary for a review to occur under the stated rule. It isn't stated enough to produce a review.

Prediction pause: **A complaint has been filed. Does that alone establish that a review occurs?**

Reveal: No. Write **Review occurs → Complaint filed**. A filed complaint with no review fits this statement. “Only when a complaint is filed does a review occur” moves the required condition to the front, but it keeps the same relationship.

### Only where: keep the requirement at the same place

**The service operates only where a station is available.**

The service needs a station wherever it operates. Only where points to station availability as the requirement. This differs from the Group 1 sentence, which made station availability enough for operation.

Prediction pause: **The service operates at South Square. What must be true there?**

Reveal: A station is available there. Write **Service operates at a location → Station available there**. The condition concerns that location. Finding a station somewhere else wouldn't satisfy what the statement requires at South Square.

### Always: identify what happens in every covered case

**A finalist always receives an invitation.**

Read the subject before deciding what always does. The sentence tells us that receiving an invitation holds in every case of someone being a finalist. That makes the invitation necessary for finalist status under this rule.

Prediction pause: **What is guaranteed here: an invitation for a finalist, or finalist status for anyone invited?**

Reveal: An invitation for a finalist. Write **Finalist → Receives invitation**. Always modifies the result, receives an invitation. It doesn't tell us that invitations go only to finalists. A familiar word helps us find the relationship only after we understand its job in this sentence.

### Requires: the target can appear before the word

**Entry requires a current pass.**

What does entry require? A current pass. That complete idea is necessary. A pass that has expired doesn't meet the condition the sentence gives us.

Prediction pause: **How would you diagram “A current pass is required for entry”?**

Reveal: **Enters → Has current pass**. The requirement appears before required, but its role hasn't changed. Read the whole sentence to locate what is required. Automatically taking the words after the indicator would point you toward entry and reverse the rule.

The current pass still doesn't guarantee entry. Someone could keep one in a drawer and never enter. Required means needed for the other condition, not enough to establish that it happened.

### Must: preserve an obligation as an obligation

**A finalist must attend the interview.**

Here must states what finalists are required to do. If someone is a finalist, that person has an obligation to attend. The sentence hasn't supplied a fact proving that every finalist complied.

Prediction pause: **Maya is a finalist. Which statement follows: she is required to attend, or she actually attended?**

Reveal: She is required to attend. Write **Finalist → Required to attend interview**. Actual attendance would need information about what Maya did. Keeping required to in the label preserves the difference between the obligation the rule states and a completed action.

The same caution applies when we shorten a condition. “Attends interview” looks tidy, but in this sentence it would change the logical strength. A readable label should preserve what was said.

### Find the requirement, then place it

Use the existing Group 2 TranslationLab after all seven explanations. Keep target-before-diagram feedback, current-pass check, controlled only/the only comparison, fresh DiagramBuilder, seven separate recall sentences, and final graduation meaning check. The whole-target sentence should read: “Ask what the indicator refers to. Only what? Only if what? What is required? The target can come before or after the expression, so check its meaning in the complete sentence.”

## Preserve the practice, clarify why it is here

Before each DiagramBuilder: You have seen the expression with help. Now use a new sentence. Identify both complete ideas, choose the sufficient one for the left, and check whether your diagram says what the sentence says. The wording changes; the job of the arrow stays the same.

Before each recall sequence: Read the new sentence and explain its direction before revealing. If you hesitate, ask what the indicator refers to, then try the proposed relationship in ordinary English. Compare your explanation with the one that appears.

After the final meaning check: The guided practice asks you to write your reasoning with the method beside you. Then the attached drill gives you fresh questions. Use the explanation to inspect the direction and complete conditions, not only whether you chose the same answer.
