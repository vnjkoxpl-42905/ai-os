## A guarantee gives you a direction

If a storage unit's alarm is armed, its status light is illuminated.

The sufficient condition is **the storage unit's alarm is armed**. That is enough to guarantee **its status light is illuminated**, the necessary condition.

**Alarm armed → status light illuminated.**

The rule does not say that an alarm is armed now. It tells you what follows if an alarm is armed. A separate fact, such as “Unit 12's alarm is armed,” lets you apply the rule to Unit 12. You can then conclude that Unit 12's status light is illuminated.

An illuminated light does not prove an armed alarm. The light could be illuminated in another way. An unarmed alarm does not prove an unilluminated light either. The rule gives no outcome for that starting situation. Both conclusions would add a direction the author did not give you.

For the rule **alarm armed → light illuminated**, compare these fully described cases:

| Case | Does it fit the rule? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Alarm armed; light illuminated | Allowed | The stated guarantee is satisfied. |
| Alarm armed; light not illuminated | Forbidden | The sufficient condition occurs without its required result. |
| Alarm not armed; light illuminated | Allowed | The rule does not prohibit other ways to illuminate the light. |
| Alarm not armed; light not illuminated | Allowed | The rule makes no positive demand when its trigger is absent. |

“Allowed” means consistent with the rule. It does not mean the case actually happened. When judging a conclusion from supplied information, use **Guaranteed**, **Ruled out** or **Unknown**. Unknown means the conclusion could be true or false while the supplied information stays true. This avoids treating “possible” as “proven.”

## Translate complete ideas before shortening them

Use these four steps:

1. Identify the phrase expressing the guarantee.
2. Write the complete condition that is sufficient. Include its subject, action, negatives and relevant modifiers.
3. Write the complete condition that follows. Keep the same person or object when the sentence concerns one subject.
4. Write **sufficient condition → necessary condition**, then read it as an ordinary if-then sentence. Compare that sentence with the original.

Short labels come after those complete ideas. For example, “a delivery vehicle is registered with the harbor office” may become **registered with harbor office**. It should not become just **vehicle**, because that loses the condition you need to satisfy.

### Worked example: a universal statement has the same direction

Every delivery vehicle registered with the harbor office displays a numbered permit.

The sufficient condition is **a delivery vehicle is registered with the harbor office**. The necessary condition is **that vehicle displays a numbered permit**.

**Delivery vehicle registered with harbor office → displays numbered permit.**

Read it back: if a delivery vehicle is registered with the harbor office, that vehicle displays a numbered permit. The complete subject survives. The statement does not say that every delivery vehicle displays a permit. It also does not say that every vehicle displaying a permit is registered with that office.

### Guided attempt: clause order does not decide direction

A status report is sent whenever a sensor restarts.

1. Which complete idea follows whenever?
2. What does the statement guarantee from that idea?
3. Write the arrow and read it back in English.

**Optional hint:** the sufficient condition can appear second in the sentence. Start with the idea introduced by whenever.

**Answer:** **Sensor restarts → status report sent.** Whenever the sensor restarts, a report is sent. A sent report alone does not establish that the sensor restarted.

### Guided attempt: keep a modifier inside the subject

Any membership application submitted through the evening service receives a confirmation number.

1. Is the subject every application, or a restricted group of applications?
2. Write that complete sufficient condition.
3. State what follows about that same application.

**Optional hint:** include how the application was submitted before putting it left of the arrow.

**Answer:** **Membership application submitted through evening service → receives confirmation number.** The rule gives no guarantee for an application submitted through another service.

## Read the sentence before trusting an indicator list

Group 1 commonly includes if, whenever, when, where, every, all, any and the only. Guarantee verbs can express the same direction. A list helps you recognize familiar wording; the sentence determines whether a guarantee is present and what its conditions are.

- **When** can report a single event: “When I arrived on Tuesday, the lights were off.” That sentence does not establish a general arrival-to-darkness rule.
- **Where** can identify a location rather than a guarantee. Ask what the sentence actually commits the author to.
- **If** can appear in an indirect question: “We asked if the certificate was valid.” That does not give a conditional rule.
- **The only** needs careful parsing. “The only applications accepted are applications with a signed declaration” gives **application accepted → has signed declaration**. It does not make a declaration sufficient for acceptance. Do not assign a direction merely to the next isolated noun.

Group 2 develops requirement language such as only if and requires. Groups 3 and 4 develop translation that changes a condition's negation. This Group 1 package can compare directions, but it does not claim to teach those groups' procedures. Negatives already written inside a Group 1 condition must still be preserved.

## Use harder Group 1 language after its prerequisites

Medium adds longer predicates, clause placement, simple existing negation and contextual indicator checks. It does not require contraposition, compound connective rules or chains.

Advanced returns to sufficient-condition language with explicit dependencies:

- **A1:** joint AND antecedents. Teach first that both conditions are required together; one alone cannot trigger the rule.
- **A2:** inclusive OR antecedents. Teach first that either branch can satisfy that antecedent; both are also permitted unless the statement excludes them.
- **A3:** complete modifier scope. No AND/OR notation or contraposition required if the whole restricted subject remains one predicate.
- **A4:** contraposition. Teach first that failure of the necessary condition excludes the sufficient condition. Keep the person and complete predicates consistent.
- **A5:** rule versus existence. Teach first that a universal rule does not establish that any qualifying case exists.
- **A6:** direct chaining and fact application. Teach first that a rule's result must satisfy the next rule's trigger. Linking rules alone does not establish a case; the supplied fact activates the chain here.

Assign these items only after the named dependency has been taught. They are harder applications of Group 1 language, not a substitute for those lessons.

## Beginner: six independent attempts

### B1. Build the rule from every

**Task:** Every inventory tag printed in orange is included in the daily inspection list. Write the complete sufficient and necessary conditions, then diagram the rule.

**Answer:** **Inventory tag printed in orange → included in daily inspection list.**

**Explanation:** The guarantee applies to tags printed in orange. It does not apply to every inventory tag, and it does not tell you the color of other listed tags.

**Optional hint:** include “printed in orange” in the left condition.

### B2. Build the rule when if comes second

**Task:** A parcel is released from the archive if its pickup receipt is signed. Diagram the rule without changing either predicate.

**Answer:** **Parcel's pickup receipt signed → parcel released from archive.**

**Explanation:** The if-clause identifies what is sufficient, even though it comes second. Release is the guaranteed result.

**Optional hint:** begin with the receipt condition, not the first clause on the page.

### B3. Classify complete cases

**Task:** If an access code is accepted, the green light is on. Mark each case Allowed or Forbidden: (a) accepted code, green light on; (b) accepted code, green light off; (c) code not accepted, green light on; (d) code not accepted, green light off.

**Answer:** (a) Allowed; (b) Forbidden; (c) Allowed; (d) Allowed.

**Explanation:** Only (b) supplies the sufficient condition while denying its required result. The rule gives no required outcome when a code is not accepted. Allowed cases are possible, not necessarily actual.

**Optional hint:** find the case where the trigger occurs but its result does not.

### B4. Separate a result from proof of its trigger

**Task:** Every document entered in the tracking register receives a reference number. Document R has received a reference number. Is “Document R was entered in the tracking register” Guaranteed, Ruled out or Unknown?

**Answer:** Unknown.

**Explanation:** Entry guarantees a number. Having a number does not establish entry. Document R could have received its number through another process, or through this one.

**Optional hint:** compare the supplied fact with the right side of your arrow.

### B5. Repair an inference from an absent trigger

**Task:** If a volunteer attends the briefing, the volunteer receives a printed guide. Neri did not attend the briefing. A student concludes, “Neri did not receive a printed guide.” Repair the student's conclusion.

**Answer:** Whether Neri received a printed guide is Unknown.

**Explanation:** Attendance is one sufficient way to receive a guide. The rule does not prohibit giving a guide to someone who did not attend. Negating the sufficient condition does not establish a negated result.

**Optional hint:** does the rule say attendance is the only way to receive a guide?

### B6. Apply the rule to its matching fact

**Task:** Any subscriber whose account is active receives the weekly digest. Bela is a subscriber whose account is active. State the guaranteed conclusion about Bela and identify why it follows.

**Answer:** Bela receives the weekly digest.

**Explanation:** Bela satisfies the complete sufficient condition: subscriber with an active account. Apply the guarantee to that same person. The rule by itself would not establish that Bela's account is active; the separate fact does.

**Optional hint:** match Bela's fact to the entire subject described by any.

## Medium: six attempts with more demanding language

### M1. Preserve the restricted subject

**Task:** Every research report using confidential survey data undergoes an ethics review. Report S uses public survey data; it uses no confidential survey data. Is “Report S undergoes an ethics review” Guaranteed, Ruled out or Unknown? Write the rule before answering.

**Answer:** **Research report using confidential survey data → undergoes ethics review.** The conclusion is Unknown.

**Explanation:** Report S does not satisfy this trigger. That fails to prove a review, but it also fails to rule a review out. Another policy could require one.

**Optional hint:** do not shorten the left side to “research report.”

### M2. Interpret the only as a complete sentence

**Task:** The only records accepted by the licensing office are digitally signed records. Diagram the relationship. Does a digital signature alone prove acceptance?

**Answer:** **Record accepted by licensing office → digitally signed.** A digital signature alone does not prove acceptance.

**Explanation:** The sentence restricts the accepted records to digitally signed records. It does not say every signed record is accepted.

**Optional hint:** start with a record that the office has accepted. What must be true of it?

### M3. Keep the deadline inside the predicate

**Task:** A completion certificate is emailed whenever a participant finishes the course before registration closes. Diagram the rule. Davi finishes the course after registration closes. Does the rule guarantee that Davi will not be emailed a completion certificate?

**Answer:** **Participant finishes course before registration closes → participant emailed completion certificate.** No; the proposed negative conclusion is unsupported.

**Explanation:** Finishing before registration closes is sufficient for an emailed certificate. Davi does not satisfy that complete trigger. The rule does not prohibit a certificate being emailed to someone who finishes later.

**Optional hint:** include the deadline in the sufficient condition before matching Davi's fact.

### M4. Preserve a negative already in the trigger

**Task:** If a permit has not expired, its holder may enter the exhibit. The permit held by Ivo has not expired. What follows? Does the rule also tell you what follows for an expired permit?

**Answer:** Ivo may enter the exhibit. For an expired permit, permission to enter is Unknown from this rule alone.

**Explanation:** The complete sufficient condition includes not expired. Ivo meets it. An expired permit negates that trigger, which does not prove lack of permission. “May enter” means permission, not proof of actual entry.

**Optional hint:** distinguish permission to enter from the act of entering.

### M5. Repair the direction of a guarantee verb

**Task:** The policy says that completing the identity check guarantees issuance of a visitor pass. A student diagrams **Visitor pass issued → identity check completed**. Repair the diagram and explain why the first version added information.

**Answer:** **Identity check completed → visitor pass issued.**

**Explanation:** Completing the check is sufficient for issuance. The reversed arrow would claim that every issued pass proves a completed check, excluding other possible ways to obtain a pass that this policy does not exclude.

**Optional hint:** ask which event the policy says is enough for the other event.

### M6. Distinguish a past event from a general guarantee

**Task:** (a) When I visited the records room on Monday, its service window was closed. (b) Whenever the records room's alarm is being tested, its service window is closed. Which sentence supplies a general conditional rule? Diagram that rule. Does it connect visits with closures?

**Answer:** Sentence (b): **Records room's alarm being tested → its service window closed.** It does not connect visits with closures.

**Explanation:** Sentence (a) reports what happened during one visit. Sentence (b) guarantees closure under the stated testing condition. The occurrence of when in a sentence does not automatically establish a universal rule.

**Optional hint:** look for a commitment about every qualifying occasion, rather than one dated occasion.

## Advanced: six attempts after the named prerequisites

### A1. Retain a joint trigger

**Prerequisite:** AND antecedents.

**Task:** If a proposal is filed before noon and carries the supervisor's signature, it enters today's review queue. Proposal T was filed before noon. Is “Proposal T enters today's review queue” Guaranteed, Ruled out or Unknown? Explain which condition is missing from the supplied facts.

**Answer:** Unknown. Whether Proposal T carries the supervisor's signature is not supplied.

**Explanation:** **Filed before noon AND carries supervisor's signature → enters today's queue.** The fact establishes only one part of the joint trigger. It does not establish the guarantee's prerequisites together. This also does not rule out queue entry through another rule.

**Optional hint:** underline both conditions inside the if-clause before matching the fact.

### A2. Use either sufficient branch without adding exclusivity

**Prerequisite:** inclusive OR antecedents.

**Task:** Any consignment arriving by rail or by ferry receives a transit code. Consignment U arrives by ferry. State what is guaranteed. Does the rule prohibit U from also arriving by rail as part of its journey?

**Answer:** U receives a transit code. The rule does not prohibit it from also arriving by rail.

**Explanation:** **Arrives by rail OR arrives by ferry → receives transit code.** The ferry branch satisfies the sufficient condition. Inclusive or allows either branch or both; the sentence gives no exclusivity requirement.

**Optional hint:** one stated alternative can satisfy an inclusive-or trigger. Do not add “but not both.”

### A3. Keep two modifiers attached to the same object

**Prerequisite:** complete modifier scope; no compound notation required.

**Task:** Every report released outside the agency without redactions receives a public-disclosure audit. Report V is released outside the agency, but it contains redactions. A student shortens the rule to **Released outside agency → receives audit**. Repair the shortened condition and judge whether V's audit is guaranteed.

**Answer:** **Report released outside agency without redactions → receives public-disclosure audit.** An audit of V is not guaranteed; its audit status is Unknown.

**Explanation:** The sufficient description contains both modifiers. V does not satisfy the complete description because it contains redactions. The shortened rule wrongly applies to a larger group of reports. The original still permits V to receive an audit for another reason.

**Optional hint:** keep “without redactions” inside the description of the reports covered. Here it modifies the report's release, rather than supplying a separate Group 3 sentence to translate.

### A4. Apply a contrapositive to complete predicates

**Prerequisite:** contraposition.

**Task:** Every technician certified for the pressure chamber has completed the current safety course. Mako has not completed the current safety course. What follows about Mako? State the original rule before using its contrapositive.

**Answer:** **Technician certified for pressure chamber → completed current safety course.** Mako is not a technician certified for the pressure chamber.

**Explanation:** **Not completed current safety course → not a technician certified for pressure chamber.** The fact denies the necessary condition and excludes the complete sufficient condition. It does not prove that Mako is not a technician at all or has never completed a different safety course.

**Optional hint:** negate the entire certification description, not just the word technician.

### A5. Avoid inventing an existing case

**Prerequisite:** rule versus existence.

**Task:** All permits issued by the coastal office this morning bear a blue seal. No other information is supplied. Which conclusion is guaranteed: (a) at least one blue-sealed permit exists; (b) if a permit was issued by that office this morning, it bears a blue seal; (c) every blue-sealed permit was issued by that office this morning?

**Answer:** Only (b).

**Explanation:** The rule gives **Permit issued by coastal office this morning → bears blue seal**. It does not establish that any permit was issued, so it does not prove (a). It also does not reverse the implication, so it does not prove (c).

**Optional hint:** distinguish a rule describing qualifying permits from a fact that a qualifying permit exists.

### A6. Transfer Group 1 translation into a short argument

**Prerequisite:** direct chaining and fact application.

**Task:** Everyone who completes the archive orientation is assigned a reader badge. Every person assigned a reader badge receives a locker code. Sol has completed the archive orientation. A student concludes that Sol receives a locker code and that everyone receiving a locker code has completed the orientation. Evaluate the two conclusions separately.

**Answer:** Sol receives a locker code is Guaranteed. The universal claim about everyone receiving a locker code is not established.

**Explanation:** **Completes archive orientation → assigned reader badge → receives locker code.** Sol's fact activates that chain. The second conclusion reverses the linked rule. The premises do not exclude other ways to receive a locker code.

**Optional hint:** trace Sol forward from the supplied fact, then check whether the second conclusion tries to travel backward.
