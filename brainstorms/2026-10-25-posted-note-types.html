What we did on Thursday

In order

What an argument is. A premise and a conclusion. That is all of it. Background, opposing views, explanation: none of that is the argument, and once you can find the two real pieces you can read every stimulus on the test the same way.

What makes something a conclusion. It has to be an opinion. A fact is handed to you as true and there is nothing left to argue about. A conclusion is where the author says what follows, or what ought to be done, and that is the only place a gap can live.

What validity means on this test. Not the everyday sense of the word. It means a guarantee: if the premises are true, the conclusion has to be true. Being from Florida against being in Florida was the case we used. The conclusion had quietly helped itself to something the premise never gave it.

How to build an objection. You do not fight the evidence. You accept it, then invent a specific situation where the evidence still holds and the conclusion fails anyway. That one move is what answers flaw questions, weakening questions and necessary assumption questions. Only what you do with the answer changes.

The political speeches question. You got to D by arguing that a selfish reason for making a promise does not make the promise unreliable. Wanting to be reelected is itself a reason to keep it.

Writing the attacks down. You named the real difficulty yourself: two answers left and no way to separate them. The fix is physical. On paper, one bullet per surviving answer, the specific reason that answer fails. A vague suspicion does not survive being written out as a sentence. That is what turned B into a dead answer instead of a maybe.

The irrigation question. You isolated the conclusion, that modern societies relying on irrigation will collapse the way Sumeria did, and then built the objection yourself: modern irrigation may use methods that avoid the salt buildup. B did not disprove that irrigation caused the collapse. D was about societies the argument was never talking about. E was about the ancient case, not the modern claim. C said your own objection back to you, which is what a correct weakening answer usually does.

What changed

You came in reading an argument as the issue being presented. By the second question you were pulling the conclusion out first, accepting the evidence, and building a counterexample against the jump before you looked at a single answer choice. Doing it in that order is what stops a well written wrong answer from getting to you, because you already know what you are looking for when you arrive.

Both questions turned on the same move, and it is worth carrying: something was true of one case, and the author assumed it was true of every case.

Carry forward

  • Say the conclusion back to yourself in one compact sentence before you do anything else.
  • Accept the premises as given. Attack the jump, never the evidence.
  • Write out the attack on every answer still standing, one bullet each, before you choose.
  • Work without a clock running until you are getting them right consistently. Speed follows accuracy, and a question missed quickly is worth nothing.
  • Practise restating an argument compactly and designing an objection to it, including on arguments nobody is testing you on.

Assumption Family (Medium) Set 1

10 questions. Negation practice.

Every question here is an assumption question, and all ten were chosen because Too Strong is the trap that catches most people on them. That is the rule from our session: soft language is easy for an author to be committed to, so it is easy for an argument to require.

Run both steps, every time:

  1. If this were true, does the argument get stronger?
  2. Negate it. Does the conclusion collapse, or does it only wobble?

Remember that negating means stepping outside the category, not flipping to the opposite. And when you are down to two, ask which one is the safer claim.

Your questions are fixed for this one.

Explain Curious Fact (Medium) Set 2

20 questions. This is the bulk of the work.

Phenomenon questions were the third thing we flagged. The pattern is always the same: you are handed a result, and you need the answer that actually accounts for it.

The trap that dominates this set is No Impact. A choice sits next to the topic, sounds informed, and changes nothing about why the result happened. That is the same test as an objection answer in the flaw manual: assume it is true, then ask whether it does real work.

One habit for this set. Before you look at the choices, say out loud what needs explaining, in one sentence. If you cannot say it, you are not ready to judge an answer.

This drill draws a fresh set each time you open it, so it is the one to repeat.

Famous Flaws (Medium) Set 1

10 questions. Work them untimed first.

This set is the drill for the three kinds of flaw answer choice. Before you evaluate any option, name what kind it is: an assumption the author was standing on, an objection from outside, or a description of what the argument did.

Nine of these ten were chosen because their most-tempting wrong answer is either Too Strong or Not An Objection. That second one is exactly the confusion we worked on. A choice that reads like an attack, in a slot where the question is asking what the author assumed, is the trap.

For every question, write one line: which kind was the credited answer, and which kind did the loser pretend to be.

Parallel Reasoning
Covers Mode selection · the two tags · pre-phrasing · one striking feature · the features and indicator lists · both-directions matching · or/and under contraposition · Parallel Flaw
Use it for Working a set, and reviewing one. The lists in sections 5 and 6 are the reference you keep open while drilling.

1. Pick your mode before you read

The decision most people skip, and it costs more than any single rule.

Diagram mode. Conditional words or quantity words in the stimulus. These convert cleanly into something close to algebra, and holding them conversationally is how you end up rereading.

Conversational mode. No conditional logic at all. A rebuttal, a warning that a sample is unrepresentative, an if-it-was-true-yesterday shape. Do not force notation onto these. Naming what the author is doing in one plain sentence is faster and more reliable than notating an argument that was never built for it.

Decide while you read the first sentence. Forcing the wrong mode is the most common way to lose two minutes here.

2. Eliminate, do not confirm

You are not hunting for the right answer. You are removing answers as fast as they can be removed, and the last one standing wins.

The answers are hollow, which is what makes this possible. Each one is a simple, one level argument padded out to look as dense as the stimulus. You are not meant to read it. You are meant to poke at the foundational stilts.

The moment an answer breaks one requirement, stop reading it. Not at the end of the sentence, not after you have been fair to it. One fatal mismatch is the whole verdict, and finishing the choice is time you have set on fire. You can always come back if nothing survives.

3. Tag the conclusion

Type. Fact, conditional, causal, comparative, quantity, or prescriptive. Strength. Certain (must, will, guarantees), probable (likely, usually, generally), or possible (can, may, might, some, at least partially).

Exactly one from each list. Not roughly, exactly, because "whenever" means all and "can" means possible, and swapping either changes what you are matching on.

4. Pre-phrase before you look down

Say what the correct conclusion has to sound like, in your own words, before reading a single answer.

Not "something similar." Something specific: a conditional with an "or" in the outcome. Or a moderately strong rebuttal, nothing as strong as "could never be."

When you know what you are looking for, a deviation announces itself. When you do not, you end up letting each answer argue its own case.

5. Find one striking feature. One.

One thing, usually in the conclusion, that you can hold in your hand as a guiding light. Name it in a phrase. Five seconds. Then scan the five answers for that one feature.

The limit, and it matters. This only works when the conclusion has distinctive vocabulary to call out. A conditional with an "or" in the outcome is a gift. A flat statement of fact gives you nothing to scan for, and the right response is to stop hunting and go match premises instead. Do not burn thirty seconds looking for a feature that is not there.

Feature What to watch for
Conditional if, only if, unless, whenever, requires
Causal causes, leads to, results in, produces, responsible for
Comparative more, less, higher, older, greater than
Quantity all, most, some, none, every, not all
Strength must, will, probably, likely, can, could
Pivot but, yet, however
Connective and, either/or, neither/nor
Prescriptive should, ought to

6. The indicator words
Premise (FABS) For · After all · Because · Since
Conclusion so · thus · hence · therefore · it follows · as a result · consequently · this shows that
Pivot but · yet · however
Opposing viewpoint some people say · it is commonly believed · some economists believe

FABS always has a premise attached, which is what makes it the most reliable set. Point at a premise and the conclusion is usually right beside it.

Punctuation is working too. A comma tells you a complete thought just ended, so when a premise indicator opens a clause and that clause closes with a comma, what follows is usually your conclusion.

"And" conjoins premises. It will never be in a conclusion. If you think you have found the conclusion and it has an "and" holding it together, you have found a premise.

An opposing-viewpoint phrase means a pivot is coming. The author sets up a position in order to push back on it, and the pushback is where the conclusion lives.

7. It runs both ways

A feature in the stimulus must appear in the answer. And a feature in the answer that the stimulus never had also kills it. Adding is as fatal as missing.

Take that seriously here, because this is one of the types that punishes imported information. On Strengthen or Weaken you get rewarded for supplying an outside idea. Here you get your hand slapped for it.

Two refinements:

Match polarity, not just direction. If the stimulus runs through negated terms, the answer runs through negated terms.

Order does not matter. Premises can arrive in any sequence. You need the same ingredients, not the same arrangement.

8. "Or" and "and" decide more of these than you expect

Where the connective sits changes the structure: an "or" in the trigger is not the same as an "or" in the outcome.

And under contraposition, "or" becomes "and" and "and" becomes "or." Get them right going in or the contrapositive comes out wrong.

Which leads to the disguise. The correct answer often states the chained inference in contrapositive form, so it reads as a mismatch when it is an exact match. "Unless" is the usual tell, because unless translates to "if not."

So when an answer looks structurally off but you cannot name the rule it broke, flip it before you eliminate it. If A then B is the same claim as if not B then not A, and they are counting on that not feeling true at speed.

9. The five ways an answer dies

Your elimination checklist, roughly in order of how often each fires.

What it means
Wrong reasoning type The stimulus runs on comparison, the answer runs on conditional logic, or the reverse
Wrong conclusion Wrong claim type, wrong strength, or an idea that never appeared in the evidence
Wrong premise A missing premise, an extra one, or one of the wrong type
Wrong validity A valid argument offered to parallel a flawed one, or the reverse
Wrong flaw Flawed, but the wrong error. Parallel Flaw only

Wrong reasoning type is your fastest kill. Comparison against conditionals, and you are done without understanding either argument in detail.

Wrong validity is worth its own beat, because this is the rare family where the stimulus is sometimes sound. When it is, that becomes a requirement in both directions: a valid stimulus needs a valid answer, a flawed one needs a flawed answer. No negotiating.

10. Parallel Flaw

You are matching the mistake, not the skeleton. So naming the error stops being optional. Say it in one sentence before you look down, because if you cannot say it, you cannot match it.

Most of these run on conditional logic. Comparison carries far more weight here than on ordinary Parallel, so expect it. Causal is rare.

Wrong flaw is where these are won and lost. An answer can be genuinely broken and still be wrong, because it is broken differently:

  • Reversed. Affirm the necessary, conclude the sufficient. If A then B. B. Therefore A.
  • Negated. Deny the sufficient, conclude the denial of the necessary. If A then B. Not A. Therefore not B.

Both are errors. Only one is your error. Treating "commits a conditional mistake" as good enough will pick the wrong answer on exactly this.

One comparative archetype worth recognising on sight. Two things sit at opposite ends of a spectrum, they get combined, and the argument concludes the result lands in the middle. The match has to blend two opposites and land between them. An answer where both groups share the same outcome is a wrong-premise failure, not a match.

At the hardest level, the flaw outranks the skeleton. On a level five the structures will not line up one for one, and the answer committing the same error wins even when its shape is imperfect.

11. Abstract when the content gets heavy

Strip the proper nouns, put letters in their place. Cut it to A and B, or X and Y, and the relationship shows up almost immediately.

With conditionals this is close to algebra. Underline the conditional indicators, label the repeating terms A, B, C, find the argument indicator to locate the conclusion. Watch the repeating concepts especially, because the term appearing twice is usually the hinge.

If you catch yourself reading a sentence a third time because the content is heavy, that is the cue to abstract. Not to read it again.

12. Two moves for the clock

If an early answer matches cleanly, take it. Pick it, flag it, move on. Come back at the end if you have time. The better your pre-phrase was, the safer this is, because you are not gambling on a hunch, you are trusting a structure you already derived.

You are allowed to lose one. If you have spent your minute on a level five and it has not resolved, take your best elimination and go. Do not sacrifice the forest for the trees. These are the most time-expensive questions on the section, and one is worth exactly as much as a question you can do in forty seconds.

All of this is annoying to do up front. It really is. Tagging a conclusion while the clock runs feels like spending the one thing you do not have.

But the few seconds it costs buy back a full reread of the stimulus plus partial rereads of all five answers, and that is the trade every time. Do it for a week and it stops being a checklist. It becomes how you see the question.

REMEMBER: Pick your mode, tag the conclusion, pre-phrase what the answer must sound like. Then run the five ways an answer dies, and the instant one fires, stop reading and move.
