Most Strongly Supported

Keep this open beside a Most Strongly Supported set.

What you're looking for

Information, then a question about what that information supports best. It is never an argument. Think of it as a Must Be True question with a lighter touch: the answer doesn't have to be guaranteed. It has to be the most supported one, and it works like the conclusion the information reaches.

The steps

1 · Expect sentences, not an argument. You won't be asked to find a conclusion or judge reasoning. You'll be asked what the sentences support.

2 · Name each claim. Conditional, causal, correlation, quantity, or a should claim. Mark any pivot (but, yet, however). Mark any and or or claim too. They change what you need to focus on.

3 · See how the claims fit. A repeated idea means a link. Cause and effect claims may chain. If nothing chains, find what the sentences share, and expect the answer to stay inside it.

4 · Predict the range. Put yourself in the situation. What do you know? What don't you know? What range of answers is possible? Do this before you read the choices.

5 · Weigh each answer. Check its strength and its exact words against what the stimulus said.

6 · Take the answer with the fewest problems. It won't be perfect.

What the claims are, and what the answer usually does

The claims are The right answer usually
Rules that link Joins two ideas that sit far apart, or its contrapositive
A rule plus a fact Applies the rule to the fact
A causal chain Plays with the chain: the first idea makes the last more likely, or seeing the last is some evidence of the first
Several causal claims that don't chain Stays inside what they share
A pivot Settles the tension between the ideas on either side

When the claims are rules

Two rules that link. Every sourdough loaf is baked in the stone oven. Every loaf baked in the stone oven sells out by noon. The stone oven is the idea in the middle, and it won't be in the answer. The answer joins the ends: every sourdough loaf sells out by noon, or a loaf that doesn't sell out by noon isn't sourdough.

The ends sit far apart. The test often puts one end early in the stimulus and the other near the end, buried in extra detail. Look for the idea that repeats. The ideas that appear once are usually your two ends.

Not every pair links. Every sourdough loaf is baked in the stone oven. Every rye loaf is baked in the stone oven. Both rules end in the same place, and that links nothing. Don't force a chain.

A rule plus a fact. Find the rule, write its contrapositive, then apply the fact. Take: everyone on the guest list gets a wristband.

You learn You can say
Dana is on the guest list Dana has a wristband
Dana has a wristband Nothing about the list
Dana isn't on the list Nothing about the wristband
Dana has no wristband Dana isn't on the list

The second and third rows are the bait. The test hopes you'll see something that isn't there.

Causal chains: a dial, not a switch

A rule is a hard line. A causal chain is a dial, like a volume knob. Turn it up and the volume rises. Turn it down and it falls.

If A conditional says A causal chain says
The first idea is absent Nothing about the last The last is less likely
The last idea is present Nothing about the first The first may be the reason

If the effect keeps showing up when the cause is absent, doubt that it was the cause.

Strength: the weightlifter

The stimulus is the lifter. Each answer is a weight. The heavier the weight, the less likely the lift.

Wording Examples
Soft can, may, some, helps, tends to, detracts
Strong inevitably, always, never, all, must

A strong word needs the stimulus to say something just as strong. Inevitably means every time, with no exceptions. A soft word still has to be about what the stimulus said. And a soft word asks for very little: detracts only needs a small dip.

Things that will fool you

A different idea that sounds close. Pulling attention away is not the same as losing it. Helping is not guaranteeing. The stimulus is your whole data set, so check each key word against it.

An answer that skips a detail. That isn't fatal. Ask whether the stimulus pushes you toward this answer more than toward any other.

Waiting for a perfect answer. There isn't one. It's the most supported answer, not a proven one.
