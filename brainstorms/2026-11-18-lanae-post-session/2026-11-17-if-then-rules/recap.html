Session recap · November 17

    Reading an
    If-Then Rule

    A conditional is a rule: if one thing is true, another thing has to be true. The LSAT writes the same rule many ways, with if, when, every, only. Most students try to memorize a separate trick for each word. A steadier way is to turn every version into one sentence, if this, then that, and then ask only what that sentence lets you conclude.

    THE RULEIf this, then that
    STEP 2Put each idea on its side
    STEP 1Which word starts the rule?
    What it says

    A conditional is a rule about what would follow

    A conditional is a hypothetical rule. It doesn't say something is true. It says that if one thing were true, another thing would have to be. A pool might have this rule: if the pool is open, a lifeguard must be on duty.

    Pool is open → lifeguard is on duty

    Read the arrow from left to right, never the other way. The left side of the arrow is the sufficient condition, the idea that is enough on its own. The right side is the necessary condition, which is what you can guarantee will occur if the sufficient condition happens. In the "if" sentence, the "if" half goes on the left and the "then" half goes on the right.

    Sufficient

    Enough. If this is true, you need nothing else to know the right side is true.

    Looks like The pool is open.
    TestIs this enough on its own?
    Necessary

    What has to be true whenever the left side is true.

    Looks like A lifeguard is on duty.
    TestDoes the left side force this?

    When you write the rule down, use full words. "Pool open" is easy to read tomorrow. A letter or two is not.

    What you know

    The rule settles two situations and says nothing about the other two

    When you're given a fact, find the idea in the rule and follow the arrow. If there's no arrow leaving it, you've reached the end of the line and the rule tells you nothing more. The contrapositive is the same rule said another way: flip the sides and negate both. It's logically equivalent to the rule, so you can use either one.

    You're toldThen you know
    The pool is open.A lifeguard is on duty.
    No lifeguard is on duty.The pool isn't open.
    The pool isn't open.Nothing new about the lifeguard.
    A lifeguard is on duty.Nothing new about whether the pool is open.

    The second row is the contrapositive at work: no lifeguard on duty, so the pool can't be open. The last two rows are where the rule goes quiet. A lifeguard might be on duty for training while the pool is closed.

    The trap that costs the most Reading the rule backward. The LSAT will say a lifeguard was on duty at noon, so the pool must have been open. You can't conclude that. A lifeguard on duty is the right side of the arrow, and nothing points back to the left.
    On the Nebraska rule, you named the US as the necessary side, which is exactly why being in the US tells you nothing about Nebraska. Then you got all four snow and cold situations right, including the two where the rule says nothing. Those two are what the LSAT builds its wrong answers on.
    Group 1

    If, when, all, every, and any start the left side

    Some words introduce the sufficient idea. Whatever idea comes right after one of them goes on the left side of the arrow.

    ifwhenalleveryany

    These all say the same rule. If the pool is open, a lifeguard is on duty. When the pool is open, a lifeguard is on duty. Every open pool has a lifeguard on duty. In each one, "the pool is open" is enough, so it goes on the left.

    Learn these words well enough that you don't have to think about them. They take practice to remember, and the boot camp gives you that practice apart from the questions.

    Read the sentence before you trust the word These words don't always start a rule. "I asked if the pool was open" doesn't. Check that the sentence is actually saying what would follow.
    Group 2

    Only points to the right side

    A different set of words introduces the necessary idea. Whatever follows one of them goes on the right side of the arrow, and the other idea goes on the left.

    onlyonly ifonly when

    Take only members can swim after six. "Members" follows "only," so it goes on the right. The other idea, swimming after six, goes on the left.

    1. Find the word. It's "only."
    2. Put the idea after it on the right side. Members.
    3. Put the other idea on the left side. Swims after six.
    4. Say it as if-then. If you swim after six, you're a member.
    5. Find the contrapositive from there. If you're not a member, you don't swim after six.

    Think of it like exchanging money. "Only" is the currency you arrived with. Swap it for if-then, and from then on you spend if-then.

    Drop the word once it's an if-then The contrapositive of "if you swim after six, you're a member" is "if you're not a member, you don't swim after six." It doesn't say "not only members." Once the rule is in if-then form, "only" has done its job.
    You asked whether the necessary side flips to the left when a sentence starts with "only." That's the right question, and the answer is no. The right side is always the necessary side. What changes is which idea goes there.

    Only residents can park on Elm Street after nine.

    If you're a resident, you can park on Elm Street after nine.Not provenNot proven. This puts resident on the left, but "only" points it to the right side. Residents may have other rules to follow.
    If you park on Elm Street after nine, you're a resident.FollowsFollows. Parking after nine is the left side and resident is the right side.
