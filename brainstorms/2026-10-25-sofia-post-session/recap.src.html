What the Argument
Cannot Lose

Keep the author’s exact claim in view. Then test which unstated idea its support cannot do without. The answer can be required without being enough to prove the conclusion.

Start here

What does the argument need?

A necessary assumption is an unstated idea the argument depends on. Without it, the author's evidence no longer supports the conclusion in the way the author needs.

That doesn't mean the conclusion becomes false. Someone could reach a true conclusion with a broken argument. Our job is to test this argument's support.

Keep the task preciseAsk, “Could the author keep this reasoning if this answer were false?” An answer that merely helps is not enough.
Two ways an answer can help

Connect the ideas. Rule out the problem.

Supply a missing connection

A café has longer opening hours. The author predicts more sales. The argument needs some way for those extra hours to produce sales.

Rule out a damaging possibility

A shop plans to cut delivery costs by changing couriers. The argument needs the new courier not to add fees large enough to wipe out the savings.

These are two ways an assumption can work. An answer can connect what is given to what is claimed, or deny a possibility that would undermine that connection. The wording alone does not tell you which job it does.

Keep the original strength

A factor is not a requirement

“The awning helps keep the pavement dry” says the awning contributes. It does not say dry pavement is impossible without an awning. A rain-free day could leave it dry too.

Contributes

Helps produce an outcome. Other ways may exist.

Is required

Without it, the outcome cannot happen.

Keep that distinction while rephrasing the conclusion. If you turn “helps” into “is required,” you'll begin looking for an answer to a stronger argument.

Test the finalist

Negate the whole answer

Suppose an author says, “Use the courtyard for lunch because it has enough tables for our group.” One required idea is that the group can actually use those tables at lunchtime.

AnswerThe tables are not all reserved for another group at lunchtime.
→
NegationAll the tables are reserved for another group at lunchtime.

Under that negation, having enough tables no longer gives this group a reason to choose the courtyard. The tables cannot serve the purpose the author gives them.

Here, removing “not” negates the answer correctly. Always check the whole meaning. Don't delete a word mechanically from a sentence with quantities or several clauses.

The trap“Not” is not a correctness signal. A positive answer can be necessary, and a negative answer can be irrelevant. What matters is what happens to the reasoning after the answer is negated.
Keep the same subject

General information is not a fact about this group

“Extra opening hours can increase sales” gives a general possibility. “These three cafés increased their sales” makes a claim about particular cafés. To connect them, the author needs relevant information about those cafés.

Likewise, a change can happen during a period without producing a different final total. Spend $10 and receive $10: the balance ends where it began. Don't turn “something happened” into “the total must have changed.”

Say the answer first

Three checks before you reveal

A museum should extend its hours because visitors want more evening access. Must every visitor want evening access?

No. The recommendation could have support without every visitor wanting it. “Every” adds a claim the argument does not need.

A collection gained five books and lost five books. Must its total have increased?

No. The gains and losses can cancel. Activity is not the same as a net increase.

The argument says a new chair will reduce discomfort. A finalist says the chair is not too large for the desk. What should you test?

Assume the chair is too large for the desk. Then ask whether that fact defeats the particular reason offered for using the chair. A nearby inconvenience alone does not automatically defeat the reasoning. You need a connection to what the argument claims.

When two answers remain

Keep this order

  1. Rephrase the conclusion without changing its group, strength or recommendation.
  2. Name the evidence. What reason is supposed to support that conclusion?
  3. Explain what each finalist supplies or rules out.
  4. Negate the complete answer. Keep the evidence fixed and describe the damage to its support.
