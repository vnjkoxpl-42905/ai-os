# Necessary Assumption

A necessary assumption is an idea the author has to agree with and never says. The trap is an answer the author only might agree with. Take the answer away and see whether the conclusion still stands.

## An assumption is a premise nobody wrote down

In everyday talk, to assume means to guess. On the LSAT it means a premise the author needs and never states. Here's an argument.

> **Evidence:** You want to go to a top law school.
> **Conclusion:** You should apply to USC.

1. **Find the conclusion.** What does the author want you to believe? That you should apply to USC.
2. **Name the evidence.** What reason does the author give? That you want to go to a top law school. Accept it as true.
3. **Ask what the author never wrote.** The argument only works if USC is a top law school. Nobody said so. That unstated premise is the assumption.
4. **Grant nothing that isn't on the page.** The LSAT is written like a contract. Anything you fill in for the author counts against you.

Put the missing premise back and the argument holds together.

> **Evidence:** You want to go to a top law school.
> **Assumption:** USC is a top law school.
> **Conclusion:** You should apply to USC.

> **What you're practicing.** Don't ask whether the argument sounds reasonable. Ask what has to be on the page for the conclusion to follow.

That's because valid is strict here. An argument is valid only if the conclusion can't be false when the evidence is true. There's no halfway, and almost every argument on the LSAT falls short. The assumption is what closes the gap.

> **What you said:** “There's a missing link … whether USC is a top law school or not, because we're assuming that it is.”
>
> That's the move. A missing link is a missing premise, and a missing premise is an assumption.

## Required, or enough?

Necessary and sufficient assumptions sound alike but answer different questions. Take a cash register. A pack costs $20 and the customer puts $18 on the counter.

> **Evidence:** The customer puts $18 on the counter.
> **Assumption:** The customer pays $2 more.
> **Conclusion:** The $20 pack is paid for.

The $2 in between is the assumption. So what does the customer have to be able to pay? Check each amount two ways. Is it required to cover the $2? That's necessary. Is it enough to cover the $2? That's sufficient.

- **At least 50 cents.** The customer has to be able to pay this much, but it's far short of $2. Necessary: yes. Sufficient: no.
- **At least $1.95.** The customer has to be able to pay this much. It's still 5 cents short, so it isn't enough. Necessary: yes. Sufficient: no.
- **At least $2.** The customer has to be able to pay this much, and it covers the $2 exactly. Necessary: yes. Sufficient: yes. This is the main assumption.
- **$10.** More than enough, but the customer didn't have to pay this much. Necessary: no. Sufficient: yes.

**Necessary assumption.** What the conclusion requires. The customer has to be able to pay at least $1.95. It's the floor, so the right answer is usually weak. Looks like: The customer can pay at least $1.95. Test: If this were false, would the conclusion fail?

**Sufficient assumption.** Enough to guarantee the conclusion. At least $2 is the least that's enough, and more is fine, so the right answer is usually strong. Looks like: The customer puts down $10. Test: If this were true, would the conclusion have to follow?

> **Where they meet.** At exactly $2, the least that's required and the least that's enough are the same amount. I call that the main assumption: the perfect bridge, not one penny too much or too little. It's why one statement can be both kinds.

A question only has to give you one required idea, and any of them can be the answer. Under “USC is a top law school” sits “USC has a law school.” It's obvious and it's required. That's why necessary answers can look too easy to be right.

## Two directions, one test

In the guessing game you asked true or false questions to find the person I had in mind. Singer, American and male all had to be true. Alive had to be false, because a living person can't be Michael Jackson. So a necessary assumption comes in two kinds.

**Linking: has to be true.** A missing idea the author has to agree with. You're repairing the argument, so stay inside what the author said and find the link. Treat it like a Must Be True question.

**Defender: has to be false.** An objection that would sink the argument. The author has to reject it, even if nobody raised it. It can bring in something the author never mentioned.

Two checks sort any answer. Did the author say it? Does the conclusion fail without it?

Two checks: (1) The author never said it. (2) The conclusion fails without it. If neither: Neither. This isn't an assumption the argument needs. If only the first: Only unstated. It may add something the argument can do without. If only the second: Only required. If it's already written, it's evidence, not an assumption. If both: Both pass. This is a necessary assumption.

The second check is the one to practice:

1. **Say what the answer claims.** Use plain words.
2. **Negate it.** Treat it as not true. Leave the author's evidence alone.
3. **Pull it out like a block from a Jenga tower.** If the conclusion falls, the author needed the answer. If it still stands, the answer wasn't required.

Example. Take the USC argument and the answer “USC is a top law school.” Negate it, and leave the evidence alone.

> **Evidence:** You want to go to a top law school.
> **Assumption:** USC is not a top law school.
> **Falls:** You should apply to USC.

There's now no reason to apply to USC. The conclusion falls, so the answer was required.

> **Negating a rule.** “Unless you have a passport, you can't enter Canada” means “If you don't have a passport, you can't enter.” To negate it, make the rule fail: no passport, and you enter anyway. You said, “if you don't have a passport, you will enter Canada.” As one person who gets in, that's the negation. As a rule about everyone without a passport, it claims too much, so write it as one case. Quantities negate their own way: the negation of all is not all, and the negation of some is none.

## Necessary answers ask for as little as possible

Words to slow down on: only, most, all, every.

A necessary assumption is the least the argument needs, so the right answer is usually weak. A strong claim usually asks for more than the argument requires. Slow down when you see these words.

Judge the idea, not the word. “The author requires the capacity to feel emotion” sounds firm, but it only asks that you can feel one emotion, ever. That's a low bar. And weak wording doesn't make an answer right either. It still has to be needed.

> **The trap that costs the most.** Choosing an answer the author might also agree with, or one that helps the argument. The author may like it, but the argument doesn't need it. A necessary assumption is one the author has to agree with. Negate the answer and see whether the argument still stands. If it does, the answer wasn't required.

> **What you said:** “I'm always looking for a stronger answer choice. And for necessary, we're looking for the weaker answer choice.” “Too strong for a necessary assumption.”
>
> The second line was your reason for cutting an answer with only. Both are the right reasons. Keep using them.

## Check yourself

**Say it out loud, then tap to check** A ticket costs $15 and the customer put $12 on the counter, so $3 is owed. For each payment, is it required, enough, or both?

- The customer can pay at least $2.50. **Required only.** The customer has to be able to pay that much. It still leaves 50 cents owed, so it isn't enough.
- The customer can pay at least $3. **Both.** Anything less leaves the debt unpaid, and this much covers it. This is the main assumption.
- The customer puts down $20. **Enough only.** It covers the debt with room to spare. The customer didn't need to pay that much.

> **Evidence:** The café added a second register.
> **Conclusion:** Lines will get shorter.

**Negate it. Does the conclusion fall?** Treat each answer as not true, leave the evidence alone, and ask whether the conclusion still stands.

- A second register will be staffed. **Necessary.** Negate it: the second register won't be staffed. Then adding it changes nothing, and the conclusion falls.
- The owner wants shorter lines. **Not required.** The author might agree with it. Negate it and lines can still get shorter.
- Every customer will use the second register. **Too strong.** Negate it: some customers still use only the first register. Lines can still get shorter.

**Is the argument valid?** Valid means the conclusion can't be false if the evidence is true.

*Say what the rule is, then ask whether the evidence meets what the rule needs.*

- If you work really hard, you'll get a 170. Dana works really hard. So Dana will get a 170. **Valid.** The if part of the rule is met, so the result has to follow.
- Working hard is required for a 170. Dana works really hard. So Dana will get a 170. **Not valid.** In your words, “just because that's a requirement doesn't mean that you're going to get that.” Required isn't enough.
- If you're in Toronto, you're in Canada. Dana is from Toronto. So Dana is in Canada. **Not valid.** You caught this in the session: “Just because someone's from there doesn't mean they're there right now.” The rule is about being in Toronto. The evidence is about where she's from.

## Before you read the answer choices

Do the work on the left, in the stimulus. The choices on the right are where you check the idea, not where you find it.

*Stimulus on the left. Choices on the right.*

1. **Read the stem.** “Depends on,” “requires,” “presupposes” and “relies on” ask for a necessary assumption. “What would make the conclusion follow” asks for a sufficient one.
2. **Find the conclusion and the evidence.** Say them in plain words. Accept the evidence.
3. **Predict what's missing.** What has to be on the page for the conclusion to follow? Is the answer likely a link or a defender?
4. **Check the choices against your prediction.** Negate the one you like. If the conclusion still stands, it wasn't required.
