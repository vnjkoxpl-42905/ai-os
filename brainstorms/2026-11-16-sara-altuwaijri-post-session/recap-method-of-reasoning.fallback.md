# Method of Reasoning

Every argument gets from evidence to conclusion by some route. Name the route as you read, and you know where that argument is most likely to break.

## The author starts somewhere and ends somewhere

Every argument starts with evidence and ends with a conclusion. The method of reasoning is how the author gets from one to the other, like flying or taking the train to the same city.

> **Evidence:** You work really hard.
> **Conclusion:** You'll get a 170.

Same start, same finish. There are three ways to get there.

**Conditional.** Uses a rule. If one thing is true, another has to be. Looks like: If you work really hard, you'll get a 170. Test: Is the if part actually met?

**Causal.** Says one thing produces another. Looks like: Working hard leads to a 170. Test: Could something else explain it, or could it run the other way?

**Comparative.** Says what happened to one case will happen to another. Looks like: Michael studied really hard and got a 170, so you will too. Test: Are the two cases alike where it matters?

> **Don't read past it.** When you see a conditional, a cause or a comparison, don't read past it. The author is using it to get from the evidence to the conclusion, so check whether it can carry that weight.

## Each route fails in its own way

Once you know the route, you know where to look for the gap.

### Conditional: a requirement is not a guarantee

This is the break to expect. The author treats something that's required as if it were enough.

> **Evidence:** Working hard is required for a 170. You work hard.
> **Doesn't follow:** You'll get a 170.

Working hard is required. It isn't enough. In your words, “just because that's a requirement doesn't mean that you're going to get that.”

### Causal: another cause, or the other direction

The author says one thing produces another. Ask whether something else could explain the same result, or whether it runs the other way.

### Comparative: the two cases have to be alike

The author says what happened to one case will happen to another. Ask whether the two differ in a way that matters.

## The route points at the missing idea

On a necessary assumption question, name the route before you read the choices. A conditional points at a missing link. A cause points at another explanation the author has to rule out. A comparison points at a similarity the author needs.

> **Name it every time.** You said the conditional and the causal versions were “kind of saying the same thing.” They're close, which is why you name the route every time. Each one fails differently.

## Name the route

**Say it out loud, then tap to check** Is each sentence conditional, causal or comparative?

- If the museum is open, the lights are on. **Conditional.** It's an if/then rule.
- Heavy rain makes the river flood. **Causal.** It says one thing produces another.
- Her sister took the class and passed, so she will pass too. **Comparative.** It says what happened to one case will happen to another.
- Only members may enter the library. **Conditional.** “Only” sets a requirement: if you enter, you're a member.
