Why RC feels intimidating, and the two fixes

RC often feels intimidating for two reasons:

  1. The passages can seem mysterious or random. The fix is the Passage ID system. RC passages use recurring structures. As those patterns become familiar, the section becomes more predictable and less mysterious.
  2. There seem to be too many details to remember. Names, theories, examples, dates, and objections can make it feel as though you need a photographic memory. The fix is key chunks. You do not need to remember every detail.

Key chunks: passages are objective-driven, not creative writing

RC passages are not streams of random sentences, and they are not creative-writing exercises. They are thesis-driven and objective-driven. The author has a goal, and every sentence contributes to it.

More specifically, the passage is built from key chunks. Each chunk has its own mini-objective: a specific job that advances the main point. Every sentence inside that chunk serves the same job.

If you know what a chunk basically boils down to, then even when you forget its details, you still know what could and could not have been supported by that chunk.

Author strategy tracking: read as the author

Formal writing starts from a main point. Faced with a blank page, a writer needs a thesis before building an outline. The author then crafts a strategy for delivering that point to the reader.

When you read only to take in the information, the passage can start to feel like one long stream. Instead, move into the writer’s seat. Boil each paragraph down to its strategic move and ask how that move advances the main point.

As you read, ask:

  • What does this chunk basically boil down to?
  • How does this move advance the main point?

That is author strategy tracking.

The boss-email analogy

Imagine that you have found a problem at work and want your boss to approve your solution. Your main point is the action you want the company to take, but you still need a strategy for delivering it.

Strategy What the email does Why it works or fails
1 Jumps straight to the solution The boss has no reason to agree and may not even understand the problem.
2 Establishes the problem, explains why it matters, then gives the solution The solution now has a reason and a clear purpose.
3 Establishes the problem, considers competing solutions and why they fall short, then gives the solution The competing views are taken off the table, and the contrast makes the proposed solution stronger.

An RC passage works the same way. A paragraph may exist mainly to establish a problem, introduce a competing view, or create a contrast that allows the author’s real point to land later. Instead of asking only what each paragraph says, ask what job it performs in the author’s strategy.

Why the map works

You do not need a photographic memory to answer RC questions. You need the gist of the passage’s key chunks.

Think of the passage like a puzzle with a few missing pieces. If the completed part clearly shows a person hiking in the mountains, you already know that a computer mouse or a pink strand of hair will not fit. You do not need every piece before you can reject what is incompatible with the picture.

RC works the same way. If a chunk clearly explains why a thinker rejects a theory, you may forget some details, but you still know what could and could not fit that person’s position. The map preserves that useful gist.

The major moves

Major moves determine the passage ID. These deserve most of your attention.

Mark Move What it means
P Author position The author states, evaluates, recommends, or builds a case for their own view.
V Viewpoint A person, group, institution, or school of thought takes a position.
VX Opposing viewpoint A second viewpoint conflicts with the first.

Author position: P

This is the most important move. P does not mean that the author is simply the person writing the sentence. It means the author is giving their own position or evaluation. Look for the author judging, recommending, criticizing, approving, or building a case for something.

When the author’s position is explicit, it usually gives you the center of the main point.

Viewpoint: V

A viewpoint is not just information about a person. It is a position that someone holds.

Keep the label short, but identify whose view it is. In PT104, Passage 4, you could write:

  • V-Freud for Freud’s view
  • V-Bettelheim for Bettelheim’s view
  • V-critics for a group of critics

Clash: V and VX

A clash occurs when two viewpoints oppose one another.

Do not assume that every pair of viewpoints creates a clash. Two views may simply appear beside each other. Mark a clash when the passage gives you a real disagreement, contrast, or competing explanation.

The supporting moves

Supporting moves help organize the passage, but they usually matter less than P, V, and VX.

Mark Move What it means
CI Contextual information Background facts that set up the discussion.
V Development Viewpoint development A viewpoint already introduced is developed or expanded.
E Explanation An idea, position, or process is developed further.
I / SE Illustration or supporting evidence An example or evidence supports a larger idea.
RO Rhetorical question or transition A question or brief move shifts the reader’s attention.

The most important distinction is between background and position. The author may be the one writing a factual sentence, but that does not make the sentence the author’s opinion. Ask whether the author is taking a stance or simply giving you information you need.

Keep each note to a few words. The legend helps you identify the move, but the map should remain a snapshot rather than a rewritten passage.

P is the base author-position mark. Add X when the author directly attacks a viewpoint. Add A when the author presents an alternative. For example, P + X means the author is criticizing a viewpoint, while P + A means the author is giving a different position.

Passage Structural ID legend

The major moves combine to give you the passage ID. The ID tells you what kind of passage you are reading and where its main point is likely to be.

Passage ID What it means What the map usually shows
Clash-A*V Author takes a side V and VX clash, then P shows which side the author supports.
Clash-A*V! Author criticizes a viewpoint The author directly attacks a viewpoint with P + X.
Clash-V+A Author presents an alternative position The passage presents the competing position or positions, then P + A gives the author’s alternative without a direct attack.
Clash-A*V+A Author criticizes a viewpoint and presents an alternative The author uses P + X to reject a view, then P + A to give the alternative.
Explained / Development Passage Non-clash passage The passage explains, develops, or evaluates a subject without a central clash.

How to map a clash

Who is opposing whom? Mark it as Key distinction
One viewpoint opposes another viewpoint V + VX Use VX whether the conflict is a direct attack or a simple contrast.
The author takes one side V + VX + P P shows which side the author supports.
The author directly attacks a viewpoint V + P + X X means the author explicitly criticizes or knocks down V.
The author gives a different position V + P + A A means the author pivots to an alternative without directly attacking V.
The author attacks a view and gives an alternative V + VX + P + X + A The author first rejects the existing views, then gives a different position.

Major moves vs. minor moves

Most passages revolve around three to five major authorial moves. These are the key chunks. Everything else is a minor move that falls under a major umbrella.

Process the minor moves as you read, but mark and internalize the major ones. Note-taking is a means to that end. The goal is not to create the most detailed notes. The goal is to recognize the key chunks, retain their gist, and see how they advance the main point.

How to build the passage map

1. Understand the chunk

Read for the basic content first. If labeling while reading interferes with comprehension, finish the paragraph or chunk, then name its job. You do not need to understand and classify every sentence at the same instant.

2. Identify the strongest move

Ask:

  • Is the author taking a position?
  • Is someone else taking a position?
  • Is there a clash?
  • Is the author criticizing a view or presenting an alternative?
  • If none of those applies, is this context, viewpoint development, explanation, illustration, evidence, or a transition?

Aim for one or two useful moves per paragraph. Do not label every sentence.

3. Tie the label to the text

Underline or highlight the exact line that triggered the label. A mark without a location is too abstract to help you later.

4. Add only the gist

Use a few words to capture the position or function:

  • P1: CI uncanny
  • P2: V-Freud excludes fairy tales
  • P3: P: Bettelheim's work is flawed but valuable
  • P4: V Development-Bettelheim
  • P5: RO → E-Bettelheim

The map is a router, not a rewritten passage.

5. Identify the passage’s center

Before looking at the questions, ask:

  1. Did the author state a position? If yes, anchor the main point there.
  2. If the author presented competing views without an explicit P, which view received the most development, support, or favorable treatment?
  3. If the passage is mainly descriptive, what broad idea unifies the information?

Pre-phrase the main point before reading the answer choices. The correct answer must capture the passage’s center, not one interesting detail.

This is your passage ID: the passage’s basic structure and the main point that follows from it. Once you know the ID, the answer choices are no longer five open possibilities. Each choice either fits the passage you mapped or it does not.

Read the map from top to bottom. P1 gives you the foundation. P2 introduces Freud’s decision to exclude fairy tales. P3 reveals the author’s favorable evaluation of Bettelheim. Passage map at a glance

Paragraph Move Gist
P1 CI Freud’s theory of the uncanny provides the background.
P2 V-Freud + author signal Freud excludes fairy tales; “alas” shows the author’s regret.
P3 P The author sees real flaws in Bettelheim’s work but evaluates it favorably overall.
P4 V Development-Bettelheim Bettelheim’s therapeutic view is developed through supporting details.
P5 RO → E-Bettelheim A rhetorical question creates the transition, then Bettelheim’s reasoning is explained.

Why those labels fit

P1: CI

The opening gives Freud’s theory of the uncanny. The author is relaying Freud’s framework, not endorsing it. This is the background the rest of the passage needs.

P2: V-Freud + an author signal

Freud excludes fairy tales from his analysis because everything in them is possible, so nothing seems incredible. That is Freud’s viewpoint. The word “alas” is also the author’s first small signal: the author regrets Freud’s decision.

P3: P

The author evaluates Bettelheim’s work. The criticisms are real: Bettelheim’s reading is reductive and unaware of an existing critical tradition. But the sentence turns on “nevertheless.” The author’s final verdict is that the book is a splendid achievement full of useful ideas. In an “although X, nevertheless Y” structure, Y carries the author’s conclusion.

P4: V Development-Bettelheim

This paragraph develops Bettelheim’s view through his work with autistic children, the problems fairy tales address, and the role of parents. These are supporting details under the Bettelheim umbrella, not a new major position.

P5: RO → E-Bettelheim

The opening rhetorical question creates a transition: why should fairy tales themselves be therapeutic? The rest of the paragraph gives Bettelheim’s answer through the child-as-interpreter idea and the assumptions behind it.

Passage ID

Explained / Development Passage

Freud declines to apply his framework to fairy tales. Bettelheim does what Freud would not, and the author evaluates Bettelheim favorably overall despite genuine reservations. Freud is the setup and contrast that make Bettelheim’s achievement significant. The passage does not center on the author directly attacking Freud, so it should not be forced into an author-criticizes Passage ID.

Main-point expectation

The correct main point should center on Bettelheim because the author gives Bettelheim the favorable evaluation and most of the passage’s development. It should say that Bettelheim’s reading of fairy tales is a valuable achievement for what it reveals about how children interpret them, even though his approach is reductive and overlooks an existing critical tradition.

This is the key lesson from the map: a correct answer cannot merely compare Freud and Bettelheim. It must reflect where the author places the weight of the passage.

What to compare

  • Did you mark P1 as background, or did you mistake the author’s explanation of Freud for the author’s own position?
  • Did you mark P2 as Freud’s viewpoint?
  • Did you notice what “alas” reveals about the author?
  • In P3, did you preserve both the criticism and the favorable final verdict?
  • Did you keep P4 under the Bettelheim umbrella instead of treating it as a new major move?
  • Did you recognize the rhetorical question in P5 as a transition?
  • Did your main-point prediction evaluate Bettelheim rather than merely compare him with Freud?

What to avoid

  • Do not rewrite the passage in your notes.
  • Do not treat every detail as equally important.
  • Do not confuse background facts with the author’s position.
  • Do not force two viewpoints into a clash unless they actually conflict.
  • Do not spend too long searching for the perfect label. Use the best useful label and keep reading.
  • Do not open the answer choices before you have a main-point prediction.
