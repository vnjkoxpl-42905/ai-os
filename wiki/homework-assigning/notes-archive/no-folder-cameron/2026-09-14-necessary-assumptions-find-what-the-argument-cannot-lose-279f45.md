A necessary assumption is something an argument needs in order to survive. It does not have to prove the conclusion. Remove it and the reasoning should collapse.

## First, preserve the argument

- **Premises:** the evidence the author wants you to accept.
- **Conclusion:** what the author believes that evidence proves.

Grant the premises. Ask: **If every premise is true, could the conclusion still be false?** If yes, the gap reveals what the author must be assuming. Keep every person, group, time period, and measurement exactly as stated.

### Watch for changes in language

Suppose Maya has worked as a paramedic for ten years. The author concludes that Maya will be an excellent emergency-room nurse. The argument needs a connection between paramedic experience and the skills required for excellent emergency-room nursing. Do not silently supply that link. Mark the change and test what the author cannot lose.

## Necessary and sufficient assumptions do different jobs

- **Necessary:** take it away and the argument collapses.
- **Sufficient:** add it and the conclusion becomes guaranteed.

### Nine questions for testing necessity

These are nine different prompts, not a nine-step process. Use whichever wording makes the dependency easiest to see:

1. If the conclusion is true, does this have to be true?
2. Does the author have to believe this?
3. Does the author have to think this is true?
4. If the conclusion is proven, does this have to be true?
5. If the premises worked to prove the conclusion, is this required?
6. If these premises proved this conclusion, what must the author assume?
7. Does the argument rely on this?
8. For the argument to make any sense, does this have to be its foundation?
9. What must the author believe to think they actually proved this conclusion?

For questions 1 and 4, treat the conclusion as proven by these premises. It may be true for some unrelated reason.

## Link the evidence to the conclusion

A linking assumption connects something new in the conclusion to the evidence.

> **Premise:** Cameron wants to attend a good law school.
> **Conclusion:** Cameron wants to attend Michigan Law.

The argument needs the bridge: **Michigan Law is a good law school.** When the conclusion introduces a new person, category, standard, or relationship, look for that missing connection.

## Defend the argument from an objection

A defender assumption rules out an alternative explanation that would break the reasoning.

> **Evidence:** A preserved sample contains less of a chemical than a modern sample.
> **Conclusion:** The chemical must be more common in the environment today.

The comparison works only if preservation did not substantially reduce the chemical in the older sample. If it did, the samples cannot show an environmental change.

## Use the negation test

1. Negate the answer choice.
2. Add that negation to the argument.
3. Ask whether the conclusion can still be supported.

If the negation destroys the reasoning, the original answer was necessary. If the argument survives, it was not required.

## A comparison needs both sides

A fact about one group matters only if it changes the comparison. If older birds were not fully grown when tested, you also need to know whether the modern birds were equally mature. **Could matter** is not enough. The argument must require the answer.

## Repeatable approach

1. Restate the premises and conclusion without changing variables.
2. Find one way the premises could be true while the conclusion is false.
3. Decide whether the argument needs a link or protection from an objection.
4. Use the necessity question that makes the dependency easiest to see.
5. Negate the answer and choose it only if the argument collapses.
