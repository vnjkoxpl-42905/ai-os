Ask How Much
the Answer Needs

Must Be True and Necessary Assumption questions both reward the answer that needs the least from the passage. The hard ones catch you in two ways. A rule gets run backwards, or a big, confident answer looks like it says more than it can.

Start here

Two jobs a conditional question can give you

When a passage is built from rules, the question usually asks you to do one of two things. Name the job first. It tells you what to look for.

Apply a fact to a rule

You get a rule and a fact about one case. The question is what the rule lets you say about that case.

Looks likea rule, then a sentence about one named person, thing or place
TestDoes the fact match the starting side of the rule, or the opposite of its result? If neither, nothing follows.

Combine the rules

There is no named case. Two rules share an idea, and you work out what follows when you connect them.

Looks liketwo rules and no single case
TestWhich idea ends one rule and starts another? Join them there.

A rule is a general sentence: when this happens, that follows. A fact is what is true about one case. A rule does nothing until a fact starts it. As you read, mark each sentence as a rule or a fact and keep them apart.

The opposite of the result works because of the contrapositive. It is the same rule said another way: if the result did not happen, the starting side did not happen either.

Rules and facts

A rule runs one way

Being in the rare room guarantees a catalogue entry. Seeing a catalogue entry guarantees nothing about the room. The second row is the one wrong answers are built from.

It happened in our session. A museum shop sold prints of a painting. Wrong answers read that as news about the original: that it was on display, or on loan. A shop can sell posters of the Mona Lisa without owning it. All you know is that a print exists.

The trap that costs the mostA fact about the copy is not a fact about the original. The first rule in that passage was about works on display. Nothing said this painting was on display, so that rule never started.

Combining rules

The OR was put there on purpose

If a rule has an OR or an AND in it, expect the question to test it. Test writers rarely add one for decoration. Here is what an OR can hide.

If you got into the gala → you have a ticket or a membership
  1. Start with the rule. Everyone who got into the gala has a ticket or a membership.
  2. Add a fact. Priya got in, and she has no ticket.
  3. Cross out one half. The OR says at least one of the two is true. The ticket is out, so the membership has to be true.
  4. Add a second rule. Every member gets a parking pass. Priya has a membership, so she has a parking pass.

Put the two rules together and you get a new one: anyone who got in without a ticket has a parking pass. A hard question hides a conclusion like that behind the OR.

RememberWhen one half of an OR is ruled out, the other half has to be true. When both halves are out, the rule's starting side did not happen.
Before the answers

Say what you do not know

Knowing what you don't know is almost as important as knowing what you do. The answers that sound like what you know are the ones that catch you.

  1. Hold on to one rule until you could say it without looking. If a rule has an OR or an AND, pick that one.
  2. Say the wrong version out loud. For the gala rule: "Having a ticket or a membership does not tell me anyone got in."
  3. Now read the answers. The ones that sound like your wrong version are easy to spot.
Strength

Pick the safer bet

Must Be True and Necessary Assumption sound like two different questions. They reward the same instinct: the answer that needs the least.

Say I pick a random student and bet on her LSAT score. Is a bet on 150 safer, or a bet on 180? The 150. The higher the guess, the more evidence it needs. Answer choices work the same way. The bigger the claim, the more the passage has to give you.

A weak answerAsks for little. The passage can back it easily.
A strong answerAsks for a lot. The passage has to back all of it, and one exception ends it.

Words that make a claim big:

onlyneveralwaysalleverynot possible

Before you test any answer, sort all five from weakest to strongest. On Must Be True and Necessary Assumption, the right answer is more often near the weak end than the strong end.

Strength is how much the answer needsThe words are a clue, not the test. In our session one answer said "every work" and was correct, because a rule in the passage already covered every case. Another answer was wrong because of its scope: it was about any entity in the world, when the argument only needed one situation. Ask how much the passage must give you for this answer to be true.
On the obligation question, you said the answer you first picked was too strong, and that the conclusion was about cutting trees down, so the answer had to stay there. That is the scope check.

Two limits. A weak answer still has to matter to the argument. Soft wording about an unrelated idea is still wrong. Strengthen and weaken questions treat strength differently, so know which question you are in before you judge an answer's strength.

Must Be FalseThe right answer contradicts something the passage gives you, or something you can work out by combining two claims. In PT127 Section 1, Question 25, "it is not possible to raise cacti" tempted. The passage never ruled that out, so it could be true, which means it cannot be the answer.
Necessary assumption

A duty about a thing is not a duty to the thing

Take this argument. A statue cannot have rights. An obligation can only be owed to something that has rights. So we have no obligation not to damage the statue.

The evidence is about who we owe something to. The conclusion is about whether we have any obligation at all. Those are different. If I have a duty not to smash my neighbor's car with a bat, I don't owe that duty to the car. I owe it to my neighbor.

  1. The missing idea. The author is assuming that any duty not to damage the statue would have to be owed to the statue.
  2. Negate it. A duty not to damage the statue could be owed to someone else, such as the person who made it.
  3. Does the argument survive? No. If that duty exists, the conclusion is false even though the evidence is true.
You took the "not" out of the answer, read it straight, and saw that the argument fell apart. That is the negation test working the way it should.
Role questions

Name the conclusion before you answer

On the last question, the hard part was finding the conclusion, not choosing the answer. Get the conclusion right and the answer is much easier to see.

  1. Look for the claim the whole passage builds toward. It is often in the last sentence, after a word like "thus" or "so".
  2. Test every other claim: why should I believe this? A claim the author supports and then uses to support something else is a middle step, not the main conclusion.
  3. Only then ask what the underlined phrase does for those claims.

Here is a small one. Tractors once looked too complicated for ordinary farmers, yet farms everywhere use them now. This shows that complicated machines can spread. So today's drones, which also look complicated, may spread too.

The main conclusion is that drones may spread. "Complicated machines can spread" is a middle step. The phrase "which also look complicated" puts the drones in the same starting spot the tractors were in. It is the barrier, not proof that the barrier can be crossed. The proof is the tractors.

A phrase inside the conclusionA descriptive phrase inside a conclusion is presented as a fact. "Therefore Dan's attorney, who went to Harvard, can solve this tax problem." The author treats the Harvard part as given. What the argument is trying to prove is the tax problem.

The order to follow

On every Must Be True, Necessary Assumption or Must Be False

  1. Mark each sentence as a rule or a fact.
  2. Decide the job: apply a fact to a rule, or combine rules.
  3. Read any OR or AND slowly. Cross out one half of an OR and see what the other half has to be.
  4. Say out loud what you do not know.
  5. Sort the answers from weakest to strongest. For each big word or wide scope, ask how much the passage must give you.
  6. On a Necessary Assumption question, negate the answer. If there is a "not", take it out and read it straight. Does the argument fall apart?
