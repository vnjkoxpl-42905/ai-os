Two famous flaws, one underlying mistake

Read this before you open Famous Flaw (Easier) Set 1. This is where the last two days pay off.

A famous flaw is just a broken move the test reuses. Learn the shape once and you recognize it in any subject matter. The two below are the same error running in opposite directions, and both come from treating a one way arrow as if it went both ways.

1. Treating what is required as if it were enough

The argument gets the necessary half and declares victory.

admitted → passed the exam

"She passed the exam, so she will be admitted." Passing was required. It was never enough. Plenty of people pass and are turned down, and the argument gave you nothing that rules that out.

2. Losing the trigger and concluding nothing follows

The argument finds the sufficient half missing and decides the whole thing is off.

rain → the ground is wet

"It did not rain, so the ground is dry." Losing the trigger does not cancel the outcome. Something else can produce it. Only the version you learned on day one is legal here: dry ground tells you it did not rain.

Both errors are one habit. The argument treats the arrow as if it ran in both directions. If you can draw the conditional, you can name the flaw, and you can usually do it before you read an answer choice.

Say it before you read the choices

On any flaw question, describe what went wrong in your own words first. Something as blunt as "it needed that, it did not need only that" is enough. Going in with your own description is what keeps a well written wrong answer from talking you into it, which is the thing that has cost you points before.

While you drill

  1. Diagram the conditional in the stimulus. These flaws are visible in the arrow.
  2. Name the flaw in plain language before you look at the options.
  3. Then find the choice that says your sentence in the test's vocabulary.
