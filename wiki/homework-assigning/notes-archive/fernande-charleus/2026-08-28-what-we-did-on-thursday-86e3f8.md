# What we did on Thursday

## In order

**What an argument is.** A premise and a conclusion. That is all of it. Background, opposing views, explanation: none of that is the argument, and once you can find the two real pieces you can read every stimulus on the test the same way.

**What makes something a conclusion.** It has to be an opinion. A fact is handed to you as true and there is nothing left to argue about. A conclusion is where the author says what follows, or what ought to be done, and that is the only place a gap can live.

**What validity means on this test.** Not the everyday sense of the word. It means a guarantee: if the premises are true, the conclusion has to be true. Being from Florida against being in Florida was the case we used. The conclusion had quietly helped itself to something the premise never gave it.

**How to build an objection.** You do not fight the evidence. You accept it, then invent a specific situation where the evidence still holds and the conclusion fails anyway. That one move is what answers flaw questions, weakening questions and necessary assumption questions. Only what you do with the answer changes.

**The political speeches question.** You got to D by arguing that a selfish reason for making a promise does not make the promise unreliable. Wanting to be reelected is itself a reason to keep it.

**Writing the attacks down.** You named the real difficulty yourself: two answers left and no way to separate them. The fix is physical. On paper, one bullet per surviving answer, the specific reason that answer fails. A vague suspicion does not survive being written out as a sentence. That is what turned B into a dead answer instead of a maybe.

**The irrigation question.** You isolated the conclusion, that modern societies relying on irrigation will collapse the way Sumeria did, and then built the objection yourself: modern irrigation may use methods that avoid the salt buildup. B did not disprove that irrigation caused the collapse. D was about societies the argument was never talking about. E was about the ancient case, not the modern claim. C said your own objection back to you, which is what a correct weakening answer usually does.

## What changed

You came in reading an argument as the issue being presented. By the second question you were pulling the conclusion out first, accepting the evidence, and building a counterexample against the jump before you looked at a single answer choice. Doing it in that order is what stops a well written wrong answer from getting to you, because you already know what you are looking for when you arrive.

Both questions turned on the same move, and it is worth carrying: something was true of one case, and the author assumed it was true of every case.

## Carry forward

- Say the conclusion back to yourself in one compact sentence before you do anything else.
- Accept the premises as given. Attack the jump, never the evidence.
- Write out the attack on every answer still standing, one bullet each, before you choose.
- Work without a clock running until you are getting them right consistently. Speed follows accuracy, and a question missed quickly is worth nothing.
- Practise restating an argument compactly and designing an objection to it, including on arguments nobody is testing you on.
