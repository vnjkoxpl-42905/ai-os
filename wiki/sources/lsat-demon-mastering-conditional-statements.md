## What the article does

The article starts with ordinary conditional language and defines sufficient as enough to establish the other condition under the supplied rule. Its poison/sickness anchor demonstrates that a sufficient condition guarantees the necessary condition without making the reverse inference valid. It asks readers to accept a supplied premise rather than invent real-world exceptions.

Its most useful teaching passage supplies a deliberately artificial restaurant-meal/tax-credit rule. It then asks what someone **needs** to do to receive the credit. The tempting answer mistakes a guaranteed route for the only route. Other routes could fit the rule. This distinguishes “enough for the result” from “required for the result” using an ordinary question rather than labels alone.

The article also demonstrates if-clauses at the end of sentences and omitted “then.” Its examples concern crops, a car-payment instruction and ability to sell spiders after cleaning cages. These are evidence of the article's method, not recommended student-facing examples.

## The supplied six-question structure

The article uses a satirical law-firm/misery conditional. Abstract it as A → B for source analysis:

| Question function | Supplied answer, paraphrased | Precise interpretation for adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| What is sufficient for B? | A. | A establishes B under the supplied rule. |
| What is necessary for B? | Unknown. | The rule does not establish that A is required for B. Do not claim absolutely no necessary conditions exist. |
| What is sufficient for A? | Unknown. | The rule does not establish that B is enough for A. Do not deny trivial or other independently supplied sufficient conditions. |
| What is necessary for A? | B. | A cannot hold while B is false under the rule. |
| Which clause is necessary for the other? | The then-clause. | B is required for A. |
| Which clause needs the other? | The if-clause. | A needs B. This wording tests the learner's understanding from the opposite grammatical perspective. |

The distinction between the last two questions is particularly useful: **B is required for A** and **A requires B** describe the same relationship. Ask both naturally without turning the exercise into a vocabulary trick.

## Original adaptation authorized for development

Joshua approved an original raffle example modeled on this teaching pattern: **If you win this raffle, you get a bicycle.** The relationship is supplied for the exercise, not a claim about every raffle. Winning is enough to establish getting a bicycle. Getting a bicycle alone does not establish winning this raffle: receiving one as a gift or buying one could be consistent alternatives.

Keep the alternatives as possible cases, not new premises about a particular person. Scope each question to what this rule establishes. “Does this rule make winning the raffle necessary for getting a bicycle?” is clearer than asking for an unrestricted inventory of every necessary condition for obtaining a bicycle.

After the roles have been explained, vary the supplied fact and ask what follows, then ask the learner to describe a bicycle recipient who did not win the raffle. Later contraposition can ask whether someone who never gets a bicycle could satisfy the winning condition under this same rule. Avoid ambiguous snapshots such as “doesn't have the bicycle yet,” which do not deny eventual receipt. ## Limits and corrections before reuse

- Unsupported prevalence claims, scholarship/tax implications, medical assertions and the law-firm caricature are not imported into student material.
- The article's statement that a then-clause is not sufficient and an if-clause is not necessary is too absolute. A supplied A → B does not establish B → A; another rule could establish it. Name the missing inference rather than declaring the roles impossible.
- “Nothing can fill the blank” overstates what is unknown. Restrict the question to the relation between the two named conditions established by this rule.
- Distinguish instructions and permission/ability from observed compliance. A car-payment instruction does not prove how someone paid; ability to sell does not prove a sale.
- A premise accepted for an exercise need not be causal, chronological or independently plausible. Do not turn every conditional into a physical trigger.
- The article supplies examples and teaching advice, not comparative learning-science evidence. Do not claim measured learning gains from adopting its question pattern.
