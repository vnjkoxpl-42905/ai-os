# Five patterns Nate Herk says separate paid-quality AI websites

Nate Herk's [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mImbbszCjCI) was published 2026-11-09 (11:18). The clip has the full auto-caption transcript with timestamps; there are no visuals, so the example sites he scrolls through were not seen. [Provenance](../../raw/sources/clips/10000-ai-websites-nate-herk/PROVENANCE.md).

"Studied 10,000 websites" is the creator's framing, not a described method. The conversion figures he quotes (Truckers Report +79.3%, City Click +90%, Campaign Monitor +31.4%, Doc Braces about 45%) and the research he cites (Nielsen Norman Group on motion, a Purdue parallax study, Harvard/Duke IKEA effect, a US inner-ear survey) are relayed second-hand and were not checked against the originals here.

## The five patterns, as he states them

1. **Mirror test (copy, 0:24).** The first screen should make the right visitor see their own problem. It answers three questions: who is this for, what do I get (outcome, not feature), and what do I do next (one call to action). Write those answers before asking an AI for any design. Same idea as his "three P's": pain, person, promise.
2. **Highlighter rule (colour, 2:36).** One accent colour, used only on what you want clicked. Spreading the accent over headlines, icons and borders makes the button disappear. Use 60-30-10: 60% background, 30% structure (text, menus, cards), 10% accent (buttons, links). His own site looked cheap with blue everywhere and better as mostly black and white.
3. **Front, middle, back (depth, 4:19).** Flat pages give the eye no first place to land. Back: something real (a photo of the work, a soft shape). Middle: headline, text, cards. Front: the one action. Three steps: put something real in the back, let one element break a section edge, and lift only what matters with a soft shadow.
4. **Motion with a job (6:51).** Each animation does one job: show the product, point to the next step, or show something that just happened. Rules: one thing moves per screen; never make visitors wait for the words (first-screen text, headline and button readable immediately); give them an off switch and respect reduced-motion settings. Clients want customers, not a movie.
5. **Free sample (9:09).** Give a taste of the result before asking for anything: a quiz, a price estimator, or a useful tool that answers the question every customer asks before buying. It only works if people finish, so keep it short.

## How this sits against what the vault already holds

- Patterns 1, 3 and 4 restate methods already in [website design planning](../../references/website-design-planning.md) (visitor's decisions first, three P's, depth through layering) and the [design principles](../../design/principles.md#material-depth-and-a-richer-layered-feel) (purposeful layers, motion with a reason, reduced motion). Complementary, not new direction.
- New specifics: the three first-screen questions, 60-30-10 as a proportion check, "let one thing break the edge", "one thing moves per screen", and "no animated text on the first screen".
- Pattern 2 agrees with Joshua's house look (near-white ground, one charcoal anchor, one muted accent); see the near-white base rule in [design principles](../../design/principles.md#the-selected-dashboard-reference). The source's palette examples (navy, burnt orange) are illustrations, not a palette to adopt.
- Pattern 5 is new to the vault. It is queued for LSAT U as [LQ-007](../lsatu-idea-queue.md).
- Qualification, not contradiction: his earlier [Fable 5.1 video](fable-5-1-kills-ai-website-slop.md) recommends planes moving at different rates on scroll; here he cites a study that parallax felt more fun but was no easier to use and cut his own 3D ring.
