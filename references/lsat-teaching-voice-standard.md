# LSAT teaching: make the reasoning sound like someone thinking

**This is Joshua's selected reference for writing LSAT teaching material.** Use it for bootcamp lessons, lecture notes, explanations, guided examples, drills, flashcards, quizzes, reference pages, and instructional feedback. It develops the teaching-content register in [the canonical teaching voice](../wiki/teaching-voice.md). That document still governs correspondence and marketing in their own registers.

Joshua selected the supplied **Conditional Reasoning** transcription on 2026-10-02 because of its writing, tone, word choice, cadence, point of view, progression, and depth. The goal is to reproduce those qualities closely through original wording and examples. The central quality is patient, conversational reasoning that the reader can follow and eventually perform.

The source is a calibration example. This guide separates **observed features** from **instructions for our material**. A source habit is not automatically a rule, a research finding, or permission to import a topic. Matching the voice does not require repeating the same metaphors, anecdotes, exercise sentences, or personal claims.

## The voice in one original passage

The following is a new calibration passage. Its example was created for this guide; it is not a source quotation or an approved bootcamp page.

> A conditional tells us that if one thing is true, something else has to be true too.
>
> Suppose a shop has this rule: **Every parcel with a blue sticker has been inspected.**
>
> You pick up a parcel with a blue sticker. What do you know about it?
>
> It has been inspected. The rule covers every parcel with that sticker, including the one in your hand. A blue-sticker parcel that hadn't been inspected would break the rule.
>
> Now turn the parcel over. It has an inspection stamp, but you can't see a blue sticker. Does it have to have one?
>
> We don't know. The rule tells us something about parcels with blue stickers. It doesn't tell us that every inspected parcel gets a sticker.
>
> That's the distinction we need: **what the sentence makes certain, and what it leaves open.**

Notice the opening definition before the example, the small prediction, the explanation connecting the rule to the case, and the change in what the reader is given. The last sentence names a distinction the reader has just seen. The passage has a conversational rhythm because it follows a person's thoughts. Casual vocabulary alone would not accomplish that.

In an interactive lesson, pause after the first question and again after the inspection-stamp question. Reveal each explanation after the learner has had an opportunity to answer. The printed passage shows the whole progression for calibration.

## How the teacher relates to the reader

### The reader is beside the teacher

The narrator talks directly to someone doing the work. “Let's,” “you,” and “we” keep the reader involved. The writing seldom steps back to describe what students generally do. It asks this reader to notice, try, decide, or check something now.

The reader is treated as capable of understanding ordinary meaning. The teacher supplies support when a technical step is new. In the opening, the source first gives the literal relationship, then asks the reader to take its certainty seriously. Later, it encourages a guess before revealing the contrapositive. These are expectations of participation, not claims that everything should feel easy.

**For our writing:** speak to the reader without making their intelligence the subject. Do not copy a line such as “Because you're a thinking human.” Give them a manageable question and the information to answer it. If they miss it, explain the precise move that went wrong.

### Confidence lives in the reasoning

The source is decisive about a valid consequence. It also says “We don't know” when the information cannot settle a question. These two kinds of confidence belong together. Clear teaching can be certain about the limits of what is known.

The teacher does not apologize for asking the reader to think. The explanation makes the request reasonable. In the FAQ, the narrator acknowledges early mistakes and explains the value of practicing anyway. The tone combines patience with a specific demand: attempt the question, write the diagram, inspect the error.

**For our writing:** state the consequence plainly and show why. Preserve legitimate uncertainty. Avoid changing a logical “could” into “must” to make the prose sound stronger. Avoid guarantees about learning outcomes, timing, or score changes unless we have evidence.

### Warmth points at the work

Encouragement is usually tied to an action. The reader can look back at the lesson, make a provisional attempt, learn from a wrong translation, or build confidence by checking work. The warmth comes from useful help and a teacher who expects progress.

Useful form:

> If the direction felt unclear, read the statement back in ordinary English. What does it say you need? Keep that complete idea in your diagram.

Weak form:

> You've got this! Conditionals are easy once you know the trick!

The weak version offers reassurance without a way forward and minimizes the actual difficulty.

### “I,” “we,” and “you” have different jobs

| Pronoun | What it does in the source | Use in our material |
|---|---|---|
| **You** | Gives the reader an action or asks for a decision. | “Try the sentence in the other direction. Does it still say the same thing?” |
| **We / let's** | Makes the explanation a shared investigation. | “Let's keep the rule and change the fact.” |
| **I**, thinking aloud | Shows the teacher making a decision: noticing an indicator, identifying its target, placing a label. | “I see ‘requires.’ What does it say is required?” |
| **I**, hypothetical | Places the speaker inside a condition. | “If I'm taking this course, what does the sentence say I need?” |
| **I**, personal judgment | Gives a recommendation or reports experience. | Use only a genuine Joshua judgment or verified experience. Do not borrow the source narrator's student history. |

Keep these roles stable. Do not rotate pronouns for cosmetic variety. In a platform instruction, “you” may be enough. In a teacher-authored walkthrough, “I” can make a decision visible. Marketing remains governed by its separate platform voice.

## Sentence length and spoken cadence

### Short sentences settle; longer sentences explain

The source does not make every sentence short. A longer sentence introduces a situation, explains motivation, or qualifies advice. Short sentences supply a result, mark a limit, or redirect attention.

The opening at HTML line 144 moves from a definition to a plain rephrasing and then the two-word landing “That's it.” Later, the source uses several brief sentences to think through a missing requirement, then states the consequence. The broad-purpose paragraph at line 152 is substantially longer. Its job is to explain why understanding the relationship matters across varied wording.

**For our writing:** let each sentence carry a manageable thought. A difficult inference may need several sentences. Do not trim away the intermediate reason just to produce a punchy conclusion. A short landing belongs after the explanation has earned it.

Original rhythm:

> The inspection stamp tells us the parcel was inspected. It doesn't tell us which sticker the shop gave it afterward. The rule hasn't answered that question.

The first sentence identifies what is known. The second identifies the missing connection. The third closes the uncertainty.

### Contractions are normal; expansion creates contrast

The source uses contractions throughout: I'm, you're, we've, we'll, there's, doesn't, don't, can't, couldn't, isn't, and what's. This includes serious explanation and procedure, not only playful asides.

It sometimes expands a form for emphasis. “If I **am** breathing” makes the presence of the sufficient condition audible. We can use the same mechanism with new language:

> You don't know whether the parcel has a sticker. You **do** know it was inspected.

Do not mechanically contract every phrase. Keep a fuller form when it makes the distinction easier to hear.

### Punctuation follows the voice

Use full stops for changes in thought, colons before an example or sequence, and questions when a decision is genuinely at issue. Parentheses can hold a brief aside or notation clarification. Fragments are occasional conversational landings, not mandatory decorations.

Joshua's no-em-dash rule governs our writing. The source's occasional stretched spelling, all caps, ellipses, or exclamations are observations, not a punctuation recipe. Repeated “Awesome!”, “Cool,” “That's it,” or “NO!” would exaggerate the surface while weakening the teaching.

## Word choice and useful verbal habits

### Ordinary language carries the formal idea

The source retains sufficient condition, necessary condition, contrapositive, indicator, variable, and stimulus. It gives these words a familiar handle and shows what they mean. It does not hide the vocabulary the student will need.

The conversational words are often functional: “have to,” “need,” “what happens,” “what do you know,” “the other side,” “what's left,” “this fits.” They describe a move the reader can make.

| Word or phrase function | Source tendency | Writing instruction |
|---|---|---|
| Certainty | must, has to, necessary, no way around | Preserve the exact logical strength, then demonstrate it. |
| Possibility | might, could, may, we don't know | Say what remains open and why the rule leaves it open. |
| Drawing a consequence | so, that means, that implies, this tells me | Put the actual reason immediately before the consequence. |
| Giving a reason | because, since | Explain the matching relationship, not merely “because that's the rule.” |
| Inviting attention | let's look, notice, check, imagine | Name the thing to inspect or the fact to change. |
| Naming a boundary | but, that said, sometimes, unless | Keep the qualification beside the advice it limits. |
| Recovering context | so far, return to, now that | Remind the reader of the specific idea the new step depends on. |
| Testing an interpretation | this fits, does that follow, what would I need | Compare the proposed meaning with the whole original sentence. |

Do not replace natural speech with “identify the guaranteed outcome,” “activate the sufficient trigger,” or “execute the conditional reasoning procedure” when the learner needs an explanation. Technical wording earns its place by helping them think.

Joshua's browser corrections, 2026-10-03: define “guarantee” as the certainty supplied by the stated rule before using it to explain another idea. Explain “direction” through an actual relationship before using the word as advice. A beginner needs to see what the arrow means, which complete idea belongs on each side, and why sentence order need not match diagram order. “Read left to right” alone does not teach that. Introduce sufficient as enough and necessary as required through the example, then develop their limits.

For rule versus fact, make the hypothetical explicit: a rule tells us what would follow **if** a condition were true. It does not report that the condition happened. Contrast “Suppose this order is marked ready” with “This order is marked ready” before drawing a conclusion about an actual case. Keep the anchor sentence idiomatic and complete. Emphasis and motion around “if” should reveal that hypothetical relationship, with equally clear static information when motion is reduced.

### Technical names arrive at the useful moment

The source gives a simple relationship before dissecting sufficient and necessary. It explains the contrapositive in ordinary terms before presenting the flip-and-negate shortcut. It explains why symbols help before building the first diagram. The label arrives when there is a concrete idea to attach it to.

This does not mean withholding a definition for suspense. Start with what the topic is. Then give the learner an experience that makes its vocabulary meaningful. A name should settle a distinction the reader has seen.

### Small verbal irregularities create a person

The source sometimes says “little squiggle,” “little bracket cage,” “for real,” or “a bunch of different ways.” It also occasionally uses elevated words such as “ballyhoo” or “cumbersome.” This combination makes the prose human without eliminating precision.

For our material, choose the natural word for the situation. Do not manufacture slang or rare vocabulary to imitate personality. Do not import profanity, dismissive reactions, or author-specific tics. The recognizable quality should be a teacher's thoughtful judgment, not a checklist of eccentric phrases.

## Questions that make the reader think

Questions are one of the source's main ways of controlling pace. They have several distinct functions. Choose the function before writing the question.

| Kind of question | What the source does | What our version should do |
|---|---|---|
| **Prediction before a reveal** | Coffee/walking thought experiment asks what the reader knows, gives a blank, and says STOP. | Ask a bounded question with enough information to answer. Wait for an attempt before showing the reasoning. |
| **One step inside a worked example** | Asks whether the sufficient condition could occur without the necessary burden. | Let the reader see the missing connection, then answer it in the walkthrough. |
| **Meaning check** | Personalized if/then asks what the speaker knows in a proposed situation. | Read the original sentence naturally and test whether the proposed diagram preserves it. |
| **Tempting reverse** | Tries the wrong sufficient condition and shows the interpretation failing. | Make the attractive wrong choice visible without ridiculing it. Explain the unsupported inference. |
| **Reader objection** | FAQ voices worry about time, confidence, and whether to write. | Use the learner's actual objection, then explain the decision criterion. |
| **Notation clarification** | Asks about parentheses and abbreviations. | Answer small questions where the notation first becomes relevant. |
| **Reason for practice** | Explains why the drill asks for written contrapositives. | Say what ability the task checks and why practice may be more explicit than test-day work. |
| **Boundary test** | Four-situation chart asks what has to happen in each case. | Compare the strongest justified answer with what could still be true. |

An exposition question can receive an immediate answer. A student question intended to train reasoning needs an actual pause. Do not ask “What do you think?” and reveal the answer in the same breath. Do not add a rhetorical question to every paragraph merely to sound interactive.

A useful check has three parts: a precise prompt, a learner decision, and a reasoned explanation. For example:

> **Rule:** Every blue-sticker parcel has been inspected.
>
> **Fact:** This parcel has been inspected.
>
> Does the parcel have to have a blue sticker? Explain what the rule says about that direction.

The feedback should identify the gap: the rule covers blue-sticker parcels; it hasn't said all inspected parcels have a blue sticker. “Incorrect. Review sufficient and necessary” is too thin.

## Transitions explain why the next move follows

The source's transitions keep the learner oriented. “So far” recovers what is established. “Let's return” reuses an anchor. “Now that we have a handle on” names the prerequisite for notation. “This leads me to the next question” connects a practical worry to its answer.

A transition should answer one of these:

1. What have we established that the next idea uses?
2. What is still unclear?
3. What will we change while holding the rule fixed?
4. Why is the next representation useful now?

Original transitions:

> We know what the sticker tells us. Now let's see what the inspection stamp tells us.
>
> These sentences use different words, but they describe the same relationship. Let's write that relationship once.
>
> The arrow is pointing the right way. Check one more thing: did we keep the whole condition?

Do not fill the gap with a curriculum announcement such as “Next, we will explore necessary conditions.” Say why the requirement is the next thing worth examining.

## Repetition changes the reader's view

The breathing/air anchor appears in the opening, indicator families, contrapositive, fact chart, diagram, and target analysis. The reader keeps the same relationship while the representation changes. That saves the effort of understanding a new scenario at every turn.

Within the contrapositive explanation, the source repeats the requirement in several forms: air is needed; breathing has to have air; without air the requirement is missing; the original condition cannot hold. Each repetition supplies a step or a new angle.

For our writing, reuse an anchor while doing different work:

1. Read the ordinary meaning.
2. Apply it to a case.
3. Identify the sufficient and necessary ideas.
4. Compare an unsupported reverse.
5. Reword the same relationship.
6. Show the diagram after its meaning is understood.

Introduce a fresh example for independent practice. The learner should not be able to answer only because the lesson already solved that sentence.

A repeated “Keep this” slogan without new reasoning is not explanatory repetition. Nor is rendering the same instructions twice in different panels. See the demonstrated corrections in [COPY-CORRECTIONS](../wiki/COPY-CORRECTIONS.md).

## Metaphor gives the reader a usable handle

The source uses doors, families, activation, burdens, brackets, and putting yourself in a condition's shoes. Each metaphor gives an abstract operation a physical or social handle. A small set of terms recurs, so the reader can keep using it.

Transfer the method of choosing a metaphor, not the particular inventory. Prefer a familiar image that helps answer a logical question. Explain its literal meaning immediately. Remove it when it adds more things to remember than it clarifies.

For instance, a ticket can illustrate a requirement only if the rule is stated carefully. “A ticket is required to enter” does not say a ticket guarantees entry. An analogy fails if it smuggles in that reverse.

Do not build a competing metaphor for every sentence. Do not invent branded names for every skill. The standard technical terms remain the stable vocabulary.

## Humor reduces tension without changing the rule

The source's coffee obsession makes an unusual absolute rule memorable. Its reluctant-student aside acknowledges the experience of being asked to guess. The imagined green checkmark gives the reader's desire for certainty a recognizable shape.

Humor can occur inside an explanation when it makes the thought easier to understand. Joshua's source selection supersedes the earlier blanket restriction that wit must stay outside the explanation. It does not authorize mockery, forced slang, exaggerated praise, or an expanding comic story.

Use a light aside only when it helps the reader inhabit a situation or relax around a specific mistake. Keep it optional. A paragraph should still teach clearly if the aside is removed. Do not use the source's distinctive jokes as templates to be copied.

Never turn first-person source anecdotes into Joshua's biography. “I ask every student this” requires that Joshua actually does. “Students always learn better this way” requires evidence or a narrower, truthful statement.

## Emphasis and visuals identify the decision

Bold in the source often marks the rule, an inference, a warning, or an instruction. Italics distinguish examples and sometimes emphasize a word that changes the contrast. A table lets the reader compare controlled variations. A STOP asks the reader to attempt something. A side note answers a local question without interrupting the main line for everyone.

For our material:

- Bold the relationship or phrase that controls the next decision. Do not bold most of a paragraph.
- Give an example its own space. The student should be able to find the sentence currently under discussion.
- Put comparisons side by side when the same rule or wording is being contrasted.
- Reveal diagram construction one meaningful step at a time. Explain each placement in English.
- Preserve a readable text equivalent for every visual. The illustration is an explanation, not a substitute for one.
- Make an interactive pause real. Let the learner choose when to reveal the reasoning.
- Give a side note a specific job: answer a question, define new notation, or qualify advice. Avoid a second full lesson in a callout.

Joshua's notation correction still applies: teaching arrows should look like teaching notation, not little implementation-code boxes. Clear ordinary typography and meaningful labels should communicate the relationship.

The source's adjusted HTML design is not a visual brand template. Our bootcamp should preserve the established Intro to LR student flow while using original visuals that serve the current concept. Student pages show the assigned work and the lesson itself; internal stage planning, assigned-start metadata, production notes, and selectable difficulty controls do not belong in the teaching prose.

## The complete source progression and depth

This map records what the supplied excerpt does in order. It is not an assertion that every future lesson should cover every listed topic.

### 1. The relationship comes before its parts

**The Art of the If/Then**, HTML lines 142 to 154, opens with a plain definition, a single familiar rule, a literal interpretation, several statements of certainty, and a reason to understand the relationship beneath varied language. The transition promises to take the same example apart.

The depth comes from making “must” meaningful before presenting a list of words. The reader learns what kind of relationship they are looking for. The source also connects certainty to prior “Power Players” material, which is not included in this attachment. Our standalone introduction must supply any needed context itself.

**Transfer:** first explain what a conditional says and why its direction matters. Then give a concrete example. Do not open with a diagramming task or make the definition a label attached after a quiz.

### 2. Sufficient is explained through presence and absence

**The Sufficient Condition**, lines 156 to 199, contains a definition, the recurring anchor, a metaphor, the possibility that the sufficient condition does not occur, a contrast between its absence and presence, and a clarification of “activation.” It then shows **eight indicator rows with eleven displayed example forms**, all expressing the same anchor relationship, and explains why language recognition cannot replace understanding.

This is more than “sufficient goes left.” The text develops what the condition does, what it does not establish, and how wording changes around it.

**Transfer:** give sufficient its own explanation, contrasting cases, and nearby practice. A left-side label is useful only after “enough for what?” has a clear answer.

### 3. Necessary is developed as a requirement

**The Necessary Condition**, lines 201 to 230, defines the other part, reuses the same rule, explains the requirement, and shows **ten indicator rows with eleven displayed forms**. It compares the varied wording with the sufficient-family statements and gives special attention to “only if.” It ends by explaining why symbols help isolate the relationship.

The source uses the ordinary force of “necessary,” “required,” and “need” to make the technical term familiar. It does not jump directly to a memorized side rule.

**Transfer:** explain what is required and why having a requirement is not the same as satisfying every condition for the result. Teach “if” and “only if” using a controlled comparison. Keep the target of each expression clear.

### 4. The contrapositive is discovered before it is manipulated

**The Contrapositive**, lines 234 to 285, first recovers the original relationship. It introduces a new coffee/walking thought experiment, removes the required condition, asks for a prediction, and explicitly pauses. The answer is derived from the unmet requirement. The term is then named, the original anchor revisited, and a four-situation chart sets limits on inference.

The order is important: meaning, prediction, reason, name, comparison. The flip-and-negate shortcut appears later, after the reader has a reason to believe the result.

**Transfer:** preserve this order when contraposition is actually in scope. Do not rush the full procedure into the opening definition merely because it appears early in this source.

### 5. Notation is introduced as a solution to varied wording

**How to Diagram Conditionals**, lines 289 to 388, recovers the indicator families, explains the arrow, gives a four-step construction procedure, and builds one diagram in stages. It then explains negation, displays four original/contrapositive sign patterns, gives the shortcut, and distinguishes writing a contrapositive from reading one when needed.

The first diagram is narrated: notice the indicator, place its target, then complete the other side. Its final appearance is not used as evidence that the learner understands how to make it.

**Transfer:** show each operation and its reason. Preserve Joshua's method and meaningful labels rather than copying the source's four-step count or abbreviation rule.

### 6. Practical objections receive substantial answers

**Quick Conditional Diagramming FAQ**, lines 390 to 433, has five questions:

| Reader question | Depth of the answer | Reusable teaching value |
|---|---|---|
| When should I diagram? | Three paragraphs distinguish early training from later judgment, discuss difficult wording and chains, and address avoidance caused by uncertainty. | Give a criterion, a reason, and an honest account of the learning process. |
| How do I know the diagram is correct? | Three paragraphs connect confidence with checked work and studying specific errors. | Teach a verification habit instead of offering confidence as a substitute for one. |
| Does diagramming take too long? | Two paragraphs explain the proposed tradeoff and separate learning from speed. | Acknowledge the concern; explain why the slower practice step is useful now. |
| Should I always write the contrapositive? | Two paragraphs qualify the advice. A separate note explains why the drills still require it. | Distinguish training evidence from the eventual performance habit. |
| How should I abbreviate? | Gives a short example, a complete diagram, and advice about compact labels. | Make the notation choice purposeful and readable. Joshua's label guidance governs where it differs. |

This is unusually important to the overall tone. The writer gives practical uncertainty enough room to be answered. It is not squeezed into one generic reassurance.

### 7. A useful shortcut is shown, then limited

**Figuring Out The Language**, lines 437 to 471, defines an indicator's target and brackets the target in familiar sentences. It first shows the common placement immediately after the indicator. Then it gives five examples in which the target comes before the indicator, followed by three paired wording variations showing that the same indicator can switch position.

The sequence deliberately prevents an attractive mechanical generalization. The reader sees why adjacency often helps and why it cannot replace reading the sentence.

**Transfer:** teach the indicator's complete target. Change clause order while holding meaning steady. Include one placement trap only after the usual case is understood. Mark the obscured final line as missing evidence.

### 8. A translation is checked by trying both interpretations

**Your Friend, the Personalized If/Then**, lines 475 to 508, defines the check, explains its purpose, gives four steps, establishes the ordinary meaning of a new sentence, and works through a correct guess. It then deliberately tries the reversed guess and explains why that meaning fails.

This adds a verification strategy after notation. It also models the experience of being uncertain without abandoning the task. The teacher does not merely say “use your intuition”; the reader sees how to test an interpretation.

**Transfer:** use the imagined situation as a meaning check. Preserve who or what the sentence concerns. Personalization is optional if pretending to be an object makes the wording less natural.

### 9. Practice changes the language while keeping the skill

**Basic Conditional Drill**, lines 512 to 563, gives supportive instructions and six themed items, with original and contrapositive diagrams displayed in the supplied HTML. The task is the same across varied sentence forms. The source says the adapted sentences come from Shakespeare and points to an answer key not included here.

**Transfer:** original practice can have a memorable theme, but it should change only what the learner is ready to handle. Reuse the skill, not the source's exercise wording. Show answers after an attempt and explain why each diagram preserves the complete ideas. Do not treat the displayed annotations as an independently verified answer key.

The six items do not cover every expression in the source's own indicator tables. In particular, they do not independently train only, only if, depends, or most other necessary expressions. Their displayed diagrams also lack step-by-step explanations. Our Group 1/2 practice needs broader expression coverage and reasoned feedback; the source's basic set alone would not establish that coverage.

| **Group 2** | Joshua's Group 2 expressions identify the necessary idea; target and force must be read in context. | Teach the requirement first, compare “if” with “only if,” then develop each expression's target and nuances. | Separate Group 2 practice, then a controlled Group 1/2 comparison when both are learned. |

These are curriculum planning rows. Student-facing headings should teach the actual concept, such as **What does this sentence require?** They should not expose “Joshua's assigned starting stage,” internal sequencing metadata, or a difficulty selector. Joshua assigns the appropriate material; the learner works through that assigned lesson and its practice.

### Give an idea several teachable moments

The source rarely handles a significant concept with one definition and one answer. It may need a literal explanation, a familiar example, a variation, a pause, a mistaken interpretation, a correction, and a short practice task.

For Group 1, that could become several small lessons: recognizing “if,” locating the complete if-clause, reading an if-clause at the end of the sentence, handling a universal expression, and checking that the diagram preserves the meaning. Group 2 deserves its own development. Do not flatten all expressions into a word table and assume coverage.

Keep micro-assignments possible. A student who needs “only if” should be able to receive that work without repeating every beginner concept. A general assignment can connect the lessons into a full sequence. Beginner, medium, and advanced describe teacher-assigned work; optional timing belongs to medium and advanced practice after accuracy is established.

### Preserve the real indicator groups

The current reviewed opening materials use:

- **Group 1:** if, when, where, all, the only, every, any.
- **Group 2:** only, only if, only when, only where, always, requires, must.

See the [reviewed opening source constraints](../brainstorms/2026-09-30-conditional-curriculum/notebooklm-opening-assets/sufficient-necessary-infographic-prompt.txt). These lists are current project teaching groups, not a claim that every occurrence of each word mechanically diagrammed in isolation has the same function. The supplied source's two families have different members. Do not merge inventories without checking Joshua's notes and scope.

“The only” especially requires its complete grammatical target. The only people admitted are members means **Admitted → Member**. Only members are admitted means the same relationship, reached through a different expression. The lesson must show the meaning rather than encourage a next-word shortcut.

### Preserve Joshua's five steps

In [Sandra's 2026-09-28 transcript](../students/sandra/transcript-downloads/2026-09-28-sandra-notes-by-gemini.md), at 00:43:22, Joshua gives this sequence: identify the indicator; label the two ideas; apply the left/right rule; find the contrapositive; say it back in English. This is a summary of his sequence, not a verbatim quotation. At 00:37:08, he stresses understanding and reading the diagram rather than retaining bare letters.

The supplied source has four construction steps. Do not quietly replace Joshua's five with those four, or claim a shortened opening exercise has taught all five. In a gradual introduction, teach the operations whose prerequisites have been established and identify the full contrapositive step when that lesson is reached. Keep English readback visible as a meaning check throughout.

### Carry these improvements forward

1. **Define before demonstrating.** Start by explaining what a conditional says. Let the example make the definition concrete.
2. **Explain the purpose through a decision.** Show how direction changes the conclusion the learner may draw. Avoid unsupported prevalence statistics.
3. **Keep one relationship stable while changing the view.** Ordinary sentence, case fact, roles, wording variation, and diagram should fit together.
4. **Make each pause a useful thinking task.** Ask for a prediction or explanation, then reveal the reason.
5. **Show a mistake being detected.** A wrong arrow becomes understandable when its English meaning fails the original sentence.
6. **Teach the target before the placement shortcut becomes a habit.** Complete ideas and their qualifications matter.
7. **Explain why the drill exists.** The learner should know what ability they are practicing.
8. **Use fresh practice.** Worked examples teach the method; new items show whether the method transfers.
9. **Give practical objections an answer.** Time, confidence, notation, and when to write deserve specific guidance.
10. **Keep each operation behind its prerequisites.** Under the 2026-10-03 sequence, conceptual contraposition follows both condition roles and families, and precedes notation. Chains, Groups 3/4 and quantities still receive later development. The source does not contain those complete later curricula.

## Accuracy and method boundaries

Writing that sounds like the source must still preserve correct logic and Joshua's chosen method.

| Source simplification or context | Boundary for our material |
|---|---|
| “Ignore the conditional” when sufficient is absent or necessary is present. | For this one rule and fact, no new conclusion about the other condition follows. The rule remains available for later facts and chains. Never teach that it disappears. |
| Necessary has to happen “at some point.” | A conditional is not necessarily a time sequence or causal claim. Prefer “has to be true if the sufficient condition is true.” |
| The original and contrapositive are “all you know.” | The central warning is against an unsupported reverse or inverse. Equivalent formulations and other supplied rules still matter. State the immediate inference limits precisely. |
| Required words are described as obligations. | Preserve modality. “Interviewees must bring ID” can assert an obligation, not prove compliance. Diagram the stated requirement unless the facts establish compliance. |
| Indicator usually comes before target. | Read its full grammatical target. Targets can precede the indicator, and familiar words can serve other roles in context. |
| Shorten conditions to one or two letters, never more than four. | Joshua's clear condensed labels govern. Keep negation, scope, qualifiers, and readability. A short label that loses meaning is not an improvement. |
| Try a personalized sufficient guess. | Compare with the whole ordinary meaning and look for a counterexample. A feeling that an arrow is plausible is not enough. |
| Original and contrapositive training. | Explain why a drill requires explicit work and when test-day practice may differ. Do not turn a training requirement into a universal performance rule. |
| “Always” is in a necessary-family teaching list. | Establish the sentence's subject and relationship. It is not a universal instruction to place the next word on the right. |
| Confidence guarantees the diagram is correct. | Confidence can grow from practice, but correctness rests on preserving meaning and valid reasoning. Provide checks. |
| Timing and learning claims are absolute. | Treat them as the author's advice unless independently supported. Give a practical explanation without invented evidence. |
| References to Logic Games, a book, page 148, or named source tools. | Preserve the teaching function only where our course has an actual equivalent. Do not import obsolete or unavailable context. |
| A theme makes a drill memorable. | Keep each sentence logically interpretable and its answer reviewed. Novelty must not obscure the condition or require untaught knowledge. |

For the parcel example, three possible cases distinguish the permitted relationship from an invalid reverse:

- Blue sticker and inspected: fits the rule.
- Inspected without a blue sticker: also fits the rule.
- Blue sticker without inspection: breaks the rule.

The rule does not itself say any such parcel exists. Examples of possible cases illustrate consistency, not existence claims. This distinction matters when moving from individual facts to general rules.

## Calibration examples for different LSAT surfaces

All examples below are original drafts for this reference. They demonstrate writing mechanisms; they are not assigned work or approved curriculum.

### Explain sufficient and necessary without rushing

> Every parcel with a blue sticker has been inspected.
>
> Suppose yours has a blue sticker. That's enough to know it has been inspected. You don't need another fact about the parcel. The rule has already told you what must be true of every parcel with that sticker.
>
> We call the blue sticker the **sufficient condition**. Sufficient means enough. Here, enough to establish inspection under this rule.
>
> Inspection is the **necessary condition**. A blue-sticker parcel has to meet that requirement. Otherwise it would be an exception to a rule that allows no exceptions.
>
> Now suppose a parcel has been inspected. Does it have to have a blue sticker?
>
> We still don't know. Inspection was required for the blue-sticker parcels. The shop never said those were the only parcels it inspected.

This returns to meaning at every term. The example does not assert why the shop uses stickers or when they are applied. Those extra facts are unnecessary.

### Teach a necessary expression through its whole meaning

> Editing this newsletter requires an account.
>
> What does the sentence say the editor needs? An account. Keep that complete idea on the required side of the relationship:
>
> **Edits this newsletter → Has an account**
>
> Read it back: if someone edits this newsletter, they have an account. That matches the sentence.
>
> Try the reverse. If someone has an account, do they necessarily edit the newsletter? The sentence hasn't told us that. An account could belong to someone who only reads it.

The final possible counterexample is introduced as something consistent with the sentence, not an asserted fact about this system. Technical placement has a semantic reason.

### Give a flashcard a reasoning task

**Front**

> Every package in Locker 4 has been scanned. A package has been scanned. Must it be in Locker 4? Why?

**Back**

> No. The rule tells us about packages already in Locker 4. It doesn't say every scanned package goes there.

This is more useful than a card that only asks “Necessary condition = which side?” Keep simple recall cards when recall is the actual skill; do not mistake that for complete reasoning practice.

### Make feedback identify the missed connection

> You used the sentence in the reverse direction. “Only registered visitors enter the archive” requires registration for entry. It hasn't said every registered visitor enters. Read the diagram as **Enters → Registered** and compare it with the sentence.

The feedback names the mistake, shows the relevant meaning, and gives a repair. It avoids generic praise, mockery, and labels such as “weak reasoning.”

### Explain an ordinary learning objection

> “I understood the sentence. Why write it down?”
>
> With one short rule, you may not need to. When a passage gives you several relationships, a diagram lets you compare them without rereading the wording each time. In this exercise, write the relationship so we can check that you kept its direction and complete ideas.

The answer gives a decision criterion and distinguishes the purpose of this exercise from a blanket rule about every test question.

### Transfer the voice to another LSAT topic

> A conclusion is the claim the author is asking you to accept. The other statements are supposed to support it.
>
> Nora says, “The library should stay open later because every evening seat was taken this week.” What is she trying to persuade us of?
>
> That the library should stay open later. The full seats are her reason. She expects that reason to support the proposed change.
>
> You don't have to agree with her. First, separate what she wants you to accept from what she gives you in support.

The same mechanism works outside conditionals: definition, small example, question, explanation, and a useful boundary. No conditional topic has been imported into the conclusion lesson.

## Revise the draft in a useful order

### First, check the reasoning and scope

List what is given, what follows, and why. Try to construct a case that satisfies the given information but makes the proposed conclusion false. If that case is possible, revise the logic before polishing the voice. Check modality, negation, quantifier scope, and whether the learner has actually been taught every required operation.

For a rewrite, preserve Joshua's established method and explicit lesson scope. The new reference governs delivery. It does not authorize removing content that students need or adding source topics the current assignment does not cover.

### Then, check the learner's experience

Read as someone who has never seen the notation. Can they tell what the concept is? Can they answer the first question with what the page has taught? Does each new term refer to an idea they have seen? Does the page tell them why the correct answer follows?

Put the explanation beside the point where it is needed. Do not rely on an interaction to imply a distinction the prose never explains. A student who cannot use the animation must still be able to learn from the text.

### Then, listen to the voice

Read aloud at an ordinary teaching pace. Replace stiff phrasing with the words the teacher would naturally use. Keep the intermediate thoughts. Vary sentence length where the task changes. Remove repeated catchphrases, inflated enthusiasm, branded metaphors, and irrelevant asides.

Do not repair generic prose by scattering “Cool,” “Let's,” or “That's it” through it. Repair the missing reasoning, the abrupt transition, or the abstract example.

### Finally, verify the student surface

Check the actual prompt, answer state, labels, buttons, diagrams, and explanations. A good note can be undermined by a shell that exposes internal planning or reveals the answer too soon. Preview desktop and mobile when the interface changes. Keep the instructor's planning map separate from what the learner sees.

## Use this rubric before handing over LSAT writing

Score each dimension from 0 to 2: **0** absent or misleading; **1** partly present but needing revision; **2** clear and usable. This is an editorial tool, not a validated learning measure. A logical error or scope violation requires revision regardless of the total.

| Dimension | What a strong draft does |
|---|---|
| **Ordinary meaning** | Defines the idea in language the reader can understand before notation. |
| **Concrete anchor** | Uses an original, small, unambiguous situation and states needed assumptions. |
| **Visible reasoning** | Shows the intermediate connection instead of asserting a result. |
| **Point of view** | Keeps the reader involved; uses teacher narration purposefully. |
| **Cadence** | Gives complex thoughts room and short conclusions a clear place to land. |
| **Vocabulary** | Keeps useful technical terms, explains them, and avoids internal jargon. |
| **Questions and pauses** | Asks a genuine decision and gives an appropriate chance to answer. |
| **Boundaries** | Shows what the information leaves open and why a tempting move fails. |
| **Progression** | Each operation has a prerequisite, a reason to appear, and adequate practice. |
| **Transfer** | Fresh questions require the taught skill, with reasoned feedback. |
| **Tone and warmth** | Expects effort, supports the next action, and never mocks uncertainty. |
| **Presentation** | Examples, comparisons, notation, and interactions clarify the reasoning. |

Do not treat a high total as proof of an identical voice. Use the original source and Joshua's response to calibrate the subjective feel. His concrete corrections override a score.
