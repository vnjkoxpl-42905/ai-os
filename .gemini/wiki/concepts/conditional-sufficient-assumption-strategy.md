# Conditional Sufficient Assumption Strategy

A Sufficient Assumption question asks for an unstated premise that, when combined with the provided evidence, mathematically guarantees the truth of the conclusion (100% validity). 

In contrast to Necessary Assumption questions (which test minimum requirements and lean toward provable, weak answers), Sufficient Assumption questions belong to the powerful family: you want an answer strong enough to force the conclusion into existence.

## The Mechanical Structure of Conditional SA Questions

Most conditional Sufficient Assumption questions fall into predictable algebraic templates:

### 1. The Head-to-Tail Bridge
- **Premise 1:** A → B
- **Premise 2:** B → C
- **Conclusion:** A → D
- **Missing Link (SA):** C → D (or its contrapositive, ~D → ~C)

### 2. The Trigger Fact + Rule
- **Premise (Rule):** A → B
- **Premise (Fact):** X is true (where X aligns with an unstated condition)
- **Conclusion:** B is true
- **Missing Link (SA):** X → A (activating the rule)

### 3. The Definition Equation
- An argument equates two terms without formally defining them (e.g. "possessing more money" vs "wealthier").
- **Missing Link (SA):** Equate the premise condition with the conclusion condition as an absolute rule.

## Execution Rules for Students

1. **Find the Conclusion and Premises first:** Do not diagram until the target conclusion is identified.
2. **Inspect the Conclusion's Terms:**
   - Look for any "new" or rogue term in the conclusion that never appeared in the premises.
   - That rogue term MUST be accounted for in the correct sufficient assumption.
3. **Prephrase the Bridge:**
   - Mentally or physically write down the connection: `[Last Premise Term] → [New Conclusion Term]`.
4. **Anticipate the Contrapositive:**
   - Test writers often express the bridge as its contrapositive: `~[New Conclusion Term] → ~[Last Premise Term]`.
5. **Trap Answers to Eliminate:**
   - **Illegal Reversal:** `[Conclusion Term] → [Premise Term]` (fails to activate the conclusion).
   - **Illegal Negation:** `~[Premise Term] → ~[Conclusion Term]`.
   - **Too Weak:** Answers using "some" or "most" cannot guarantee a conditional outcome.
   - **Missing the Rogue Term:** An answer choice that talks about premise relationships without touching the conclusion's new concept is powerless.
