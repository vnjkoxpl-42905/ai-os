### Instructions

1. Make a spreadsheet with two columns, or use this template :

   1. **Column 1**: High Density

   2. **Column 2**: Low Density
2. Complete an LR section*  using any workout, but with one small adjustment: Write your Mission in either the High-Density or Low-Density column in the spreadsheet.

3. Choose which column to place your Mission into based on how information-dense your Mission is.

   1. The High Density column is where you write your high information density Missions.

      1. A Mission with high information density will use words and concepts from the stimulus directly without referential phrasing, glazing over the details, and characterizing information ("all that stuff") to avoid having to say it directly.

      2. **Example Mission in the High-Density Bucket**: Whether there actually is more food in the ocean tropics versus the freshwater tropics
   2. The Low Density column is where you write your low information density Missions.

      1. A Mission with low information density will use referential phrasing ("this," "that," "it," "they," "the conclusion," etc.) to gloss over the direct words and concepts from the stimulus, omit the details like specific nouns and verbs, and group information together with broad characterizations ("good things").

      2. **Example Mission on the Same Question in the Low-Density Bucket**: Food is important
4. After you complete the section, review the spreadsheet to note which questions produced Low-Density Missions.

5. Return to the questions that produced Low-Density Missions the next day and try to make a new higher density Mission before you Camo the section.

## Stop Oversimplifying

### What problem are we solving?

There is no such thing as "*just* a detail" on the LSAT.

Ignoring "details" leads to missing question through oversimplifying. If you run with an inaccurate, oversimplified version of the text, you're bound to miss questions.

Answers are right and wrong based on "just one word." That doesn't make the LSAT mean, evil, or unfair. It makes the LSAT a test of precision.

And precision is something you'd to have when you're a lawyer, right? You don't want to be the attorney who harms their client because you missed "*just* one word."

Every word on the LSAT deserves equal levels of respect, as it works with every other word to create a conceptual Translation of each sentence.

Conceptual Translation can be the single biggest differentiator between high and lower scorers. It is the opposite of oversimplifying. When you ignore some words in an answer choice and focus solely on other words (keywording), you're not conceptually translating that answer. You're oversimplifying and massively increasing your risk of missing the question.

Keywording reduces an answer's fate solely to whether it has certain words from the stimulus.

However, there are many correct answers that contain no words from the stimulus. There are many wrong answers that contain tons of words from the stimulus.

You cannot oversimplify answer choice selection to a shallow matching game and expect the LSAT to tolerate it. It won't.

But this is just one way oversimplification decreases your score. There are approximately one million other ways oversimplification decreases your score.

This Accuracy Fix trains you to embrace complexity because your mind is strong enough to handle it. Luckily, this is something you were trained to do in the Translation Prep Stage. Now is the time to put that training into action, especially in the answer choices.

That's how we stop you from missing so many questions.

Instructions

Make a spreadsheet with two columns, or use this template:

Column 1: High Density

Column 2: Low Density

Complete an LR section* using any workout, but with one small adjustment: Write your Mission in either the High-Density or Low-Density column in the spreadsheet.

Choose which column to place your Mission into based on how information-dense your Mission is.

The High Density column is where you write your high information density Missions.

A Mission with high information density will use words and concepts from the stimulus directly without referential phrasing, glazing over the details, and characterizing information (&quot;all that stuff&quot;) to avoid having to say it directly.

Example Mission in the High-Density Bucket: Whether there actually is more food in the ocean tropics versus the freshwater tropics

The Low Density column is where you write your low information density Missions.

A Mission with low information density will use referential phrasing (&quot;this,&quot; &quot;that,&quot; &quot;it,&quot; &quot;they,&quot; &quot;the conclusion,&quot; etc.) to gloss over the direct words and concepts from the stimulus, omit the details like specific nouns and verbs, and group information together with broad characterizations (&quot;good things&quot;).

Example Mission on the Same Question in the Low-Density Bucket: Food is important

After you complete the section, review the spreadsheet to note which questions produced Low-Density Missions.

Return to the questions that produced Low-Density Missions the next day and try to make a new higher density Mission before you Camo the section.
