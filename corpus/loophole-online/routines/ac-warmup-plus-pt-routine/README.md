#### Answer Choice Warmup

##### Instructions

1. Open up a previously-seen LR or RC section* .

2. Translate the first four questions' answer choices in the style of a Written Answer Choice Translation Spreadsheet.

   1. Read only the answer choices for these four questions.

      1. Do not read the stimulus or the question stem.
   2. Write down each individual answer choice.

   3. Write down a Translation of each answer choice.

      1. This means read A, write the literal text of A, and write the Translation of A. Then repeat the same process on B, C, D, and E.
   4. Use the same spreadsheet and same style as you used when you completed the Answer Choice Translation Primer.
3. Translate the next four questions' answer choices in the style of an Out Loud Answer Choice Translation Drill.

   1. Read only the answer choices in the section.

      1. Do not read the stimulus.

      2. Do not read the question stem.
   2. Translate the answer choice out loud.

   3. Quip the answer choice out loud.

      1. This means read A, translate A, and quip A. Then repeat the same process on B, C, D, and E.

### Ellen's Advice

#### Why Are We Doing This?

We're taking a PT to practice exactly how we will perform on test day. Luckily, the process we use on test day and during PTs has been meticulously honed throughout approximately one million other Workouts you've performed throughout your prep.

This is the PT Routine for all our Answer Choice Translation Primer fans out there. For some test takers, the Answer Choice Translation Primer is an absolute game changer and reviving the Primer on just four questions at a time raises the score on the section, PT, or test day that follows.

Just like the Primer, the Answer Choice Warmup forces you to acknowledge every word of every answer, a great habit to reinforce as you head immediately into answering questions.

Notice that there is no Camo + Wrong Answer Journal in this Routine. That is because I want you to focus on performance when you take the PT and focus on review when you have enough emotional distance to view your performance objectively (and therefore get much more improvement out of your review).

You might counter that you need to remember your rationale for why you chose an answer when you review and that means you need to review your PT same day.

I would counter that you'll remember your rationale just fine, and this is an excuse so you can rush your review, check your answers, and get the external validation of a score faster. You'll get more out of your PT by spending your subsequent prep day investing in the PT Off Day Routine.

Show Less

#### How This Should Be Done

When you take a PT, make sure that you're treating the experience exactly the same way you treat every other LSAT prep activity you do.

PTs aren't special. Test day isn't special. PTs should not be taken "more seriously" than a Translation Drill, a Mini-Cycle, a Thunderdome, or anything else you do in your prep.

Treat your warmup just as seriously as you treat the PT itself. How you do anything is how you do everything. No breaks between the warmup and the start of the PT.

When you treat every piece of LSAT preparation with the significance of a PT, you allow even investment across *all* your practice. This makes a PT much less of a departure from your normal process, which makes the score on the PT less of a departure from your normal score.

The point of taking a PT is to do everything exactly the same way you've practiced. Don't do anything special just because we're calling it a PT.

### Stop Oversimplifying

#### What problem are we solving?

There is no such thing as "*just* a detail" on the LSAT.

Ignoring "details" leads to missing question through oversimplifying. If you run with an inaccurate, oversimplified version of the text, you're bound to miss questions.

Answers are right and wrong based on "just one word." That doesn't make the LSAT mean, evil, or unfair. It makes the LSAT a test of precision.

And precision is something you'd to have when you're a lawyer, right? You don't want to be the attorney who harms their client because you missed "*just* one word."

Every word on the LSAT deserves equal levels of respect, as it works with every other word to create a conceptual Translation of each sentence.

Conceptual Translation can be the single biggest differentiator between high and lower scorers. It is the opposite of oversimplifying. When you ignore some words in an answer choice and focus solely on other words (keywording), you're not conceptually translating that answer. You're oversimplifying and massively increasing your risk of missing the question.

Keywording reduces an answer's fate solely to whether it has certain words from the stimulus.

However, there are many correct answers that contain no words from the stimulus. There are many wrong answers that contain tons of words from the stimulus.

You cannot oversimplify answer choice selection to a shallow matching game and expect the LSAT to tolerate it. It won't.

But this is just one way oversimplification decreases your score. There are approximately one million other ways oversimplification decreases your score.

This Accuracy Fix trains you to embrace complexity because your mind is strong enough to handle it. Luckily, this is something you were trained to do in the Translation Prep Stage. Now is the time to put that training into action, especially in the answer choices.

That's how we stop you from missing so many questions.

Ellen&#x27;s Advice
Why Are We Doing This?

We&#x27;re taking a PT to practice exactly how we will perform on test day. Luckily, the process we use on test day and during PTs has been meticulously honed throughout approximately one million other Workouts you&#x27;ve performed throughout your prep.

This is the PT Routine for all our Answer Choice Translation Primer fans out there. For some test takers, the Answer Choice Translation Primer is an absolute game changer and reviving the Primer on just four questions at a time raises the score on the section, PT, or test day that follows.

Just like the Primer, the Answer Choice Warmup forces you to acknowledge every word of every answer, a great habit to reinforce as you head immediately into answering questions. 

Notice that there is no Camo + Wrong Answer Journal in this Routine. That is because I want you to focus on performance when you take the PT and focus on review when you have enough emotional distance to view your performance objectively (and therefore get much more improvement out of your review).

You might counter that you need to remember your rationale for why you chose an answer when you review and that means you need to review your PT same day.

I would counter that you&#x27;ll remember your rationale just fine, and this is an excuse so you can rush your review, check your answers, and get the external validation of a score faster. You&#x27;ll get more out of your PT by spending your subsequent prep day investing in the PT Off Day Routine.

SHOW LESS
How This Should Be Done

When you take a PT, make sure that you&#x27;re treating the experience exactly the same way you treat every other LSAT prep activity you do.

PTs aren&#x27;t special. Test day isn&#x27;t special. PTs should not be taken &quot;more seriously&quot; than a Translation Drill, a Mini-Cycle, a Thunderdome, or anything else you do in your prep.

Treat your warmup just as seriously as you treat the PT itself. How you do anything is how you do everything. No breaks between the warmup and the start of the PT. 

When you treat every piece of LSAT preparation with the significance of a PT, you allow even investment across all your practice. This makes a PT much less of a departure from your normal process, which makes the score on the PT less of a departure from your normal score.

The point of taking a PT is to do everything exactly the same way you&#x27;ve practiced. Don&#x27;t do anything special just because we&#x27;re calling it a PT.
