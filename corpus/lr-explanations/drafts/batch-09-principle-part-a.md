# Batch 09, part A: Principle questions, PT102 to PT120

Twenty-three of the 40 Principle gaps. Most are in the database with no explanation. PT108-S3-Q16 is missing only its C note and PT119-S2-Q15 only its E note. PT118-S1-Q9 and PT118-S3-Q8 have steps on the standard explanation but no notes, so they get five notes each and no new steps.

Modelled on `models/Principle.md`: put the judgment on the right side of the rule, check the outcome first and the trigger second, and give the most-picked trap the fullest note.

Labels: a rule in the stimulus that the answers apply gets Rule / Evaluate / Goal. A situation that the answers generalize gets Situation / Evaluate / Goal. An argument that a principle must fit or justify gets Conclusion / Evidence / Evaluate / Goal.

## PT102-S2-Q11 | Principle-Conform | D

> A Habitat Conservation Plan (HCP) is based on a law that allows developers to use land inhabited by endangered species in exchange for a promise to preserve critical habitat or provide replacement land nearby. Some individuals of endangered species are lost in return for assurances by the owner or developer that habitat for those remaining animals will be protected. Environmentalists are pleased that HCPs allow them to win concessions from developers who would otherwise ignore rarely enforced environmental laws. Satisfied property owners prefer HCPs to more restrictive prohibitions of land use.
>
> The situation described above most closely conforms to which one of the following principles?
>
> (A) In order to avoid protracted legal battles environmentalists should compromise with developers.
> (B) Developers should adhere only to those environmental laws that are not overburdensome.
> (C) Laws should not be designed to serve the interests of all the parties concerned since they are often so weak that no one's interest is served well.
> (D) Laws should be fashioned in such a way as to reconcile the interests of developers and environmentalists.
> (E) The most effective means of preserving endangered species is to refrain from alienating property owners.

### Argument Breakdown

**Situation:** Under an HCP, developers get to use land where endangered species live. In return, they protect critical habitat or provide replacement land. Environmentalists like it because they win concessions from developers who would otherwise ignore weak laws. Property owners like it because it beats stricter bans.

**Evaluate:** The last two sentences are the payoff: both sides are satisfied. Each gives something up and each gets something it wants. A law that leaves opposing parties both pleased is a law built to balance their interests.

**Goal:** A principle about laws that reconcile the interests of developers and environmentalists.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Not Described.** Nobody mentions legal battles. The compromise here comes from the design of a law, not from environmentalists trying to stay out of court.

**(B) Not Described.** Developers here give up land and make promises. Nothing says they get to skip laws they find burdensome.

**(C) Opposite.** The HCP serves the interests of both parties, and both are satisfied. This principle says laws shouldn't try to do that.

**(D) Correct.** Developers get to build, environmentalists get habitat protection, and both are pleased. That is a law fashioned to reconcile their interests.

**(E) Too Strong.** "The most effective means" ranks every option for saving species. The passage describes one arrangement that pleases everyone. It never compares it to anything else or says species fare best this way.

## PT102-S4-Q14 | Principle Conform | D

> We have a moral obligation not to destroy books, even if they belong to us. The reason is quite simple: If preserved, books will almost certainly contribute to the intellectual and emotional enrichment of future generations.
>
> Which one of the following most accurately expresses the principle underlying the argument?
>
> (A) It is morally incumbent upon us to devote effort to performing actions that have at least some chance of improving other people’s lives.
> (B) We are morally obligated to preserve anything that past generations had preserved for our intellectual and emotional enrichment.
> (C) The moral commitments we have to future generations supersede the moral commitments we have to the present generation.
> (D) We are morally obligated not to destroy anything that will most likely enrich, either intellectually or emotionally, our posterity.
> (E) Being morally obligated not to destroy something requires that we be reasonably assured that that thing will lead to the betterment of someone we know.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** We have a moral obligation not to destroy books, even our own.

**Evidence:** If preserved, books will almost certainly enrich future generations, intellectually and emotionally.

**Evaluate:** The argument moves from "this thing will very likely enrich people who come after us" to "we're obligated not to destroy it." The principle has to have that fact on the left and the obligation not to destroy on the right: if something will very likely enrich posterity, we must not destroy it.

**Goal:** If a thing will most likely enrich future generations, we are morally obligated not to destroy it.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Obligation.** This is the trap, picked by 14%. It sounds generous and moral, but look at what it obligates: devoting effort to performing actions. The conclusion is a duty to refrain, not to perform. A also lowers the bar to "at least some chance", while the evidence says "almost certainly". Even if we accept A, we only learn we should work on helpful projects. We still don't have a rule about destroying anything.

**(B) Wrong Trigger.** The evidence is about what books will do for the future, not about what past generations saved for us. Nothing says these books were preserved by anyone before us.

**(C) No Conflict.** The argument never sets future generations against the present one. Keeping your own book doesn't cost the present generation anything the argument mentions.

**(D) Correct.** "Will most likely enrich" matches "almost certainly contribute to the enrichment", "posterity" matches "future generations", and the right side is exactly the conclusion: an obligation not to destroy. Apply it to books and the conclusion follows.

**(E) Opposite.** This requires that the thing benefit "someone we know." Future generations are people we will never meet, so E would actually block the conclusion.

## PT104-S4-Q1 | Principle-Conform | C

> Politician: Governments should tax any harmful substance that is available to the general public at a level that the tax would discourage continued use of the substance.
>
> Which one of the following is an application of the politician’s principle of taxation?
>
> (A) The tax on products containing sugar is raised in an effort to raise revenue to be applied to the health costs resulting from the long-term use of these products.
> (B) The tax on certain pain relievers that, even though harmful, are available over the counter is raised, since studies have shown that the demand for these products will not be affected.
> (C) The tax on a pesticide that contains an organic compound harmful to human beings is raised to give people an incentive to purchase pesticides not containing the compound.
> (D) The tax on domestically produced alcoholic beverages is not raised, since recent studies show that the tax would have a negative impact on the tourist industry.
> (E) The tax on products that emit fluorocarbons, substances that have proven to be harmful to the earth’s ozone layer, is lowered to stimulate the development of new, less environmentally harmful ways of using these substances.

### Argument Breakdown

**Rule:** If a substance is harmful and available to the general public, the government should tax it at a level that discourages continued use.

**Evaluate:** Two things to check in each choice. Is the substance harmful and available to the public? And is the tax set to discourage use? The purpose matters. Raising money, helping tourism or encouraging new technology are other goals.

**Goal:** A tax raised on a harmful, public substance so that people use less of it.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Purpose.** The tax goes up to raise revenue for health costs. The rule sets the tax to discourage use, and A never says use should fall.

**(B) Opposite.** The studies say demand won't be affected, so this tax can't discourage use. That's the one effect the rule cares about.

**(C) Correct.** The pesticide contains a compound harmful to people, the tax is raised, and the point is to push buyers toward pesticides without it. Higher tax, less use of the harmful product. That's the rule in action.

**(D) Not an Application.** The tax isn't raised at all, and the reason is tourism. The rule never weighs tourism.

**(E) Opposite.** The tax on a harmful product is lowered. The rule calls for a tax high enough to discourage use.

## PT104-S4-Q3 | Principle-Conform | A

> A just government never restricts the right of its citizens to act upon their desires except when their acting upon their desires is a direct threat to the health or property of other of its citizens.
>
> Which one of the following judgments most closely conforms to the principle cited above?
>
> (A) A just government would not ban the sale of sports cars, but it could prohibit unrestricted racing of them on public highways.
> (B) An unjust government would abolish many public services if these services did not require compulsory labor.
> (C) A just government would provide emergency funds to survivors of unavoidable accidents but not to survivors of avoidable ones.
> (D) A just government would not censor writings of Shakespeare, but it could censor magazines and movies that criticize the government.
> (E) An unjust government would incarcerate one of its citizens even though it had been several years since that citizen harmed someone.

### Argument Breakdown

**Rule:** A just government never restricts citizens from acting on their desires, except when acting on them directly threatens other citizens' health or property.

**Evaluate:** Put the judgment on the right. If a just government restricts something, that activity directly threatens others' health or property. If an activity poses no such threat, a just government leaves it alone. The rule only tells us what a just government does. It says nothing about what an unjust government would do.

**Goal:** A judgment where a just government allows the harmless activity and restricts only the one that directly threatens others.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Buying a sports car threatens no one, so a just government wouldn't ban the sale. Racing it without limits on public highways directly threatens other people's health and property, so restricting that fits the exception. Both halves of A line up with the rule.

**(B) Out of Scope.** Abolishing services that don't require labor isn't restricting anyone's desires. And the rule never says what an unjust government would do.

**(C) Out of Scope.** Handing out emergency funds isn't a restriction. The rule says nothing about who gets aid.

**(D) Violates Rule.** Magazines and movies that criticize the government don't directly threaten anyone's health or property. A just government couldn't censor them.

**(E) Wrong Kind of Judgment.** E predicts what an unjust government would do. The rule only describes just governments, so it can't tell us how an unjust one behaves.

## PT106-S3-Q23 | Principle-Conform | D

> Ethicist: It is widely believed that it is always wrong to tell lies, but this is a rule almost no one fully complies with. In fact, lying is often the morally appropriate thing to do. It is morally correct to lie when telling the truth carries the reasonable expectation of producing considerable physical or psychological harm to others.
>
> Which one of the following most closely conforms to the principle the ethicist endorses?
>
> (A) When Juan asked Judy if the movie they were going to was North by Northwest, Judy said yes, though she knew that Persona was playing instead. This was the only way Juan would see the film and avoid losing an opportunity for an aesthetically pleasing experience.
> (B) A daughter asked her father which candidate he supported, McBride or Chang. Though in fact he preferred Chang, the father responded by saying he preferred McBride, in order to avoid discussion.
> (C) A husband told his wife he felt ready to go on a canoe trip, though he had recently had severe chest pains; his wife had determined a year ago that they would go on this trip, so to ask to cancel now would be inconvenient.
> (D) A young boy asked his mother if she loved his older sister more than she loved him. The mother said she loved them both to the same degree, even though it was not true.
> (E) A friend invited Jamal to a party, but Jamal was afraid that he might see his ex-wife and her new husband there. To spare himself emotional pain, as well as the embarrassment of telling his friend why he did not want to go, Jamal falsely claimed he had to work.

### Argument Breakdown

**Rule:** If telling the truth carries a reasonable expectation of considerable physical or psychological harm to others, it is morally correct to lie.

**Evaluate:** Three things have to be true. The truth must cause harm, the harm must be considerable, and it must fall on someone other than the liar. Missing a dull movie or an awkward chat doesn't count.

**Goal:** A lie told because the truth would considerably hurt someone else.

### Choice Notes

**(A) No Harm.** Juan would miss an "aesthetically pleasing experience." Missing a good film isn't considerable physical or psychological harm.

**(B) No Harm.** The father lies to dodge a discussion. An argument about candidates isn't the harm the rule requires.

**(C) Harm to Self.** The husband lies to avoid inconvenience. If anyone is at risk, it's the husband, who recently had severe chest pains. The lie creates danger rather than preventing harm to others.

**(D) Correct.** Hearing that the mother loves the older sister more would plausibly cause the boy serious psychological harm. That harm falls on someone other than the liar. So the lie fits the rule.

**(E) Harm to Self.** This is the trap, picked by 12%. Jamal does face emotional pain, and that looks like the rule's psychological harm. But the rule protects others. Jamal lies to avoid personal pain and embarrassment. Read the rule's last two words carefully: "to others."

## PT107-S4-Q10 | Principle-Conform | C

> Parents should not necessarily raise their children in the ways experts recommend, even if some of those experts are themselves parents. After all, parents are the ones who directly experience which methods are successful in raising their own children.
>
> Which one of the following most closely conforms to the principle that the passage above illustrates?
>
> (A) Although music theory is intrinsically interesting and may be helpful to certain musicians, it does not distinguish good music from bad: that is a matter of taste and not of theory.
> (B) One need not pay much attention to the advice of automotive experts when buying a car if those experts are not interested in the mundane factors that concern the average consumer.
> (C) In deciding the best way to proceed, a climber familiar with a mountain might do well to ignore the advice of mountain climbing experts unfamiliar with that mountain.
> (D) A typical farmer is less likely to know what types of soil are most productive than is someone with an advanced degree in agricultural science.
> (E) Unlike society, one’s own conscience speaks with a single voice; it is better to follow the advice of one’s own conscience than the advice of society.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Parents shouldn't necessarily raise their children the way experts recommend.

**Evidence:** Parents directly experience which methods work with their own children.

**Evaluate:** The principle: someone with direct experience of the particular case may do better to set aside the advice of general experts. Notice the soft wording. Parents "should not necessarily" follow the experts. The author isn't saying experts are always wrong.

**Goal:** A person with firsthand knowledge of a specific case who might do well to ignore experts who lack that knowledge.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Different Reason.** Theory fails here because good music is a matter of taste. The stimulus never says parenting is a matter of taste. It says parents know their own children firsthand.

**(B) Different Reason.** The experts are dismissed because they don't care about what matters to ordinary buyers. Our experts are set aside because they lack direct experience of this particular child, not because they don't care.

**(C) Correct.** The climber knows this particular mountain, and the experts don't. "Might do well to ignore" matches "should not necessarily" follow. Firsthand knowledge of the specific case beats general expertise.

**(D) Opposite.** This sides with the expert over the person who works the land.

**(E) Different Reason.** This is the trap, picked by 9%. It does favor your own voice over outside advice. But the reason is that conscience "speaks with a single voice." That's about consistency, not firsthand experience. E also says it is "better to follow" conscience, which is stronger than the stimulus's "not necessarily."

## PT108-S3-Q16 | Principle-Conform | C

> To act responsibly in one’s professional capacity, one must act on the basis of information that one has made a reasonable effort to make sure is accurate and complete.
>
> Which one of the following judgments most closely conforms to the principle cited above?
>
> (A) Peggy acted responsibly in ordering new computers for the school last year because they turned out to be needed due to an unexpected increase in enrollment this year.
> (B) Mary acted responsibly in firing John, for she first examined the details of his work record and listened to negative reports from some of his supervisors and coworkers.
> (C) Toril did not act responsibly in investing the company’s money in Twicycled Ink, for, though the investment yielded a large return, she had not investigated the risks associated with that investment.
> (D) Conchita did not act responsibly in hiring Helmer to do the company’s bookkeeping because Helmer made a mistake that cost the company a lot of money, though he had never been known to make such a mistake in the past.
> (E) Jennifer did not act responsibly in deciding where to go on her vacation because, instead of carefully weighing her options, she waited until the last minute and decided on impulse.

### Choice Notes

**(C) Correct.** The conclusion says Toril did not act responsibly, so this choice is worth reading. Does the evidence say Toril didn't make a reasonable effort to get accurate and complete information? Yes: Toril invested the company's money without investigating the risks. Investing company money is a professional act, so the principle applies. The large return doesn't matter. The rule judges the effort made before acting, not how things turned out.

## PT109-S3-Q6 | Principle | C

> The recent cleaning of frescoes in the Sistine Chapel has raised important aesthetic issues. Art historians are now acutely aware that the colors of the works they study may differ from the works’ original colors. Art historians have concluded from this that interpretations of the frescoes that seemed appropriate before the frescoes’ restoration may no longer be appropriate.
>
> Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the art historians’ reasoning?
>
> (A) The appropriateness of an interpretation of an artwork is relative to the general history of the period in which the interpretation is made.
> (B) The restoration of an artwork may alter it such that it will have colors that the artist did not intend for it to have.
> (C) The colors of an artwork are relevant to an appropriate interpretation of that work.
> (D) Art historians are the best judges of the value of an artwork.
> (E) Interpretations of an artwork are appropriate if they originated during the period when the work was created.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Interpretations of the frescoes that seemed appropriate before the restoration may no longer be appropriate.

**Evidence:** The cleaning showed that the colors of the works historians study may differ from the original colors.

**Evaluate:** The evidence is about colors, and the conclusion is about interpretations. To bridge that gap, colors have to matter to interpretation. If they didn't, a change in color couldn't unsettle anyone's reading of the frescoes.

**Goal:** A principle that says an artwork's colors bear on how it should be interpreted.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Link.** This ties interpretation to the history of the period when it was made. The evidence is about color, so A doesn't connect.

**(B) Repeats the Evidence.** This is the trap, picked by 10%. It's true to the stimulus: restoration can change colors. But that's the premise we already have. B never mentions interpretation, so it doesn't explain why old interpretations might no longer hold.

**(C) Correct.** If colors are relevant to an appropriate interpretation, then learning the colors were different gives a reason to doubt interpretations based on the old colors. Evidence about color now reaches the conclusion about interpretation.

**(D) Out of Scope.** The argument is about whether interpretations are appropriate, not about who judges an artwork's value.

**(E) Out of Scope.** This judges interpretations by when they started. That has nothing to do with the colors changing.

## PT111-S1-Q7 | Principle-Conform | C

> Due to wider commercial availability of audio recordings of authors reading their own books, sales of printed books have dropped significantly.
>
> Which one of the following conforms most closely to the principle illustrated above?
>
> (A) Because of the rising cost of farm labor, farmers began to make more extensive use of machines.
> (B) Because of the wide variety of new computer games on the market, sales of high-quality computer video screens have improved.
> (C) Because a new brand of soft drink entered the market, consumers reduced their consumption of an established brand of soft drink.
> (D) Because a child was forbidden to play until homework was completed, that child did much less daydreaming and focused on homework.
> (E) Because neither of the two leading word processing programs has all of the features consumers want, neither has been able to dominate the market.

### Argument Breakdown

**Situation:** Audio recordings of authors reading their own books became more widely available, and sales of printed books dropped significantly.

**Evaluate:** An audiobook does the same job as a printed book: you get the book. So the principle is substitution. When a new substitute becomes widely available, people buy less of the product it replaces.

**Goal:** A new substitute arrives, and consumption of the existing product falls.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Cause.** Farmers did switch to machines. But the cause is that labor got more expensive. In the stimulus, nothing happened to the old product. A substitute just became easier to get.

**(B) Opposite.** New games boost sales of screens. Those products work together, and sales rise. The stimulus has a substitute pulling sales down.

**(C) Correct.** A new soft drink enters the market, and people drink less of the established brand. A new substitute arrives, and the old product sells less. Same pattern.

**(D) Wrong Mechanism.** A rule forbids playing, and the child focuses on homework. That's a restriction, not a substitute becoming available.

**(E) Wrong Mechanism.** Neither program dominates because neither has every feature. No new substitute arrives, and no sales fall.

## PT111-S3-Q5 | Principle | B

> Parent: I had tried without success to get my young child to brush her teeth. I had hoped that she would imitate me, or that she would be persuaded by reason to brush her teeth. Then, I made a point of brushing her teeth for her immediately before reading her a story before her naps and at night. After several weeks, when I would pick up a storybook at these times, she began automatically to retrieve her toothbrush and brush her teeth herself.
>
> The parent’s experience with the child most closely conforms to which one of the following generalizations?
>
> (A) Children are most effectively taught to do something by someone’s setting an example.
> (B) Children more readily adopt a behavior through habit and repetition than through other means.
> (C) Children are too young to understand rational arguments for adopting a behavior.
> (D) Children often imitate the behavior of others rather than listening to reason.
> (E) Children ordinarily act contrary to their parents’ expectations in order to get more attention.

### Argument Breakdown

**Situation:** The parent tried setting an example and tried reasoning, and neither worked. Then the parent brushed the child's teeth every time, right before a story. After several weeks, picking up a storybook was enough to make the child brush unprompted.

**Evaluate:** The story eliminates two methods, imitation and reason, and credits a third: the same routine repeated until it became automatic. Whatever generalization fits has to favor habit over those other means.

**Goal:** Children adopt a behavior through habit and repetition more readily than through example or reason.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Opposite.** The parent hoped the child would imitate. It didn't work.

**(B) Correct.** Example and reason failed, and repetition worked. "More readily than through other means" is exactly what the parent saw.

**(C) Too Strong.** Reasoning failed with this child, but we don't know why. Maybe the child understood and just didn't care. "Too young to understand" makes a claim about children's minds that the story never supports.

**(D) Opposite.** The child didn't imitate the parent. And D's contrast between imitation and reason misses the method that actually worked.

**(E) Not Described.** Nothing suggests the child wanted attention or acted against expectations on purpose.

## PT112-S1-Q5 | Principle-Conform | A

> Company policy: An employee of our company must be impartial, particularly when dealing with family members. This obligation extends to all aspects of the job, including hiring and firing practices and the quality of service the employee provides customers.
>
> Which one of the following employee behaviors most clearly violates the company policy cited above?
>
> (A) refusing to hire any of one's five siblings, even though they are each more qualified than any other applicant
> (B) receiving over a hundred complaints about the service one's office provides and sending a complimentary product to all those who complain, including one's mother
> (C) never firing a family member, even though three of one's siblings work under one's supervision and authority
> (D) repeatedly refusing to advance an employee, claiming that he has sometimes skipped work and that his work has been sloppy, even though no such instances have occurred for over two years
> (E) promoting a family member over another employee in the company

### Argument Breakdown

**Rule:** Employees must be impartial, especially with family members. This covers everything about the job, including hiring and firing and the quality of service to customers.

**Evaluate:** Impartial means family status makes no difference, either way. Treating relatives worse is as partial as treating them better. To prove a violation, a choice has to show family status driving the decision. A choice where family might simply deserve the outcome doesn't prove anything.

**Goal:** A decision that clearly turns on someone being family, against the merits.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** All five siblings are more qualified than every other applicant, and still none are hired. The only thing that explains the decision is that they're family. That's partial, just in the unexpected direction.

**(B) Impartial.** Everyone who complained gets the same gift, the employee's mother included. Family gets no special treatment.

**(C) Not Clear.** This is a trap, picked by 7%. Never firing three siblings sounds suspicious. But maybe none of them ever deserved firing. Without knowing they performed badly, we can't call this partial.

**(D) No Family.** This might be unfair, since the problems are over two years old. But the policy targets partiality, and nothing ties this employee to any family relationship. The problems also did happen, so the claims aren't false.

**(E) Not Clear.** This is the most-picked trap, at 10%. Promoting a relative over someone else is only partial if the relative wasn't the better choice. E gives no comparison. The relative may well have earned it. A, by contrast, tells us the relatives were the better choice.

## PT115-S4-Q24 | Principle-Conform | E

> A park's user fees are employed to maintain the park. When fewer people use the park, it suffers less wear. Thus raising user fees improves park maintenance even if the number of people who stop using the park because of higher fees is great enough to reduce the revenues devoted to maintenance.
>
> Which one of the following conforms most closely to the principle illustrated by the statements above?
>
> (A) To increase its market share, a car company improves the service warranty it provides to those who purchase a new car. Making good on the warranties proves expensive enough that the company's profits decrease even though its market share increases.
> (B) A grocery store's overall revenues increase even though it no longer remains open 24 hours daily. The manager theorizes that customers find the store more pleasant because it can be cleaned well during the hours it is closed.
> (C) Road taxes are raised to encourage more people to use mass transit. But since the fee paid by each commuter does not equal the cost of providing transit for that commuter, a mass transit service will deteriorate even as it takes in more money.
> (D) By spending more on zoo maintenance, a city increases the number of zoo patrons. The extra revenue generated by the sale of memorabilia more than makes up for the extra costs of maintenance.
> (E) Library fees have been increased to raise money for book repair. Since the library now has fewer patrons, the books are in better repair even though the number of library patrons has decreased to such an extent that the money available for book repair has decreased.

### Argument Breakdown

**Situation:** Raising a park's user fees drives some people away. Fewer users means less wear, so maintenance improves. This holds even if so many people leave that the money for maintenance actually drops.

**Evaluate:** The chain is: raise the fee, lose users, less wear, better condition. The twist is in the last clause. The condition improves even though the money for upkeep goes down. Less use matters more than less money.

**Goal:** A fee increase that drives users away, so the thing ends up in better shape despite less money for its upkeep.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Different Chain.** Better warranties raise market share and cut profits. No fee drives users away, and nothing is wearing out less.

**(B) Different Chain.** The store closes overnight, not raising a fee, and its revenue rises. In the stimulus, revenue can fall while the condition improves.

**(C) Opposite.** Here more money comes in, and the service still gets worse. That reverses the stimulus, where less money and better condition go together.

**(D) Different Chain.** More spending brings more visitors, and the extra revenue covers the cost. Usage goes up, not down.

**(E) Correct.** Library fees rise, there are fewer patrons, and the books are in better repair even though the money for repairs has dropped. Every link matches, including the twist that less money still means better condition.

## PT116-S3-Q3 | Principle-Conform | B

> In an experiment, tennis players who were told that their performance would be used to assess only the quality of their rackets performed much better than an equally skilled group of tennis players who were told that their tennis-playing talent would be measured.
>
> The situation described above most closely conforms to which one of the following propositions?
>
> (A) People do less well on a task if they have been told that they will be closely watched while doing it.
> (B) People execute a task more proficiently when they do not believe their abilities are being judged.
> (C) People perform a task more proficiently when they have confidence in their abilities.
> (D) People who assess their talents accurately generally perform near their actual level of proficiency.
> (E) People who think that a superior performance will please those who are testing them generally try harder.

### Argument Breakdown

**Situation:** Two equally skilled groups of tennis players. One was told only their rackets were being assessed. The other was told their talent was being measured. The first group played much better.

**Evaluate:** The groups are matched on skill, so the only difference is what they believed was being judged. When players thought their own ability was on the line, they played worse.

**Goal:** People perform better when they don't think their abilities are being judged.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Not the Difference.** This is the trap, picked by 8%. It sounds right, since being watched can make people nervous. But both groups were being watched. What differed was what the players thought was being evaluated: the racket or themselves.

**(B) Correct.** The group that thought only the racket was being judged did better. That's people performing better when they don't believe their abilities are being judged.

**(C) Not Described.** Both groups were equally skilled, and nothing tells us anyone's confidence.

**(D) Not Described.** Nobody here assesses their own talent.

**(E) Not Described.** Nobody is trying to please the testers. E also predicts more effort, while the stimulus is about how well people played.

## PT116-S3-Q5 | Principle-Conform | E

> Art critic: The aesthetic value of a work of art lies in its ability to impart a stimulating character to the audience's experience of the work.
>
> Which one of the following judgments most closely conforms with the principle cited above?
>
> (A) This painting is aesthetically deficient because it is an exact copy of a painting done 30 years ago.
> (B) This symphony is beautiful because, even though it does not excite the audience, it is competently performed.
> (C) This sculpted four-inch cube is beautiful because it is carved from material which, although much like marble, is very rare.
> (D) This painting is aesthetically valuable because it was painted by a highly controversial artist.
> (E) This poem is aesthetically deficient because it has little impact on its audience.

### Argument Breakdown

**Rule:** A work of art's aesthetic value lies in its ability to make the audience's experience stimulating.

**Evaluate:** The rule ties value to one thing: the effect on the audience. A judgment conforms if it praises or faults a work because of how it affects its audience. A judgment that rests on the artist, the material, or originality uses some other standard.

**Goal:** A judgment of a work's value based on its effect on the audience.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Standard.** Being a copy is about originality. An exact copy could still give an audience a stimulating experience.

**(B) Opposite.** The symphony doesn't excite the audience, yet it's called beautiful because it's performed competently. By the critic's standard, failing to stimulate is the problem.

**(C) Wrong Standard.** The cube is praised for a rare material. That says nothing about the audience's experience.
