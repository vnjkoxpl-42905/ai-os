## PT142-S1-Q1 | Most Strongly Supported | C

> Children should be discouraged from reading Jones’s books. Reading them is like eating candy, which provides intense, short-term sensory stimulation but leaves one poorly nourished and dulls one’s taste for better fare. In other words, the problem with letting children read Jones’s books is that _______.
>
> Which one of the following most logically completes the argument above?
>
> (A) it will lead them to develop a taste for candy and sweets
> (B) too many children may become frustrated by their difficulty and stop reading altogether
> (C) their doing so interferes with the development of appreciation for more challenging literature
> (D) their message may undermine the positive teaching done by parents
> (E) children may become so enthralled with books that they will want to spend all their time reading

### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** Children shouldn't read Jones's books. Reading them is like eating candy: a strong, quick thrill that leaves you poorly nourished and dulls your taste for better food.

**Evaluate:** The blank starts "In other words", so it restates the analogy in plain terms. Map each piece: candy = Jones's books, poor nourishment = not much to gain, a dulled taste for better fare = less appetite for better books.

**Goal:** Reading Jones dulls children's taste for better books.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** The candy is a comparison. Nobody says the books make children want actual sweets.

**(B) Opposite.** Candy is easy and pleasant. The analogy suggests the books are too easy, not too hard.

**(C) Correct.** "Dulls one's taste for better fare" maps onto interfering with appreciation of more challenging literature.

**(D) Out of Scope.** The books' message and parents' teaching never come up.

**(E) Out of Scope.** The worry is which books children come to like, not reading too much.

## PT142-S1-Q9 | Paradox | E

> Many calcium supplements contain lead, a potentially dangerous substance even in small amounts. The body can safely store in bones trace amounts of lead from food, but high levels of lead in the blood are a major public health concern, associated with anemia and nerve damage. Despite this, many doctors contend that for some people calcium supplements containing lead are preferable to no calcium supplements at all.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, would most help to resolve the apparent discrepancy in the information above?
>
> (A) Some fruits and vegetables contain trace amounts of lead derived from the soil in which they are grown.
> (B) It is difficult to ensure that one has completely eliminated trace amounts of lead from one’s diet.
> (C) Lead is only one of the common public health concerns that are associated with anemia and nerve damage.
> (D) A high-calcium diet decreases the amount of lead that the body is able to tolerate safely.
> (E) When calcium intake is insufficient, the body draws calcium from bones, releasing stored lead into the bloodstream.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** Many calcium supplements contain lead, which is dangerous even in small amounts, and high blood lead causes anemia and nerve damage.

**How can it be that:** Many doctors say that for some people, supplements containing lead are better than no supplements at all?

**Evaluate:** Notice the detail about bones: the body safely stores trace lead in bones. What if going without calcium shakes that lead loose? Then skipping supplements could put *more* lead in the blood than taking them would.

**Goal:** A way that going without calcium also raises blood lead, and by more.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** Lead in food doesn't explain why a leaded supplement would beat no supplement.

**(B) Out of Scope.** It's hard to avoid trace lead in food. That still gives no reason to add more through supplements.

**(C) Out of Scope.** Other causes of anemia and nerve damage don't make lead any safer.

**(D) Deepens Paradox.** If calcium lowers how much lead the body can handle, calcium supplements with lead look even worse.

**(E) Correct.** Without enough calcium, the body pulls it from bone and releases the stored lead into the blood. So no supplement can mean more blood lead than a supplement with a little lead.

## PT143-S3-Q4 | Most Strongly Supported | D

> The size of the spleen is a good indicator of how healthy a bird is: sickly birds generally have significantly smaller spleens than healthy birds. Researchers found that, in general, birds that had been killed by predators had substantially smaller spleens than birds killed accidentally.
>
> Which one of the following is most strongly supported by the information above?
>
> (A) Predators are unable to kill healthy birds.
> (B) Most birds with smaller than average spleens are killed by predators.
> (C) Predators can sense whether a bird is sick.
> (D) Sickly birds are more likely than healthy birds to be killed by predators.
> (E) Small spleen size is one of the main causes of sickness in birds.

### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** Spleen size indicates health: sick birds generally have much smaller spleens. Birds killed by predators had much smaller spleens than birds killed by accidents.

**Evaluate:** Pause and infer. Small spleen means sickly. Predator victims had small spleens. So predator victims were disproportionately sickly. Accidents are a rough sample of the whole population, so sickly birds are overrepresented among the ones predators take.

**Goal:** Sickly birds are more likely to fall to predators.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Too Strong.** "Generally" and "substantially smaller" describe tendencies. Predators surely catch some healthy birds.

**(B) Reversed.** We know predator victims tend to have small spleens. That doesn't mean most small-spleen birds end up killed by predators.

**(C) Unsupported.** Maybe predators sense sickness, or maybe sick birds are just slower. The passage doesn't say how it happens.

**(D) Correct.** Predator victims skew toward small spleens, and small spleens mark sick birds. "More likely" is just as strong as the data.

**(E) Reversed.** Spleen size *indicates* sickness. Nothing says it causes it.

## PT143-S4-Q3 | Most Strongly Supported | A

> Cable TV stations have advantages that enable them to attract many more advertisers than broadcast networks attract. For example, cable stations are able to target particular audiences with 24-hour news, sports, or movies, whereas broadcast networks must offer a variety of programming. Cable can also offer lower advertising rates than any broadcast network can, because it is subsidized by viewers through subscriber fees. Additionally, many cable stations have expanded worldwide with multinational programming.
>
> The statements above, if true, provide support for each of the following except:
>
> (A) Some broadcast networks can be viewed in several countries.
> (B) Broadcast networks do not rely on subscriber fees from viewers.
> (C) Low costs are often an important factor for advertisers in selecting a station or network on which to run a TV ad.
> (D) Some advertisers prefer to have the opportunity to address a worldwide audience.
> (E) The audiences that some advertisers prefer to target watch 24-hour news stations.

### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** Cable stations attract more advertisers than broadcast networks because they can target audiences with 24-hour news, sports, or movies; they offer lower rates thanks to subscriber fees; and many have expanded worldwide.

**Evaluate:** This is an EXCEPT question, so four answers are supported and one isn't. Each cable advantage implies something. Targeting helps only if some advertisers want those audiences. Lower rates help only if price matters. Worldwide reach helps only if some advertisers want it. And subscriber fees are something cable has that broadcast doesn't.

**Goal:** Find the one claim none of the advantages supports.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Worldwide expansion is listed as a *cable* advantage. Nothing supports broadcast networks reaching several countries. If anything, the contrast suggests they don't.

**(B) Supported.** Subscriber fees are what let cable charge less than "any broadcast network". That implies broadcast networks don't have them.

**(C) Supported.** Lower rates count as an advantage for attracting advertisers, so cost matters to advertisers.

**(D) Supported.** Worldwide expansion counts as an advantage, so some advertisers must want a worldwide audience.

**(E) Supported.** Targeting through 24-hour news is an advantage, so some advertisers want those audiences.

## PT143-S4-Q12 | Paradox | C

> After the rush-hour speed limit on the British M25 motorway was lowered from 70 miles per hour (115 kilometers per hour) to 50 miles per hour (80 kilometers per hour), rush-hour travel times decreased by approximately 15 percent.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, most helps to explain the decrease in travel times described above?
>
> (A) After the decrease in the rush-hour speed limit, the average speed on the M25 was significantly lower during rush hours than at other times of the day.
> (B) Travel times during periods other than rush hours were essentially unchanged after the rush-hour speed limit was lowered.
> (C) Before the rush-hour speed limit was lowered, rush-hour accidents that caused lengthy delays were common, and most of these accidents were caused by high-speed driving.
> (D) Enforcement of speed limits on the M25 was quite rigorous both before and after the rush-hour speed limit was lowered.
> (E) The number of people who drive on the M25 during rush hours did not increase after the rush-hour speed limit was lowered.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** The rush-hour speed limit on the M25 dropped from 70 mph to 50 mph.

**How can it be that:** Rush-hour travel times fell by about 15%?

**Evaluate:** Slower driving should mean longer trips. So something other than raw speed must have been costing time before. Think about what high speed causes in heavy traffic: accidents, and accidents cause long delays.

**Goal:** A source of delay that the higher speed limit was causing.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Deepens Paradox.** Lower average speeds make faster trips even more puzzling.

**(B) Out of Scope.** Other times of day don't explain why rush hour got faster.

**(C) Correct.** High-speed driving caused accidents and long delays. Slow the traffic and the delays go away, which more than makes up for the lower speed.

**(D) Deepens Paradox.** If the limits were strictly enforced, drivers really did go slower. That makes the faster trips harder to explain.

**(E) Weaker Impact.** Traffic not *increasing* rules out one reason trips might get slower. It gives no reason they got faster.

> While studying a large colony of macaque monkeys, scientists interacting with baby monkeys under a week old found that the babies would imitate some, but not all, of the scientists’ actions. The babies readily smacked their lips and stuck out their tongues when the scientists did, but stared impassively when the scientists opened and closed their mouths or made hand gestures. Of these four kinds of actions, only lip smacking and sticking out the tongue are used by adult macaques when interacting with babies.
>
> The statements above, if true, most strongly support which one of the following?
>
> (A) Baby macaques under a week old are natural mimics of whatever they see.
> (B) Baby macaques under a week old cannot imitate hand gestures because they do not yet control the necessary muscles.
> (C) Adult macaques use lip smacking and sticking out the tongue to entertain infant macaques.
> (D) Baby macaques under a week old mistake the scientists interacting with them for adult macaques.
> (E) Baby macaques under a week old only imitate human gestures also used by adult macaques.

**Goal:** The babies imitated only the gestures adult macaques also use.

## PT151-S3-Q23 | Paradox | A

**Steps only.** The five existing notes stay as they are.

> Researchers have found that some unprotected areas outside of a national park that was designed to protect birds have substantially higher numbers of certain bird species than comparable areas inside the park.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, most helps to explain the researchers’ finding?
>
> (A) Moose are much more prevalent inside the park, where hunting is prohibited, than outside the park, and moose eat much of the food that the birds need to survive.
> (B) The researchers also found that some unprotected areas outside of the park have substantially higher numbers of certain reptile species than comparable areas inside the park.
> (C) Researchers tagged a large number of birds inside the park; three months later some of these birds were recaptured outside the park.
> (D) Both inside the park and just outside of it, there are riverside areas containing willows and other waterside growth that the bird species thrive on.
> (E) The park was designed to protect endangered bird species, but some of the bird species that are present in higher numbers in the unprotected areas are also endangered.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** A national park was designed to protect birds.

**How can it be that:** Some unprotected areas outside the park have far more of certain bird species than comparable areas inside it?

**Evaluate:** We need a difference between inside and outside that works against the birds inside. Protection from people is one difference, but it could have side effects. Something the park protects might compete with the birds.

**Goal:** Something inside the park that hurts these birds and isn't present, or isn't as strong, outside.

## PT156-S2-Q3 | Paradox | C

> A recent study has found that, surprisingly, the risk of serious injuries to workers is higher in industries that are monitored by government safety inspectors than in industries that are not so monitored.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, most helps to explain the surprising finding described above?
>
> (A) Government safety inspectors not only monitor but also train employees of the inspected firms to follow safe practices.
> (B) Government safety inspectors do not have the authority to enforce safety regulations.
> (C) Only those industries with an inherently high risk of on-the-job injury are monitored by government safety inspectors.
> (D) Workers behave especially cautiously when they believe their performance is being monitored by government safety inspectors.
> (E) Some of the industries that are monitored by government safety inspectors have much lower rates of injuries than do other industries that are also so monitored.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** Some industries are monitored by government safety inspectors.

**How can it be that:** Workers in monitored industries face a *higher* risk of serious injury than workers in unmonitored ones?

**Evaluate:** Don't assume the inspectors cause injuries. Ask which industries get inspectors in the first place. If inspectors are sent to the dangerous ones, the monitored group starts out riskier, and the comparison isn't fair.

**Goal:** A reason the monitored industries were riskier to begin with.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Deepens Paradox.** Inspectors who also train workers in safe practices should make injuries rarer, not more common.

**(B) Weaker Impact.** No enforcement power might explain why inspections don't *help*. It doesn't explain why monitored industries are *worse*.

**(C) Correct.** Only inherently high-risk industries get inspectors. The monitoring follows the danger; it doesn't cause it.

**(D) Deepens Paradox.** More careful workers should mean fewer injuries.

**(E) Out of Scope.** Differences among monitored industries don't explain the gap between monitored and unmonitored ones.

## PT156-S2-Q14 | Most Strongly Supported | D

> When polar ice caps grow (during ice ages, for example), lighter forms of oxygen from water vapor and seawater accumulate in the frozen ice caps, leaving greater concentrations of one heavy form of oxygen behind in the sea, where it is absorbed by marine organisms. When and as the ice caps shrink, the concentrations of this heavy oxygen in seawater decrease. During one 30,000- year period, concentrations of this heavy oxygen in sea shellfish increased for about 20,000 years, then decreased for 10,000 years.
>
> The information given most strongly supports which one of the following hypotheses about the period described above?
>
> (A) Average global temperatures 10,000 years after the beginning of the period approximately equaled average global temperatures 20,000 years later.
> (B) Polar ice caps at the beginning of the period were larger than they were at the end of the period.
> (C) The beginning of the period coincided with the onset of an ice age that lasted approximately 20,000 years.
> (D) The polar ice caps grew for about 20,000 years after the period began, then began to shrink.
> (E) An ice age was drawing to an end during the first 20,000 years of the period.

### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** When ice caps grow, light oxygen gets locked in the ice and heavy oxygen builds up in the sea, where shellfish absorb it. When caps shrink, heavy oxygen in seawater falls. Over one 30,000-year period, heavy oxygen in shellfish rose for about 20,000 years, then fell for 10,000.

**Evaluate:** Pause and infer by running the rule backward. Heavy oxygen rising means the caps were growing. Heavy oxygen falling means they were shrinking. So: about 20,000 years of growth, then 10,000 of shrinking.

**Goal:** The ice caps grew for about 20,000 years, then shrank.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** The passage links oxygen to ice caps, not to temperature figures.

**(B) Unsupported.** Twenty thousand years of growth followed by ten thousand of shrinking doesn't tell us the net. The caps could have ended up bigger, smaller, or the same.

**(C) Too Strong.** The caps grew, but whether an ice age began right at the start of the period is more than the data shows.

**(D) Correct.** It is the oxygen record translated back into ice: up for about 20,000 years, then down.

**(E) Opposite.** During the first 20,000 years the caps were growing, not shrinking.

## PT156-S2-Q17 | Must Be True | D

> To measure a small boat hull accurately, it is necessary to level the hull so that a baseline can be established. This does not require sophisticated tools, but it does require a leveling tool called a “line level.” When scientists discovered a small ninth-century boat, they wanted to record the dimensions of its hull accurately. None of the scientists, however, had any experience measuring hulls. Because of this they were not sure they had leveled the hull before establishing a baseline.
>
> If the statements above are true, then which one of the following must also be true?
>
> (A) The scientists did not accurately measure the hull’s dimensions because they had no experience measuring hulls.
> (B) The scientists accurately determined the hull’s dimensions, provided that they leveled the hull.
> (C) If the scientists did not accurately determine the hull’s dimensions, it was because they did not have a line level.
> (D) The scientists were able to accurately record the hull’s dimensions only if they used a line level.
> (E) If the scientists had measured the hull’s dimensions accurately, then at least one of them would have had experience measuring hulls.

### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** Accurately measuring a hull requires leveling it. Leveling requires a line level. The scientists weren't sure they had leveled the hull.

**Evaluate:** Chain the requirements: accurate measurement → leveled → used a line level. So an accurate measurement requires a line level. Notice what we *don't* know: whether they leveled it or measured accurately.

**Goal:** They measured accurately only if they used a line level.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Unsupported.** They weren't *sure* they leveled it. They might have. We can't conclude the measurement was wrong.

**(B) Reversed.** Leveling is required for accuracy, not enough for it. They could level the hull and still measure badly.

**(C) Unsupported.** A missing line level is one way to fail, not the only one. They could have had one and still erred.

**(D) Correct.** Accurate → leveled → line level. "Only if" states the requirement.

**(E) Unsupported.** Experience is never said to be required. Inexperienced people could get lucky and level it correctly.

## PT156-S4-Q2 | Most Strongly Supported | C

> The relationship between money and the things it allows one to purchase is like that between a tool and the tasks it enables its user to accomplish. Therefore, since tools are useless if there is no task that needs to be done, _______.
>
> Which one of the following most logically completes the argument?
>
> (A) money should be thought of as no more useful than a tool
> (B) any tool that enables its user to accomplish a task is valuable
> (C) money is valueless in a world where nothing is for sale
> (D) money should be regarded as a tool rather than a commodity
> (E) the value of money derives from the tasks it allows one to accomplish

### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** Money is to the things it buys as a tool is to the tasks it does. Tools are useless if there's no task to do.

**Evaluate:** Finish the analogy by swapping terms. Tool → money. Task → something to buy. "Tools are useless if there is no task" becomes "money is useless if there is nothing to buy".

**Goal:** Money has no value if there is nothing to buy.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Unsupported.** The analogy compares relationships. It doesn't rank money's usefulness against tools.

**(B) Reversed.** The premise says a tool with no task is useless. B claims that a tool *with* a task is valuable. That doesn't follow.

**(C) Correct.** Swap the terms into "tools are useless if there is no task" and you get C.

**(D) Out of Scope.** The passage doesn't classify money as a tool or a commodity. It only compares the two relationships.

**(E) Unsupported.** This mixes the two halves of the analogy: tasks belong to tools, purchases to money. And "derives from" is a stronger claim than the passage makes.

## PT156-S4-Q5 | Most Strongly Supported | E

> Gahagan’s Greenstore sells a large volume of plants. The vast majority are sold wholesale to commercial buyers, most of them to landscape contractors. Gahagan’s also sells gardening implements, most of which are purchased retail by home gardeners.
>
> Which one of the following is most strongly supported by the information above?
>
> (A) Gahagan’s makes a larger profit from the sale of plants than from the sale of all other products combined.
> (B) Most of those who make regular wholesale purchases from Gahagan’s have never purchased gardening implements from Gahagan’s.
> (C) There are more commercial buyers than home gardeners among Gahagan’s regular customers.
> (D) Gahagan’s sells a few gardening implements wholesale to landscape contractors.
> (E) A plant purchased from Gahagan’s is more likely to have been purchased by a landscape contractor than by a home gardener.

### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** Gahagan's sells many plants. The vast majority go wholesale to commercial buyers, most of them landscape contractors. Gahagan's also sells gardening tools, most of them bought retail by home gardeners.

**Evaluate:** Pause and infer with numbers. Say 90% of plants go wholesale. "Most of them" go to landscape contractors, so more than 45% of all plants. Home gardeners buy retail, and retail is at most the remaining 10%. So a given plant is far more likely to have gone to a contractor.

**Goal:** A plant from Gahagan's was more likely bought by a landscape contractor than a home gardener.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** Nothing is said about profit.

**(B) Unsupported.** Most *tools* are bought by home gardeners, but that doesn't tell us whether wholesale buyers have ever bought one.

**(C) Unsupported.** The passage gives sales volume, not head counts. A few contractors could buy huge volumes.

**(D) Unsupported.** "Most" tools sell retail leaves some sold otherwise, but we don't know to whom.

**(E) Correct.** Contractors take a large share of the vast majority; home gardeners can only account for part of the small remainder.

## PT156-S4-Q19 | Sufficient Assumption | A

> I promised Bernie that I would answer his question. He asked me whether his project had been approved. I misunderstood and thought he had asked whether I had approved it. I said, truthfully, “I would approve it if I could, but I don’t have the authority to do so—that is up to Dorothy, and she hasn’t yet made a decision.” Thus, I fulfilled my promise to Bernie.
>
> Which one of the following, if assumed, enables the conclusion above to be properly drawn?
>
> (A) All there is to answering a question is giving the questioner the information requested.
> (B) No person can be held accountable for another person’s decisions.
> (C) The person responsible for a decision is the one who should explain the decision.
> (D) One need not fulfill a promise in order to do the best that could be done to fulfill it.
> (E) Making a promise always obliges a person to keep it.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** I fulfilled my promise to answer Bernie's question.

**Evidence:** Bernie asked whether his project was approved. I misunderstood and said I'd approve it if I could, but Dorothy decides and hasn't decided yet.

**Evaluate:** The speaker answered the wrong question, but still told Bernie what he wanted to know: no decision has been made. The conclusion only follows if answering a question is just a matter of giving the information asked for, whatever you thought the question was.

**Goal:** Answering a question = giving the requested information.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** If answering is just giving the requested information, then telling Bernie that Dorothy hasn't decided answers him. The promise is kept.

**(B) Out of Scope.** Accountability for Dorothy's decision has nothing to do with whether Bernie got an answer.

**(C) Out of Scope.** Who should explain a decision doesn't tell us whether this answer counts.

**(D) Opposite.** This is about falling short of a promise. The conclusion says the promise was fulfilled.

**(E) Out of Scope.** That promises should be kept doesn't show this one was.

## PT156-S4-Q21 | Principle-Conform | E

> Company policy: Eligible employees will be granted up to six weeks of paid parental leave following the birth or adoption of a child. To be eligible, an employee must apply for the benefit at least three months prior to the beginning of the leave period and must have at least one year of full- time employment with the company at the time of application.
>
> Which one of the following judgments most closely conforms to the company policy?
>
> (A) Mac is a full-time employee who has been with the company for exactly one year. He just adopted a child, and he applied for parental leave three months ago, so Mac should be granted the leave.
> (B) Sara has worked full-time for the company for three years, and she applied for parental leave four months before the recent birth of her child. However, she should not be granted the leave because she took a leave of absence within the past year.
> (C) Hal, a second-year, full-time employee, applied for parental leave three months ago. He wishes to stay at home for only two weeks following the impending birth of his child, so the company should not grant him parental leave.
> (D) Dudley has worked part-time for the company for the past seven years. He applied for parental leave over three months before the birth of his child, so it should be granted to him.
> (E) Maria has worked full-time for the company for the past two years and applied for parental leave six months prior to her adoption of a child, so she should be granted parental leave.

### Argument Breakdown

**Rule:** An eligible employee gets up to six weeks of paid leave. Eligible means applying at least three months before the leave starts *and* having at least one year of full-time employment when applying.

**Evaluate:** Check each person against both requirements, measured at the time of application. Also watch for reasons to deny that the policy never gives.

**Goal:** Someone who meets both requirements and is granted leave.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Violates Rule.** Mac has one year now, but he applied three months ago, when he had only nine months. He wasn't eligible when he applied.

**(B) Violates Rule.** Sara meets both requirements. A recent leave of absence isn't a reason the policy recognizes.

**(C) Violates Rule.** The policy grants "up to" six weeks, so wanting only two is fine. Denying Hal on that basis doesn't follow the policy.

**(D) Violates Rule.** Dudley has worked part-time. The policy requires full-time.

**(E) Correct.** Maria has two years full-time and applied six months ahead. She meets both requirements, so she gets the leave.

## PT159-S1-Q8 | Strengthen | A

> Scientists found the remains of several species of mites trapped in rocks in a high-altitude cave. Radioactive dating shows that the mites were trapped about 2,000 years ago. The mite species in the rocks no longer live in the vicinity of the cave, but they are found a few miles away in a cooler area. Therefore, the area around the cave was probably cooler 2,000 years ago, since _______.
>
> The conclusion of the argument is most strongly supported if which one of the following completes the passage?
>
> (A) the mite species found in the rocks can only survive in very specific climates
> (B) the cooler area a few miles away from the cave also has a somewhat wetter climate
> (C) the mite species that were trapped in the rocks did not live in the vicinity of the cave much earlier than 2,000 years ago
> (D) the mite species found in the rocks only live at high altitudes
> (E) there are fewer mite species living in the vicinity of the cave today than there were 2,000 years ago

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The area around the cave was probably cooler 2,000 years ago.

**Evidence:** Mites trapped in the cave rock 2,000 years ago no longer live near the cave, but they do live a few miles away in a cooler area.

**Evaluate:** The argument treats the mites as a thermometer: they lived here then, they live somewhere cooler now, so it used to be cooler here. That only works if the mites are picky about climate. If they could live almost anywhere, their move tells us nothing about temperature.

**Goal:** The mites can live only in a narrow range of climates.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** If the mites survive only in very specific climates, their presence 2,000 years ago means the cave had their climate then. Today that climate is found in the cooler area.

**(B) Weaken.** If the cooler area is also wetter, moisture could be what the mites need. That is an alternative to temperature.

**(C) Out of Scope.** Whether the mites lived there even earlier doesn't bear on the temperature 2,000 years ago.

**(D) Weaken.** If altitude is what matters, the move could be about altitude, not temperature.

**(E) Out of Scope.** How many species live there now doesn't tell us why these mites left.

## PT159-S1-Q12 | Most Strongly Supported | D

> Many hikers now carry cell phones so they can call for emergency assistance from remote areas. Many also use their cell phones to call wildlife offices when they see poachers breaking game laws, enabling wildlife agents to reach the scene before the lawbreakers leave. Poachers, however, avoid areas they know to be frequented by hikers.
