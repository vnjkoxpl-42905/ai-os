# Method explanations: batch 05, part B

Source: same as part A. The first 19 Method and Method of Reasoning gaps in gap-list order. All 19 are in the database with no explanation. PT105-S1-Q12 is filed as Method in the bank but is a Parallel Reasoning question (the database already types it Parallel); it is explained as one.

Modelled on `models/Paradox-Method.md` (Method section):
- The Evaluate names the move from a fixed menu before the choices: analogy, counterexample, ruling out alternatives, bad implications, overlooked consideration, undercutting an assumption, appeal to authority, alternative way to the same benefit.
- Abstract answers are split into halves ("does X by Y") and each half is checked against the stimulus.
- On two-speaker questions, the second-speaker rule: the respondent almost never denies the first speaker's evidence. Answers that say otherwise usually die on that.
- The most-picked wrong answer gets the fullest note.

## PT101-S2-Q3 | Method | B

> A group of unusual meteorites was found in Shergotty, India. Their structure indicates that they originated on one of the geologically active planets, Mercury, Venus, or Mars. Because of Mercury’s proximity to the Sun, any material dislodged from that planet’s surface would have been captured by the Sun, rather than falling to Earth as meteorites. Nor could Venus be the source of the meteorites, because its gravity would have prevented dislodged material from escaping into space. The meteorites, therefore, probably fell to Earth after being dislodged from Mars, perhaps as the result of a collision with a large object.
>
> The argument derives its conclusion by
>
> (A) offering a counterexample to a theory
> (B) eliminating competing alternative explanations
> (C) contrasting present circumstances with past circumstances
> (D) questioning an assumption
> (E) abstracting a general principle from specific data

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The meteorites probably came from Mars.

**Evidence:** Their structure points to Mercury, Venus or Mars. Anything knocked off Mercury would have been pulled into the Sun. Venus's gravity would have kept material from escaping.

**Evaluate:** Three suspects, two alibis. The author starts with a closed list of possible sources, rules out Mercury, rules out Venus, and the one left standing is Mars. That is process of elimination, the same move you would make on a multiple-choice question.

**Goal:** Ruling out the alternatives to leave one explanation.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** No theory gets a counterexample. The author is building a case, not knocking one down.

**(B) Correct.** Mercury and Venus are the competing explanations, and each is eliminated with its own reason. Mars wins because it is the only one left.

**(C) Bad Description.** Nothing compares the present with the past.

**(D) Bad Description.** The author accepts every premise and questions none of them.

**(E) Bad Description.** The conclusion is about these particular meteorites, not a general principle.

## PT101-S3-Q17 | Method | A

> The widespread staff reductions in a certain region’s economy are said to be causing people who still have their jobs to cut back on new purchases as though they, too, had become economically distressed. Clearly, however, actual spending by such people is undiminished, because there has been no unusual increase in the amount of money held by those people in savings account.
>
> The argument in the passage proceeds by doing which one of the following?
>
> (A) concluding that since an expected consequence of a supposed development did not occur, that development itself did not take place
> (B) concluding that since only one of the two predictable consequences of a certain kind of behavior is observed to occur, this observed occurrence cannot, in the current situation, be a consequence of such behavior
> (C) arguing that since people’s economic behavior is guided by economic self-interest, only misinformation or error will cause people to engage in economic behavior that harms them economically
> (D) arguing that since two alternative developments exhaust all the plausible possibilities, one of those developments occurred and the other did not
> (E) concluding that since the evidence concerning a supposed change is ambiguous, it is most likely that no change is actually taking place

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** People who kept their jobs have not actually cut back their spending.

**Evidence:** There has been no unusual rise in the money they hold in savings accounts.

**Evaluate:** Here is the logic underneath. If people really were spending less, the money they did not spend would pile up somewhere, most likely in savings. It did not pile up. So the cutback did not happen. The move: a development should have left a trace, the trace is missing, so the development did not happen.

**Goal:** An expected consequence did not occur, so the claimed development did not occur either.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Split it. "An expected consequence of a supposed development did not occur": no rise in savings, which a cutback should have caused. "That development itself did not take place": spending is undiminished. Both halves match.

**(B) Bad Description.** "Two predictable consequences" sounds sophisticated, but the author only points to one consequence (savings), and only notes that it did not occur.

**(C) Bad Description.** Economic self-interest and misinformation never come up.

**(D) Bad Description.** The author does not set up two alternatives that exhaust the possibilities.

**(E) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. It gets the shape of the conclusion right (no change is happening). But the author does not call the evidence *ambiguous*. The author treats the savings data as clear and decisive: "Clearly, however..." An answer about weighing murky evidence describes a different argument.

## PT102-S3-Q8 | Method | C

> Ornithologist: The curvature of the claws of modern tree-dwelling birds enables them to perch in trees. The claws of Archeopteryx, the earliest known birdlike creature, show similar curvature that must have enabled the creature to perch on tree limbs. Therefore, Archeopteryx was probably a tree-dwelling creature.

Paleontologist: No, the ability to perch in trees is not good evidence that Archeopteryx was a tree-dwelling bird. Chickens also spend time perched in trees, yet chickens are primarily ground-dwelling.
>
> In responding to the ornithologist's hypothesis that Archeopteryx was tree-dwelling, the paleontologist
>
> (A) questions the qualifications of the ornithologist to evaluate the evidence
> (B) denies the truth of the claims the ornithologist makes in support of the hypothesis
> (C) uses a parallel case to illustrate a weakness in the ornithologist's argument
> (D) shows that the hypothesis contradicts one of the pieces of evidence used to support it
> (E) provides additional evidence to support the ornithologist's argument

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Paleontologist) Being able to perch in trees is not good evidence that Archeopteryx lived in trees.

**Evidence:** Chickens perch in trees too, yet they mostly live on the ground.

**Evaluate:** The ornithologist's chain is "perches in trees, so lives in trees." The paleontologist does not dispute that Archeopteryx could perch. Instead, they produce another animal that perches and does not live in trees. One clear case breaks the link. That is a parallel case used to show a weak step.

**Goal:** A parallel case that shows the evidence does not support the conclusion.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** The paleontologist never questions the ornithologist's credentials.

**(B) Bad Description.** The second-speaker rule applies here. The paleontologist accepts the claw evidence ("the ability to perch in trees") and attacks the step from it to the conclusion.

**(C) Correct.** Chickens are the parallel case. Same trait, perching, but a different lifestyle. That shows the weakness: perching does not establish tree-dwelling.

**(D) Bad Description.** The hypothesis does not contradict its own evidence. The paleontologist says the evidence is too weak, not that it conflicts.

**(E) Opposite.** The paleontologist is attacking the argument, not adding to it.

## PT102-S4-Q21 | Method | C

> The following passage is used for Section 4, questions 21 through 22. Sarah: Some schools seek to foster a habit of volunteering in their students by requiring them to perform community service. But since a person who has been forced to do something has not really volunteered and since the habit of volunteering cannot be said to have been fostered in a person who has not yet volunteered for anything, there is no way this policy can succeed by itself. Paul: I disagree. Some students forced to perform community service have enjoyed it so much that they subsequently actually volunteer to do something similar. In such cases, the policy can clearly be said to have fostered a habit of volunteering.
>
> Paul responds to Sarah’s argument using which one of the following argumentative techniques?
>
> (A) He argues that Sarah is assuming just what she sets out to prove.
> (B) He argues that Sarah’s conception of what it means to volunteer excludes certain activities that ought to be considered instances of volunteering.
> (C) He introduces considerations that call into question one of Sarah’s assumptions.
> (D) He questions Sarah’s motives for advancing an argument against the school policy.
> (E) He argues that a policy Sarah fails to consider could accomplish the same aim as the policy that Sarah considers.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Paul) The policy can foster a habit of volunteering.

**Evidence:** Some students forced into community service enjoyed it so much that they later volunteered for something similar.

**Evaluate:** Sarah's argument quietly assumes that forced service can never lead to real volunteering later. Paul agrees that forced service is not volunteering. What he points out is that it can lead to volunteering *afterward*. Those real cases undercut the assumption Sarah needed.

**Goal:** Introducing a consideration that undercuts one of Sarah's assumptions.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** Paul does not say Sarah's argument is circular.

**(B) Bad Description.** Paul accepts Sarah's definition. Forced service is not volunteering. He finds volunteering in what happens later.

**(C) Correct.** The assumption is that forced service never leads to a later habit of volunteering. The consideration is the students who enjoyed it and then volunteered. Paul uses the second to call the first into question.

**(D) Bad Description.** Paul never mentions Sarah's motives.

**(E) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer, because Paul does talk about volunteering happening a different way. But he is defending the *same* policy Sarah attacks, not proposing a new one. "A policy Sarah fails to consider" never appears.

## PT103-S1-Q2 | Method | A

> Lambert: The proposal to raise gasoline taxes to support mass transit networks is unfair. Why should drivers who will never use train or bus lines be forced to pay for them? Keziah: You have misunderstood. The government has always spent far more, per user, from general revenue sources to fund highways than to fund mass transit. The additional revenue from the gasoline tax will simply allow the government to make its distribution of transportation funds more equitable.
>
> Keziah uses which one of the following argumentative strategies in replying to Lambert?
>
> (A) elaborating the context of the issue in order to place the proposal in a more favorable light
> (B) appealing to the principle that what benefits society as a whole benefits all individuals within that society
> (C) challenging the presupposition that fairness is an appropriate criterion on which to judge the matter
> (D) demonstrating that the proposed tax increase will not result in increased expenses for drivers
> (E) declining to argue a point with someone who is poorly informed on the matter under discussion

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Keziah) The gas tax is not unfair. It makes funding more equitable.

**Evidence:** The government has always spent far more per user on highways than on mass transit, out of general revenue.

**Evaluate:** Lambert sees drivers paying for something they will not use. Keziah widens the frame: drivers have been getting more than their share for years, so this tax helps even things out. She does not deny Lambert's facts. She adds context that changes how the proposal looks.

**Goal:** Adding background that makes the proposal look fairer.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** "Elaborating the context" is the history of highway spending. "Place the proposal in a more favorable light" is calling it more equitable. Both halves fit.

**(B) Bad Description.** Keziah's case is about fairness between drivers and transit users, not that whatever helps society helps everyone.

**(C) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. Keziah does challenge Lambert, but she argues on fairness, his own ground. She says the tax makes things *more equitable*. She accepts fairness as the test and argues the tax passes it.

**(D) Bad Description.** Keziah never says drivers will not pay more.

**(E) Bad Description.** She engages with Lambert directly, explaining his misunderstanding rather than refusing to argue.

## PT103-S3-Q16 | Method | D

> The recently negotiated North American Free Trade Agreement among Canada, Mexico, and the United States is misnamed, because it would not result in truly free trade. Adam Smith, the economist who first articulated the principles of free trade, held that any obstacle placed in the way of the free movement of goods, investment, or labor would defeat free trade. So since under the agreement workers would be restricted by national boundaries from seeking the best conditions they could find, the resulting obstruction of the flow of trade would, from a free-trade perspective, be harmful.
>
> The argument proceeds by
>
> (A) ruling out alternatives
> (B) using a term in two different senses
> (C) citing a nonrepresentative instance
> (D) appealing to a relevant authority
> (E) responding to a different issue from the one posed

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The North American Free Trade Agreement is misnamed, because it would not create truly free trade.

**Evidence:** Adam Smith, who first set out the principles of free trade, held that any obstacle to the free movement of goods, investment or labor defeats free trade. Under the agreement, workers would be held back by national borders.

**Evaluate:** What gives the author the right to define free trade this way? Adam Smith, the economist who first spelled it out. The author borrows Smith's standard and applies it to the agreement. That is an appeal to authority, and here the authority is relevant: Smith is the right person to ask about what free trade means.

**Goal:** An appeal to a relevant authority.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** No list of alternatives is narrowed down.

**(B) Bad Description.** This is the top trap, chosen by about one student in six, because the argument is all about whether a name fits. But "free trade" means the same thing throughout: Smith's sense. The author applies one definition consistently. They do not slide between two meanings.

**(C) Bad Description.** Restricting workers is not an unrepresentative example. It is the very feature that fails Smith's test.

**(D) Correct.** Smith is the authority, and he is relevant because he first articulated the principle at issue.

**(E) Bad Description.** The issue is whether the agreement creates free trade, and that is exactly what the author addresses.

## PT105-S1-Q12 | Parallel | C

> Alice will volunteer to work on the hospital fund-raising drive only if her brother Bruce also volunteers and a majority of the others working on the drive promise to select Bruce to manage the drive. However, although Bruce is willing to volunteer, none of the others working on the drive will promise to select Bruce to manage the drive. Thus it is certain that Alice will not volunteer.
>
> In which one of the following arguments is the pattern of reasoning most similar to the pattern of reasoning in the argument above?
>
> (A) Bill and Steve will work together at the bake sale tomorrow only if Bill has enough time. However, since Bill has quite a few errands to run tomorrow, it is likely that Steve will work alone at the sale.
> (B) Leon will go to the picnic only if Vera also goes. However, according to his friends Leon does not plan to go to the picnic; so it is certain that Vera is not planning to go.
> (C) Jim will go to the party only if both Sam and Elaine also go. Sam is going to the party, but Elaine is not going. So it is certain that Jim will not go to the party.
> (D) If Paula works with Elise, then Jane will work with Arthur only. However, if Paula does not work, Elise will also work with Jane. So if Paula does not work, Arthur will work with both Jane and Elise.
> (E) Therese will work in the yard tomorrow only if Maria helps her and it is not raining. Although Maria does not like working in the yard, she will help Therese. So unless it rains tomorrow, Therese will certainly work in the yard.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Alice will certainly not volunteer.

**Evidence:** Alice volunteers only if two things both happen: Bruce volunteers, and the others promise to make Bruce manager. Bruce will volunteer, but the others will not promise.

**Evaluate:** Strip it to a skeleton. A happens only if B *and* C. B happens. C does not. So A certainly does not. One missing requirement is enough to rule A out. Look for an argument with two requirements, one met and one failed, and a certain "no."

**Goal:** A only if B and C. B yes, C no. Certainly not A.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Conclusion Match.** Only one requirement, and the conclusion is "likely," not certain.

**(B) Bad Premise Match.** The most popular wrong answer, because it has "only if" and a certain conclusion. But it reverses the rule. Leon goes only if Vera goes, yet the argument concludes something about Vera from Leon. And it rests on a report from friends, not a fact.

**(C) Correct.** Jim goes only if Sam and Elaine both go. Sam goes, Elaine does not. So Jim certainly does not. A perfect skeleton match.

**(D) Bad Premise Match.** A different structure entirely, with "if" rules about who works with whom.

**(E) Bad Conclusion Match.** This one looks the closest: two requirements, one of them met, and a certain conclusion. But it concludes that Therese *will* work if the other requirement is met. That treats "only if" as "if." The original concludes a certain "no" from a requirement failing.

## PT105-S2-Q8 | Method | B

> Millions of female bats rear their pups in Bracken Cave. Although the mothers all leave the cave nightly, on their return each mother is almost always swiftly reunited with her own pup. Since the bats’ calls are their only means of finding one another, and a bat pup cannot distinguish the call of its mother from that of any other adult bat, it is clear that each mother bat can recognize the call of her pup.
>
> The argument seeks to do which one of the following?
>
> (A) derive a general conclusion about all members of a group from facts known about representative members of that group
> (B) establish the validity of one explanation for a phenomenon by excluding alternative explanations
> (C) support, by describing a suitable mechanism, the hypothesis that a certain phenomenon can occur
> (D) conclude that members of two groups are likely to share a certain ability because of other characteristics they share
> (E) demonstrate that a general rule applies in a particular case

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Each mother bat can recognize her own pup's call.

**Evidence:** Mothers are reunited with their pups quickly. Calls are the bats' only way to find each other. Pups cannot tell their mother's call from any other adult's.

**Evaluate:** Someone is doing the recognizing. There are only two candidates: the pup finds the mother, or the mother finds the pup. The author rules out the pup (it cannot tell its mother's call from the rest), which leaves the mother. That is eliminating the alternative explanation.

**Goal:** Supporting one explanation by ruling out the other.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** Students pick this because the conclusion is about "each" mother. But the argument does not study a sample and generalize. It reasons about how the reunion could work at all.

**(B) Correct.** The phenomenon is the quick reunion. The explanation that survives is "the mother recognizes the pup's call." The one excluded is "the pup recognizes the mother."

**(C) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. Recognizing a call sounds like a mechanism. But the author never describes *how* recognition works. They establish *who* does it by elimination. "Can occur" also misses: the author takes for granted that reunions happen and asks how.

**(D) Bad Description.** No two groups are compared.

**(E) Bad Description.** No general rule is applied to a case.

## PT105-S2-Q25 | Method | D

> Tom: Employers complain that people graduating from high school too often lack the vocational skills required for full-time employment. Therefore, since these skills are best acquired on the job, we should require high school students to work at part-time jobs so that they acquire the skills needed for today’s job market. Mary: There are already too few part-time jobs for students who want to work, and simply requiring students to work will not create jobs for them.
>
> Which one of the following most accurately describes how Mary’s response is related to Tom’s argument?
>
> (A) It analyzes an undesirable result of undertaking the course of action that Tom recommends.
> (B) It argues that Tom has mistaken an unavoidable trend for an avoidable one.
> (C) It provides information that is inconsistent with an explicitly stated premise in Tom’s argument.
> (D) It presents a consideration that undercuts an assumption on which Tom’s argument depends.
> (E) It defends an alternative solution to the problem that Tom describes.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Mary) Requiring students to work will not work.

**Evidence:** There are already too few part-time jobs for the students who want them, and a requirement will not create more.

**Evaluate:** Tom's plan assumes the jobs exist. Mary points out that they do not. She does not challenge his goal or his facts about employers. She pulls out the hidden assumption that holds the plan up.

**Goal:** Undercutting an assumption Tom's plan depends on.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. Mary does describe a problem, so it feels close. But she does not say the plan would cause a bad *result*. She says it *cannot be carried out*, because the jobs do not exist. "An undesirable result of undertaking the course of action" describes a plan that works and backfires. Mary's plan never gets off the ground.

**(B) Bad Description.** Nothing about trends.

**(C) Bad Description.** Tom never states that jobs are available. That is why D says *assumption*. Mary contradicts something Tom took for granted, not something he said.

**(D) Correct.** Tom assumes there are part-time jobs for students to take. Mary's fact, that there are too few, undercuts it.

**(E) Bad Description.** Mary offers no alternative solution.

## PT105-S4-Q20 | Method | D

> James: Chemists have recently invented a new technique for extracting rhodium, an element necessary for manufacturing catalytic converters for automobiles, from nuclear waste. Catalytic converters function to remove noxious gases from automobile exhaust. The use of nuclear power is therefore contributing in at least one way to creating a cleaner environment. Marta: The technique you mention, though effective, is still at an experimental stage, so there has been no shift in the sources of the rhodium currently used in manufacturing catalytic converters.
>
> Marta responds to James’s argument by
>
> (A) casting doubt on the accuracy of the claims made by James in support of his conclusion
> (B) questioning the credibility of advocates of nuclear power
> (C) indicating that James is assuming the truth of the conclusion that he intends to establish
> (D) pointing out a fact that James, in drawing his conclusion, did not take into account
> (E) pointing out that James’s premises are no more plausible than is his conclusion

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Marta, implied) Nuclear power is not yet making the environment cleaner in this way.

**Evidence:** The extraction technique is still experimental, so the rhodium in catalytic converters still comes from the same sources as before.

**Evaluate:** Marta grants the technique works ("though effective"). What James overlooked is the gap between "can be done" and "is being done." His conclusion says nuclear power *is contributing*. Marta points out it is not contributing yet.

**Goal:** Pointing out a fact James did not take into account.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** Marta accepts James's claims, even calling the technique effective. That is the second-speaker rule at work. She attacks the step to his conclusion, not his facts.

**(B) Bad Description.** No one's credibility comes up.

**(C) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. But James's argument is not circular. His premises (rhodium can come from nuclear waste and is used in converters) are different from his conclusion (nuclear power is helping now).

**(D) Correct.** The fact is that the technique is still experimental and has not changed where rhodium comes from. James did not account for it, and it breaks his "is contributing."

**(E) Bad Description.** Marta does not compare how plausible James's premises are with his conclusion.

## PT106-S3-Q10 | Method | A

> Laura: Harold is obviously lonely. He should sell his cabin in the woods and move into town. In town he will be near other people all the time, so he will not be lonely any more. Ralph: Many very lonely people live in towns. What is needed to avoid loneliness is not only the proximity of other people but also genuine interaction with them.
>
> Ralph responds to Laura by pointing out that
>
> (A) something needed for a certain result does not necessarily guarantee that result
> (B) what is appropriate in one case is not necessarily appropriate in all cases
> (C) what is logically certain is not always intuitively obvious
> (D) various alternative solutions are possible for a single problem
> (E) a proposed solution for a problem could actually worsen that problem

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Ralph) Moving to town will not necessarily cure Harold's loneliness.

**Evidence:** Many lonely people live in towns. Avoiding loneliness takes real interaction, not just being near people.

**Evaluate:** Laura treats being near people as enough. Ralph agrees it is needed, but says it is not enough on its own. That is the classic necessary-versus-sufficient point: having one ingredient does not guarantee the result.

**Goal:** Something needed for a result does not guarantee it.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Being near people is needed to avoid loneliness, but it does not guarantee it. Lonely townspeople prove it.

**(B) Bad Description.** Ralph is not saying Harold's case is special. He says Laura's plan misses something that applies to everyone.

**(C) Bad Description.** Nothing here contrasts logic with intuition.

**(D) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. But Ralph never suggests other solutions for Harold. He points out a gap in Laura's.

**(E) Bad Description.** Ralph does not say moving would make Harold *more* lonely, only that it may not help.

## PT107-S1-Q2 | Method | A

> The following passage is used for Section 1, questions 1 through 2. Politician: The funding for the new nationwide healthawareness campaign should come from an increase in taxes on cigarettes. It is well established that cigarette smoking causes many serious health problems, and it is only reasonable that people whose unhealthful habits cause so many health problems should bear the costs of that campaign. Smoker: But it is equally well established that regularly eating high-fat, high-cholesterol foods causes as many serious health problems as does smoking, yet it would be manifestly unreasonable to force those who purchase such foods to bear the burden of financing this campaign.
>
> The smoker’s response to the politician’s argument
>
> (A) offers a counterexample that calls into question the politician’s reasoning
> (B) presents an alternative solution to that proposed by the politician
> (C) argues that the method proposed by the politician would be inadequate for its intended purpose
> (D) questions the accuracy of the information cited by the politician in reaching a conclusion
> (E) illustrates how the politician’s proposal could aggravate the problem it is intended to solve

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Smoker, implied) The politician's reasoning for taxing cigarettes does not hold up.

**Evidence:** High-fat, high-cholesterol foods cause just as many health problems, yet it would clearly be unreasonable to make people who buy them fund the campaign.

**Evaluate:** The politician's principle is that people whose habits cause health problems should pay. The smoker finds a case that fits the principle perfectly (fatty foods) but where applying it seems absurd. One case where a principle gives the wrong answer is a counterexample.

**Goal:** A counterexample to the politician's reasoning.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Fatty-food buyers are the counterexample. They meet the politician's standard, yet making them pay is "manifestly unreasonable," which calls the principle itself into question.

**(B) Bad Description.** The smoker proposes no other way to fund the campaign.

**(C) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. But the smoker never says the cigarette tax would raise too little money. The objection is about fairness, not whether the plan works.

**(D) Bad Description.** The smoker accepts that smoking causes serious health problems ("equally well established").

**(E) Bad Description.** No one says the tax would make health problems worse.

## PT107-S3-Q3 | Method of Reasoning | D

> Land developer: By attempting to preserve endangered species that otherwise would become extinct during our lifetime, we are wasting money on species that will disappear over time regardless of our efforts. Paleontologists have established that extinction is the normal fate of species on the geological time scale of millions of years. Environmentalist: To claim that we should let species disappear because all species eventually die out makes about as much sense as arguing that we should not spend money to find a cure for cancer because all humans are inevitably mortal.
>
> The method the environmentalist uses to object to the land developer’s argument is to
>
> (A) clarify a dilemma that is embedded in the land developer’s argument
> (B) attack the character of the land developer rather than the position the land developer is taking
> (C) show that more evidence is needed to substantiate the land developer’s conclusion
> (D) show that the land developer’s line of reasoning would lead to an unacceptable conclusion if applied to a different situation
> (E) argue that there are problems that money, however judiciously spent, cannot solve

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Environmentalist) The land developer's reasoning does not make sense.

**Evidence:** By the same logic, we should not fund cancer research because all humans eventually die.

**Evaluate:** The developer's logic is: everything in this group will die out eventually, so do not spend to save any of them now. The environmentalist keeps the logic exactly the same and drops it on a new subject, people with cancer. The result is obviously unacceptable, which shows the logic is broken.

**Goal:** Applying the developer's reasoning to another case to reach an absurd conclusion.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** No dilemma is laid out.

**(B) Bad Description.** The environmentalist attacks the reasoning, not the developer.

**(C) Bad Description.** No call for more evidence.

**(D) Correct.** Same reasoning, different situation (cancer), unacceptable conclusion (do not fund a cure). That is the whole response.

**(E) Bad Description.** The environmentalist is arguing that money *should* be spent, not that it cannot solve the problem.

## PT107-S3-Q11 | Method of Reasoning | E

> Astronomer: Astronomical observations in many areas have become useless because light from nearby cities obscures the stars Many people argue that since streetlights are needed for safety, such interference from lights is inevitable. Here in Sandsville, however, the local observatory’s view remains relatively clear, since the city has restricted unnecessary lighting and installed special street lamps that direct all their light downward. It is therefore possible to have both well-lighted streets and relatively dark skies.
>
> The astronomer’s argument proceeds by
>
> (A) appealing to a scientific authority to challenge a widely held belief
> (B) questioning the accuracy of evidence given in support of the opposing position
> (C) proposing an alternative scientific explanation for a natural phenomenon
> (D) making a distinction between terms
> (E) offering a counter example to a general claim

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** It is possible to have both well-lit streets and fairly dark skies.

**Evidence:** Sandsville restricted unnecessary lighting and uses lamps that point down, and its observatory's view stays fairly clear.

**Evaluate:** Many people say light interference is *inevitable*. "Inevitable" is a sweeping claim, and one real case can break it. Sandsville is that case: safe streets and dark skies, together.

**Goal:** A counterexample to a general claim.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** Sandsville is a town, not a scientific authority.

**(B) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. But the astronomer accepts the opposing side's evidence: streetlights are needed for safety. Sandsville still has well-lit streets. What fails is the step from "we need streetlights" to "interference is inevitable."

**(C) Bad Description.** No natural phenomenon gets a new explanation.

**(D) Bad Description.** No terms are distinguished.

**(E) Correct.** The general claim is that interference is inevitable. Sandsville is the counterexample.

## PT107-S3-Q13 | Method of Reasoning | C

> The following passage is used for Section 3, questions 13 through 14. Charles: During recessions unemployment typically rises. Thus, during a recession air pollution due to automobile exhaust decreases, since fewer people commute in cars to jobs and so cars emitting pollutants into the air are used less. Darla: Why think that air pollution would decrease? During a recession fewer people can afford to buy new cars, and cars tend to emit more pollutants as they get older. 13.
>
> Which one of the following most accurately describes how Darla’s response is related to Charles’s argument?
>
> (A) It calls into question the truth of the premises that Charles uses to support his conclusion.
> (B) It makes an additional claim that can be true only if Charles’s conclusion is false.
> (C) It presents an additional consideration that weakens the support given to Charles’s conclusion by his evidence.
> (D) It argues that Charles’s conclusion is true, although not for the reasons Charles gives to support that conclusion.
> (E) It presents an argument showing that the premises in Charles’s argument support an absurd conclusion that Charles has overlooked.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Darla, implied) There is no good reason to think air pollution from cars falls during a recession.

**Evidence:** In a recession, fewer people buy new cars, and older cars pollute more.

**Evaluate:** Charles's logic is: fewer people commute, so fewer cars on the road, so less pollution. Darla does not deny that. She adds a factor pushing the other way: the cars still on the road are older and dirtier. With a force on each side, Charles's evidence no longer guarantees his conclusion.

**Goal:** Adding a consideration that weakens the support Charles's evidence gives his conclusion.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** Darla does not dispute that unemployment rises or that fewer people commute.

**(B) Too Strong.** Darla's point does not *contradict* Charles's conclusion. Fewer cars could still mean less pollution overall, even with dirtier cars. Her point only makes the result uncertain.

**(C) Correct.** The additional consideration is older, dirtier cars. It weakens the link from "fewer commuters" to "less pollution" without denying either.

**(D) Opposite.** Darla doubts Charles's conclusion. She does not argue it is true for other reasons.

**(E) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. But Darla does not use Charles's premises to reach something absurd. She brings in a new fact of her own.

## PT108-S3-Q25 | Method | C

> Anthropologist: After mapping the complete dominance hierarchy for a troupe of vervet monkeys by examining their pairwise interaction, we successfully predicted more complex forms of their group behavior by assuming that each monkey had knowledge of the complete hierarchy. Since our prediction was so accurate, it follows that the assumption we used to reach it was in fact true.

Primatologist: Although I agree that your assumption helped you make those predictions, your conclusion does not follow. You might as well argue that since we can predict the output of some bank cash machines by assuming that these machines actually want to satisfy the customers’ requests, these cash machines must really have desires.
>
> The primatologist uses which one of the following argumentative techniques in countering the anthropologist’s argument?
>
> (A) citing various facts that could not obtain if the anthropologist’s conclusion were correct
> (B) offering another argument that has as its premise the denial of the thesis that the anthropologist defends
> (C) applying one of the anthropologist’s reasoning steps in another argument in an attempt to show that it leads to an absurd conclusion
> (D) attacking the anthropologist’s expertise by suggesting the anthropologist is ignorant of the analogy that can be drawn between animals and machines
> (E) suggesting that the anthropologist’s argument relies on a misinterpretation of a key scientific term

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Primatologist) The anthropologist's conclusion does not follow.

**Evidence:** By the same logic, since assuming cash machines "want" to satisfy customers helps predict their behavior, cash machines would really have desires.

**Evaluate:** The anthropologist reasons: an assumption made accurate predictions, so the assumption is true. The primatologist runs that exact step on cash machines and reaches something absurd. A useful assumption is not necessarily a true one.

**Goal:** Using the same reasoning step in another argument to show it leads somewhere absurd.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** The primatologist offers no facts that are inconsistent with the conclusion. The response is an argument of the same shape.

**(B) Bad Description.** The cash machine argument does not start by denying that monkeys know the hierarchy.

**(C) Correct.** The step is "accurate prediction, so the assumption is true." The other argument is the cash machines. The absurd conclusion is that they have desires.

**(D) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. But the primatologist never says the anthropologist is ignorant of anything. The analogy shows a flaw in the reasoning, not in the person.

**(E) Bad Description.** No scientific term is misread.

## PT109-S1-Q2 | Method | A

> Sheila: It has been argued that using computer technology to add color to a movie originally filmed in black and white damages the integrity of the original film. But no one argues that we should not base a movie on a novel or a short story because doing so would erode the value of the book or story. The film adaptation of the written work is a new work that stands on its own. Judgments of it do not reflect on the original. Similarly, the colorized film is a new work distinct from the original and should be judged on its own merit. It does not damage the integrity of the original black-andwhite film.
>
> Sheila’s argument uses which one of the following techniques of argumentation?
>
> (A) It appeals to an analogy between similar cases.
> (B) It offers a counterexample to a general principle.
> (C) It appeals to popular opinion on the matter at issue.
> (D) It distinguishes facts from value judgments.
> (E) It draws an inference from a general principle and a set of facts.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Sheila) A colorized film does not damage the integrity of the original black-and-white film.

**Evidence:** A movie based on a novel is a new work that stands on its own and does not harm the book. A colorized film is similarly a new, separate work.

**Evaluate:** Sheila takes a case everyone agrees on (adaptations do not harm their source books) and argues that colorized films are like it. "Similarly" is the giveaway. Two cases share a feature (each is a new work), so they should share a conclusion (no harm to the original). That is an analogy.

**Goal:** An analogy between similar cases.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** The adaptation and the colorized film are the similar cases. What Sheila agrees about one (no harm to the original), she carries to the other.

**(B) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer, and the difference is worth learning. A counterexample is one case that breaks a general claim. An analogy uses one case to support a conclusion about another. Sheila is not trying to break the principle "colorizing damages the original." She is building support for her view through a matching case.

**(C) Bad Description.** "No one argues" sounds like popular opinion, but Sheila's support is the similarity between the cases, not what most people think.

**(D) Bad Description.** No line is drawn between facts and value judgments.

**(E) Bad Description.** Sheila argues from a parallel case, not from a general principle.

## PT110-S3-Q8 | Method of Reasoning | B

> Dr. Jones: The new technology dubbed “telemedicine” will provide sustained improvement in at least rural patient care since it allows rural physicians to televise medical examinations to specialists who live at great distances-specialists who will thus be able to provide advice the rural patient would otherwise not receive.

Dr. Carabella: Not so. Telemedicine might help rural patient care initially. However, small hospitals will soon realize that they can minimize expenses by replacing physicians with technicians who can use telemedicine to transmit examinations to large medical centers, resulting in fewer patients being able to receive traditional, direct medical examination. Eventually, it will be the rare individual who ever gets truly personal attention. Hence, rural as well as urban patient care will suffer.
>
> Dr. Carabella uses which one of the following strategies in responding to Dr. Jones?
>
> (A) listing a set of considerations to show that a prescribed treatment that seems to be benefiting a patient in fact harms that patient
> (B) describing the application of the technology discussed by Dr. Jones as one step that initiates a process that leads to an undesirable end
> (C) citing evidence that Dr. Jones lacks the professional training to judge the case at issue
> (D) invoking medical statistics that cast doubt on the premises used in Dr. Jones’s argument
> (E) providing grounds for dismissing Dr. Jones’s interpretation of a key term in medical technology

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Dr. Carabella) Telemedicine will make rural and urban patient care worse.

**Evidence:** Hospitals will replace physicians with technicians who transmit exams to big centers. Fewer patients will get direct examinations, and eventually few will get truly personal attention.

**Evaluate:** Carabella grants the first step ("might help rural patient care initially"), then follows a chain of events forward. Telemedicine starts the chain, and the chain ends somewhere bad. When a response says "this starts a process that ends badly," that is the shape to look for.

**Goal:** Describing telemedicine as the first step in a process that leads to a bad result.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer, because it is the closest in wording: something that seems to help ends up hurting. But Carabella is not talking about a treatment given to *a* patient. She describes how hospitals will change over time. A list of considerations about one patient is not that.

**(B) Correct.** The step is applying telemedicine. The process is hospitals swapping physicians for technicians. The undesirable end is worse care for everyone.

**(C) Bad Description.** Carabella never questions Dr. Jones's training.

**(D) Bad Description.** No statistics, and Carabella does not dispute Jones's premises.

**(E) Bad Description.** No key term is reinterpreted.

## PT111-S1-Q5 | Method | E

> The following passage is used for Section 1, questions 5 through 6. Proponent: Irradiation of food by gamma rays would keep it from spoiling before it reaches the consumer in food stores. The process leaves no radiation behind, and vitamin losses are comparable to those that occur in cooking, so there is no reason to reject irradiation on the grounds of nutrition or safety. Indeed, it kills harmful Salmonella bacteria, which in contaminated poultry have caused serious illness to consumers. Opponent: The irradiation process has no effect on the bacteria that cause botulism, a very serious form of food poisoning, while those that cause bad odors that would warn consumers of botulism are killed. Moreover, Salmonella and the bacteria that cause botulism can easily be killed in poultry by using a safe chemical dip.
>
> The opponent’s argument proceeds by
>
> (A) isolating an ambiguity in a crucial term in the proponent’s argument
> (B) showing that claims made in the proponent’s argument result in a self-contradiction
> (C) establishing that undesirable consequences result from the adoption of either one of two proposed remedies
> (D) shifting perspective from safety with respect to consumers to safety with respect to producers
> (E) pointing out an alternative way of obtaining an advantage claimed by the proponent without risking a particular disadvantage

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Opponent, implied) Irradiation is not the right way to protect poultry.

**Evidence:** Irradiation does not kill the bacteria behind botulism, and it kills the bacteria whose bad smell would warn consumers. A safe chemical dip kills both Salmonella and the botulism bacteria.

**Evaluate:** The proponent's selling point is that irradiation kills Salmonella. The opponent keeps that benefit and shows a better way to get it. The dip kills Salmonella too, without the hidden danger: botulism with no warning smell. Same advantage, minus the risk.

**Goal:** Offering another way to get the proponent's benefit without a particular risk.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** No key term is ambiguous.

**(B) Bad Description.** The opponent does not show the proponent contradicting themselves.

**(C) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. It sees two remedies in the opponent's response, irradiation and the dip. But the opponent finds a downside in only one of them. The dip is called *safe*, and that is the whole point.

**(D) Bad Description.** The perspective stays on consumers throughout. Producers never come up.

**(E) Correct.** The advantage is killing Salmonella. The alternative is the chemical dip. The disadvantage avoided is undetected botulism.
