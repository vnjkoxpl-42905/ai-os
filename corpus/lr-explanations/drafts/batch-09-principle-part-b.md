## PT121-S4-Q6 | Principle-Conform | A

> In the first phase of the Industrial Revolution, machines were invented whose main advantage was that they worked faster than human workers. This technology became widely used because it was economically attractive; many unskilled workers could be replaced by just a few skilled workers. Today managers are looking for technology that will allow them to replace highly paid skilled workers with a smaller number of less-skilled workers.
>
> The examples presented above best illustrate which one of the following propositions?
>
> (A) Employers utilize new technology because it allows them to reduce labor costs.
> (B) Workers will need to acquire more education and skills to remain competitive in the labor market.
> (C) In seeking employment, highly skilled workers no longer have an advantage over less-skilled workers.
> (D) Technology eliminates many jobs but also creates just as many jobs.
> (E) Whereas technological innovations were once concentrated in heavy industry, they now affect all industries.

### Argument Breakdown

**Situation:** Early industrial machines replaced many unskilled workers with a few skilled ones, and that saved money. Today managers want technology that replaces highly paid skilled workers with fewer, less-skilled ones.

**Evaluate:** The two examples move in opposite directions on skill. Then it was unskilled to skilled, and now it's skilled to less skilled. So skill isn't the common thread. What's the same in both is fewer workers and lower labor costs. The first example even names the motive: the technology was "economically attractive."

**Goal:** Employers adopt new technology because it cuts labor costs.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Both examples swap expensive labor for cheaper labor: many workers for a few, highly paid workers for fewer, less-skilled ones. A names the motive behind both.

**(B) Opposite.** Today's managers want to replace skilled workers with less-skilled ones. That doesn't suggest more education will keep workers competitive.

**(C) Too Strong.** This is the trap, picked by 15%. It picks up the modern example, where skilled workers may be replaced. But the first example shows skilled workers winning, so C can't be illustrated by both examples. And managers are only "looking for" this technology. We don't know skilled workers have already lost their advantage.

**(D) Not Described.** Both examples cut the number of workers. Nothing says new jobs make up the difference.

**(E) Not Described.** The passage never mentions heavy industry or says which industries are affected.

## PT122-S1-Q14 | Principle Conform | D

> Laila: Though lying may be unacceptable in most cases, there are exceptions: when lying brings about more good than harm, lying is morally permissible.
>
> Which one of the following judgments conforms most closely to the principle stated by Laila?
>
> (A) It is morally permissible for Marcus to lie to his parents about where he is going for the evening as long as what he is going to do is not itself a bad thing to do.
> (B) It is morally permissible for Lane to lie to the police about the whereabouts of a friend even if Lane suspects the friend has committed a crime.
> (C) It is morally permissible to lie about anything that affects only yourself.
> (D) It is morally permissible for Debra to lie to keep Thomas from being unhappy as long as the lie helps Thomas and does no harm.
> (E) It is morally permissible to lie to innocent people if the lie will cause those people to make a choice that will benefit them but may harm others.

### Argument Breakdown

**Rule:** If a lie brings about more good than harm, it is morally permissible.

**Evaluate:** The trigger is a comparison: the good the lie does has to outweigh the harm. A choice conforms only if it gives us enough to know the lie comes out ahead. Choices that check some other condition, like whether the activity is bad or who it affects, don't trigger the rule.

**Goal:** A lie judged permissible because it does more good than harm.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Trigger.** The condition is that what Marcus does isn't bad. That doesn't tell us whether lying to Marcus's parents does more good than harm.

**(B) No Weighing.** Lying to the police about a possible criminal might do plenty of harm. B never weighs good against harm.

**(C) Wrong Trigger.** A lie that affects only yourself can still do more harm than good. Who it affects isn't the test.

**(D) Correct.** The lie helps Thomas and does no harm. Some good and zero harm means more good than harm, so Laila's principle makes it permissible.

**(E) No Weighing.** The lie benefits the people deceived but "may harm others." We can't tell which side is bigger, so the rule isn't triggered.

## PT122-S2-Q3 | Principle Conform | C

> Most employees spend their time completing unimportant tasks for which they have been given firm schedules and deadlines. Efficient employees know how to ignore such demands and instead spend their time on projects that will yield big rewards for their employers if successful, even when such projects carry the risk of significant loss if unsuccessful.
>
> Which one of the following is an example of efficiency as described above?
>
> (A) spending the entire afternoon working on a report that a supervisor has ordered completed by the following day
> (B) instead of working on a report that a supervisor has ordered completed by the following day, spending the entire afternoon completing routine correspondence that could be delayed
> (C) deciding to take an urgent call from a major customer instead of being punctual at a monthly sales meeting
> (D) meeting daily with other staff members to discuss workloads and schedules
> (E) spending time each morning scheduling tasks according to the most immediate deadlines

### Argument Breakdown

**Rule:** Efficient employees ignore unimportant tasks with firm schedules and deadlines. They spend that time on projects that could pay off big for the employer if successful, even when the projects are risky.

**Evaluate:** Two halves. The employee sets aside an unimportant scheduled demand, and puts the time into something that could yield a big reward for the employer. Following a schedule isn't efficiency by this definition, and neither is skipping a deadline for more routine work.

**Goal:** Skipping a routine scheduled obligation to pursue something with a big potential payoff for the employer.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Opposite.** This is meeting a supervisor's deadline, which is the kind of demand efficient employees ignore.

**(B) Wrong Payoff.** This is the trap, picked by 4%. It does ignore a deadline, so half the definition fits. But the time goes to routine correspondence that could wait, not a project with a big reward.

**(C) Correct.** Being on time for a monthly meeting is a routine scheduled demand. An urgent call from a major customer could mean a big reward for the employer. Skipping the first for the second is efficiency as the passage describes it.

**(D) Opposite.** Daily meetings about workloads and schedules are scheduling, not a big-reward project.

**(E) Opposite.** Scheduling tasks around the nearest deadlines is exactly what efficient employees ignore.

## PT122-S4-Q9 | Principle Conform | E

> Executive: In order to add to our profits, I was planning to promote and distribute herbal tinctures. However, some members of my advisory staff questioned the medical efficacy of such products. So I have consulted a variety of reliable medical publications, and these likewise claim that herbal tinctures are ineffective. Therefore, I must conclude that marketing such products would not produce the result I intended.
>
> The executive's reasoning most closely conforms to which one of the following generalizations?
>
> (A) To be reliable, a medical publication that evaluates consumer products must include at least some independent evidence.
> (B) If a majority of reliable sources conclude that a particular substance is medically ineffective, then that substance almost certainly is medically ineffective.
> (C) Consulting reliable publications is not, by itself, a reliable basis for determining whether or not the promotion of a new line of products will be profitable.
> (D) It would not be profitable to promote and distribute a new line of products if these products have adverse medical effects.
> (E) The promotion and distribution of a new line of products will not prove profitable if a number of reliable authorities declare them to be ineffective.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Marketing herbal tinctures wouldn't produce the result the executive wanted, which is more profit.

**Evidence:** Advisers questioned whether the tinctures work, and a variety of reliable medical publications say they're ineffective.

**Evaluate:** The evidence ends at "reliable sources say these products don't work." The conclusion is about profit. So the principle has to connect reliable authorities calling a product ineffective to the product not being profitable.

**Goal:** If reliable authorities declare a product ineffective, promoting it won't be profitable.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Not the Reasoning.** The executive never asks what makes a publication reliable. The publications are already called reliable.

**(B) Stops Short.** This is the trap, picked by 5%. It takes us from reliable sources to "the tinctures are ineffective." But the executive concludes something about profit, and B says nothing about profit. B also needs a majority of reliable sources, and the executive only consulted "a variety."

**(C) Opposite.** This says consulting publications isn't a reliable basis for predicting profit. That's exactly what the executive did, so C undermines the reasoning.

**(D) Wrong Trigger.** This is about adverse effects. The publications say the tinctures don't work, not that they cause harm.

**(E) Correct.** The trigger is a number of reliable authorities calling the products ineffective, which is the executive's evidence. The outcome is that promoting them won't be profitable, which is the conclusion. E is the whole argument as a rule.

## PT127-S1-Q8 | Principle-Conform | D

> When presented with the evidence against him, Ellison freely admitted to engaging in illegal transactions using company facilities. However, the company obtained the evidence by illegally recording Ellison’s conversations. Therefore, although the company may demand that he immediately cease, it cannot justifiably take any punitive measures against him.
>
> Which one of the following judgments best illustrates the principle illustrated by the argument above?
>
> (A) After Price confessed to having stolen money from Long over a period of several years, Long began stealing from Price. Despite Price’s guilt, Long was not justified in taking illegal action against him.
> (B) Shakila’s secretary has admitted that he is illegally receiving cable television without paying for it. Shakila would not be justified in reporting him, though, since she once did the same thing.
> (C) After Takashi told Sarah’s parents that he had seen her at the movies on Tuesday, Sarah confessed to sneaking out that day. On Monday, however, Takashi had violated the local curfew for minors. Hence Sarah’s parents cannot justifiably punish her in this case.
> (D) After a conservation officer discovered them, Kuttner admitted that he had set the illegal animal traps on his land. But, because she was trespassing at the time, the conservation officer cannot justifiably punish Kuttner in this case.
> (E) Ramirez was forced by the discovery of new evidence to admit that she lied about her role in managing the chief of staff’s financial affairs. Nevertheless, the board of directors cannot justifiably take action against Ramirez, because in past instances it has pardoned others guilty of similar improprieties.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The company can demand that Ellison stop, but it can't justifiably punish Ellison.

**Evidence:** Ellison admitted the illegal transactions when shown the evidence. But the company got that evidence by illegally recording Ellison's conversations.

**Evaluate:** Ellison is guilty, and the argument doesn't deny it. What blocks punishment is how the evidence came out: the party who wants to punish broke the law to uncover the wrongdoing. The principle: if you discovered someone's wrongdoing through your own illegal act, you can't justifiably punish them for it.

**Goal:** A guilty party who can't be punished because the punisher discovered the wrongdoing illegally.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Structure.** Long's illegal act is the punishment, stealing back, not the way Price's theft was discovered. Price confessed. Nothing about the discovery was illegal.

**(B) Wrong Reason.** Shakila can't report the secretary because Shakila once did the same thing. That's hypocrisy, not evidence gathered illegally.

**(C) Wrong Person.** Takashi broke curfew on Monday, but the sighting was on Tuesday, so Takashi's violation had nothing to do with discovering Sarah. And Takashi isn't the one punishing. Sarah's parents are, and they did nothing wrong.

**(D) Correct.** The conservation officer found Kuttner's illegal traps while trespassing on Kuttner's land, so the discovery itself was illegal. Kuttner admits it, and the officer, the one who would punish, can't justifiably do so. That matches the company and Ellison.

**(E) Wrong Reason.** The board can't act because it pardoned others before. That's about consistency, and the evidence against Ramirez wasn't obtained illegally.

## PT128-S2-Q14 | Principle-Conform | B

> A promise is defined as any agreement that yields an obligation. It is acceptable not to keep one's promise if either the person to whom the promise is made tells the promisor not to keep it, or the promisor is unable to fulfill the obligation due to circumstances beyond the promisor's control.
>
> Which one of the following is an application of the principle above pertaining to promising?
>
> (A) Paul agreed to lend Tom $10. But Paul also owes Maria $10. So it is unacceptable for Paul to fulfill his promise to lend Tom $10 while he still owes Maria money.
> (B) Felicia agreed to tutor Alan in mathematics on Tuesday. However, on his way to the tutorial session, Alan was injured in a car accident and was unable to attend that day. Thus, it was acceptable for Felicia not to tutor Alan in mathematics on Tuesday.
> (C) Mika agreed to help Owen move on Sunday, but Owen called Mika on the Thursday before the move and asked him to help him move on Saturday instead. So it would be unacceptable for Mika not to consent to help Owen move on Saturday.
> (D) Quinn agreed to water Laura's plants while Laura was out of town. But Laura had forgotten to give Quinn the keys to her place. However, it was unacceptable for Quinn to fail to fulfill his promise to Laura, since Laura did not excuse Quinn from his obligation to water her plants.
> (E) Ian agreed to pick up Ali at the train station. But Ian decided to stop and return his overdue library books first. As a result, Ian was unable to pick up Ali at the train station. Thus, it was acceptable for Ian to fail to pick up Ali at the train station.

### Argument Breakdown

**Rule:** It's acceptable not to keep a promise if either (1) the person you promised tells you not to keep it, or (2) circumstances beyond your control make you unable to keep it.

**Evaluate:** The rule only gives conditions for when breaking a promise is acceptable. It never says when breaking one is unacceptable, and it never says when keeping one is unacceptable. So a correct application should conclude "it was acceptable not to keep it," with one of the two conditions met.

**Goal:** A promise broken because the promisee released the promisor or circumstances beyond the promisor's control prevented it, judged acceptable.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Not an Application.** The rule never makes keeping a promise unacceptable, and owing Maria money isn't one of its conditions.

**(B) Correct.** Alan was hurt and couldn't come, so Felicia couldn't tutor Alan that Tuesday. That's a circumstance beyond Felicia's control, condition 2, and the conclusion is that not tutoring was acceptable.

**(C) Not an Application.** Owen asked to change the day. The rule says nothing about having to agree to a new request, and it never concludes that anything is unacceptable.

**(D) Contradicts the Rule.** Without the keys, Quinn couldn't get in. That's a circumstance beyond Quinn's control, so condition 2 makes not watering acceptable. D only checks condition 1 and reaches the opposite verdict.

**(E) Misapplies the Rule.** Ian was unable to make it, but only because Ian chose to return library books first. That was within Ian's control, so condition 2 doesn't apply, and nobody released Ian either.

## PT132-S4-Q20 | Principle-Strengthen | E

> Many workers who handled substance T in factories became seriously ill years later. We now know T caused at least some of their illnesses. Earlier ignorance of this connection does not absolve T's manufacturer of all responsibility. For had it investigated the safety of T before allowing workers to be exposed to it, many of their illnesses would have been prevented.
>
> Which one of the following principles most helps to justify the conclusion above?
>
> (A) Employees who are harmed by substances they handle on the job should be compensated for medical costs they incur as a result.
> (B) Manufacturers should be held responsible only for the preventable consequences of their actions.
> (C) Manufacturers have an obligation to inform workers of health risks of which they are aware.
> (D) Whether or not an action's consequences were preventable is irrelevant to whether a manufacturer should be held responsible for those consequences.
> (E) Manufacturers should be held responsible for the consequences of any of their actions that harm innocent people if those consequences were preventable.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The manufacturer's earlier ignorance doesn't free it from all responsibility for the workers' illnesses.

**Evidence:** Substance T caused at least some of the illnesses. If the manufacturer had investigated T's safety before exposing workers, many of those illnesses would have been prevented.

**Evaluate:** The key evidence is that the harm was preventable. The conclusion is that the manufacturer bears some responsibility. So the rule needs "preventable" on the left and "responsible" on the right: if a manufacturer's action harms people and the harm was preventable, the manufacturer is responsible.

**Goal:** Manufacturers are responsible for preventable harmful consequences of their actions.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Conclusion.** This says harmed employees should be compensated. It doesn't say who is responsible, so it doesn't reach the manufacturer.

**(B) Wrong Direction.** "Only" puts preventable on the right: responsible → preventable. That tells us when a manufacturer is not responsible, when the harm couldn't be prevented. It can't prove the manufacturer is responsible. Knowing the harm was preventable doesn't trigger anything.

**(C) Wrong Trigger.** This covers risks the manufacturer was aware of. Here it was ignorant, so C never applies.

**(D) Opposite.** If preventability is irrelevant, the argument's main evidence counts for nothing.

**(E) Correct.** The workers are innocent people harmed by T, and the evidence says the illnesses were preventable. Both conditions are met, so the manufacturer is held responsible. That's the conclusion.

## PT142-S1-Q3 | Principle-Conform | B

> Editorial: The government should not fund any part of its health services with lottery revenue. These health services are essential to our community, but lottery revenue could decline at some time in the future, leaving the government scrambling to make up a budget shortfall.
>
> The argument in the editorial most closely conforms to which one of the following principles?
>
> (A) Governments should spend more of their revenue on essential services than on nonessential services.
> (B) Essential government services must be funded from reliable sources of revenue.
> (C) No government service should be entirely dependent on lottery revenue for its funding.
> (D) Governments should consider all health services to be essential to the community.
> (E) At least some lottery revenue must be set aside in case of budget shortfalls in the future.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The government shouldn't fund any part of its health services with lottery revenue.

**Evidence:** The health services are essential, and lottery revenue could decline, leaving a budget shortfall.

**Evaluate:** Two facts lead to the conclusion. These services are essential, and lottery money is unreliable. The principle puts them together: essential services need reliable funding. Lottery money isn't reliable, so none of it should pay for them.

**Goal:** Essential services should be funded only from reliable sources.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Not the Reasoning.** The editorial never compares spending on essential and nonessential services.

**(B) Correct.** The health services are essential, so they must be funded from reliable sources. Lottery revenue could decline, so it isn't reliable. That rules out using lottery money for any part of them, which is the conclusion.

**(C) Too Weak.** This bans being "entirely" dependent on lottery money. The editorial bans funding "any part" of the services that way. Under C, partial lottery funding would be fine.

**(D) Not the Reasoning.** The editorial says these health services are essential. It doesn't say every health service must be treated that way.

**(E) Different Remedy.** This saves lottery money for future shortfalls. The editorial's answer is to stop relying on lottery money for these services at all.

## PT143-S1-Q2 | Principle-Conform | C

> Popular science publications that explain new developments in science face a dilemma. In order to reach a wide audience, these publications must rely heavily on metaphorical writing, which usually fails to convey the science accurately. If the writing is more rigorous, they get the science right but fail to reach a wide audience. These publications should therefore give up trying to explain new developments in science to a wide audience.
>
> Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the reasoning in the argument?
>
> (A) Science publications should balance the use of metaphors with more rigorous writing.
> (B) The more recent a scientific development is, the harder it is to explain it accurately to a wide audience.
> (C) In reporting scientific developments, it is better to fail to reach a wide audience than to be inaccurate.
> (D) In reporting scientific developments, it is better to reach a wide audience than to be accurate.
> (E) Even the most rigorous explanations of some scientific concepts must still contain metaphors.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Popular science publications should give up trying to explain new science to a wide audience.

**Evidence:** To reach a wide audience, they have to rely on metaphor, which usually gets the science wrong. If they write more rigorously, they get the science right but lose the wide audience.

**Evaluate:** This is a tradeoff with two options. Reach a wide audience and be inaccurate, or be accurate and reach fewer people. The author picks accuracy. Nothing in the evidence says which matters more. That ranking is what the principle has to supply.

**Goal:** Being accurate matters more than reaching a wide audience.

## PT143-S3-Q3 | Principle-Strengthen | C

> Louise McBride, a homeowner, filed a complaint against a nearby nightclub through the Licensing Bureau, a government agency. Although regulations clearly state that Form 283 is to be used for formal complaints, Bureau staff gave McBride Form 5, which she used with the intention of filing a formal complaint. The nightclub argues that the complaint should be dismissed because the incorrect form was used. But that would be unfair.
>
> Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the judgment that dismissing the complaint would be unfair?
>
> (A) People who wish to file complaints through the Licensing Bureau should be informed of all relevant regulations.
> (B) Government agencies should make their forms straightforward enough that completing them will not be unduly burdensome for the average person.
> (C) It is unfair for someone’s complaint to be dismissed because of an incorrect action on the part of a government agency.
> (D) A government agency should not make its procedures so complex that even the agency’s employees cannot understand the procedures.
> (E) It is unfair for a business to be subject to a formal complaint unless the complaint is made in a way that provides the business with an opportunity to defend itself.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Dismissing McBride's complaint would be unfair.

**Evidence:** The rules call for Form 283, but Bureau staff gave McBride Form 5. McBride used it intending to file a formal complaint.

**Evaluate:** The wrong form came from the government agency, not from McBride. McBride did what the staff said. The principle needs to connect "the mistake was the agency's" to "dismissing the complaint is unfair."

**Goal:** It's unfair to dismiss someone's complaint because of a government agency's mistake.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Outcome.** This says people should be told the regulations. Even if McBride should have been told, A doesn't say anything about whether dismissal would be unfair.

**(B) Wrong Problem.** The issue isn't that Form 5 was hard to fill out. It's that it was the wrong form. And B says nothing about dismissal.

**(C) Correct.** The incorrect action was the Bureau staff handing over Form 5, and the Bureau is a government agency. So dismissing the complaint for that reason is unfair. That's the conclusion.

**(D) Not Shown.** We know staff handed out the wrong form, not that the procedures are too complex for them. And D tells agencies how to design procedures. It doesn't say dismissing the complaint is unfair.

**(E) Wrong Side.** This protects the nightclub. Nothing says the nightclub lacked a chance to defend itself, and the conclusion is about fairness to McBride.

## PT144-S2-Q6 | Principle-Strengthen | D

> Lawyer: In addition to any other penalties, convicted criminals must now pay a “victim surcharge” of $30. The surcharge is used to fund services for victims of violent crimes, but this penalty is unfair to nonviolent criminals since the surcharge applies to all crimes, even nonviolent ones like petty theft.
>
> Which one of the following principles, if valid, would most help to justify the reasoning in the lawyer’s argument?
>
> (A) The penalties for a crime should be severe enough to deter most people who would commit the crime if there were no penalties.
> (B) The overall penalty for a violent crime should be more severe than the overall penalty for any nonviolent crime.
> (C) A surcharge intended to provide services to victims is justified only if all proceeds of the surcharge are used to provide services.
> (D) A criminal should not be required to pay for services provided to victims of crimes that are more serious than the type of crime the criminal has been convicted of.
> (E) Convicted thieves should be fined an amount at least as great as the value of the property stolen.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The victim surcharge is unfair to nonviolent criminals.

**Evidence:** The surcharge funds services for victims of violent crimes, but it applies to every crime, including nonviolent ones like petty theft.

**Evaluate:** The lawyer's complaint is a mismatch. Someone convicted of petty theft pays for services for victims of violent crime, a more serious kind of crime than theirs. The principle has to say that's not a fair thing to make a criminal pay for.

**Goal:** Criminals shouldn't have to pay for services for victims of crimes more serious than their own.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Not the Reasoning.** Deterrence never comes up. The lawyer's objection is about who pays for what.

**(B) Doesn't Reach.** This compares overall penalties. The surcharge is added "in addition to any other penalties", so violent criminals may still pay more overall. B doesn't make the surcharge unfair.

**(C) Not Shown.** Nothing suggests the money is spent on anything other than services. The lawyer's complaint is who pays, not where the money goes.

**(D) Correct.** A petty thief pays for services for victims of violent crime, which is more serious than petty theft. D says that criminal shouldn't have to pay, so the surcharge is unfair to nonviolent criminals.

**(E) Out of Scope.** Fines for theft based on the value stolen have nothing to do with the victim surcharge.

## PT144-S4-Q11 | Principle-Conform | B

> A person who knowingly brings about misfortune should be blamed for it. However, in some cases a person who unwittingly brings about misfortune should not be blamed for it. For example, a person should never be blamed for unwittingly bringing about misfortune if the person could not reasonably have foreseen it.
>
> The principles above, if valid, most help to justify the reasoning in which one of the following?
>
> (A) Although he would have realized it if he had thought about it, it did not occur to Riley that parking his car in the center lane of Main Street could lead to a traffic accident. So, if a traffic accident does result from Riley’s parking his car in the center lane of Main Street, he should not be blamed for it.
> (B) Oblicek had no idea that suggesting to her brother that he take out a loan to expand his business was likely to cause the business to go bankrupt, nor could she have reasonably foreseen this. So, if the loan does cause her brother’s business to go bankrupt, Oblicek should not be blamed for it.
> (C) Gougon had no reason to think that serving the hollandaise sauce would make his guests ill, but he was concerned that it might. Thus, if the hollandaise sauce does make Gougon’s guests ill, Gougon should be blamed for it.
> (D) When Dr. Fitzpatrick gave his patient the wrong medicine, he did not know that it would cause the patient to experience greatly increased blood pressure. So, if no one else knowingly did anything that contributed to the patient’s increase in blood pressure, no one other than Dr. Fitzpatrick is to blame for it.
> (E) Any reasonable person could have foreseen that dropping a lit cigarette in dry leaves would start a fire. Thus, even if Kapp did not realize this, she is to blame for starting a fire on Rodriguez’s farm since she dropped a lit cigarette in dry leaves there.

### Argument Breakdown

**Rule:** Two rules. (1) Someone who knowingly brings about misfortune should be blamed. (2) Someone who unwittingly brings about misfortune that they couldn't reasonably have foreseen should never be blamed.

**Evaluate:** Notice the gap. If someone acted unwittingly but could have foreseen the misfortune, neither rule applies. Rule 1 needs knowledge, and rule 2 needs it to be unforeseeable. So the rules can only justify blaming a knowing actor, or not blaming an unwitting actor who couldn't have foreseen it.

**Goal:** Someone unaware of the risk, who couldn't reasonably have foreseen it, shouldn't be blamed.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Rule Not Triggered.** Riley would have realized it with a moment's thought, so the accident was foreseeable. Rule 2 doesn't apply, and no rule says Riley escapes blame.

**(B) Correct.** Oblicek had no idea the loan could bankrupt the business and couldn't reasonably have foreseen it. That's rule 2 exactly: unwitting and unforeseeable, so Oblicek should not be blamed.

**(C) Rule Not Triggered.** Gougon worried the sauce might make people sick but had no reason to think it would. Worry isn't knowledge, so rule 1 doesn't apply. No rule says Gougon should be blamed.

**(D) Rule Not Triggered.** The rules never say who is the only one to blame. And Dr. Fitzpatrick acted unknowingly, so rule 1 doesn't make the doctor blameworthy.

**(E) Rule Not Triggered.** This is the tempting one. Kapp should have seen the fire coming, and blaming Kapp feels right. But Kapp didn't realize it, so rule 1 (knowingly) doesn't apply. Rule 2 only excuses. No rule here blames someone who was unwitting but should have known.

## PT145-S4-Q1 | Principle-Strengthen | A

> Pundit: Grenier will almost certainly not be elected as mayor. Although she says she believes in raising city employees’ wages, it was only a few years ago that she was arguing that their wages should not be increased. Her claim that she has learned more about the issue since then—though sincere—will not matter to most voters, who will see her as insincere.
>
> Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the pundit’s reasoning?
>
> (A) Voters are unlikely to vote for a politician whom they perceive to be insincere.
> (B) Voters are unlikely to notice whether a politician’s stance on issues has changed over time.
> (C) Voters are unlikely to be influenced by what a politician’s views were in the past if the voters agree with the politician’s current positions.
> (D) Voters are likely to elect a politician who they believe understands their financial concerns.
> (E) Voters are likely to question the sincerity of a politician who does not hold the same beliefs they do.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Grenier almost certainly won't be elected mayor.

**Evidence:** Grenier recently argued against raising city employees' wages and now supports it. Grenier's explanation, sincere as it is, won't matter, because most voters will see Grenier as insincere.

**Evaluate:** The evidence ends with voters seeing Grenier as insincere. The conclusion is that Grenier loses. The missing link: voters don't vote for politicians they think are insincere.

**Goal:** Voters are unlikely to vote for a politician they see as insincere.

### Choice Notes
