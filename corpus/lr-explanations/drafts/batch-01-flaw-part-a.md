## 1. PT101-S3-Q2 · Flaw · Difficulty 1 · Answer E

*Modelled on PT150-S2-Q4 (comparison), PT107-S4-Q18 (test the abstract wording), and PT154-S2-Q12 (missing alternative).*

> Advertisement: Anyone who thinks moisturizers are not important for beautiful skin should consider what happens to the earth, the skin of the world, in times of drought. Without regular infusions of moisture the ground becomes lined and cracked and its lush loveliness fades away. Thus your skin, too, should be protected from the ravages caused by lack of moisture; give it the protection provided by regular infusions of Dewyfresh, the drought-defying moisturizer.
>
> The Dewyfresh advertisement exhibits which one of the following errors of reasoning?
>
> (A) It treats something that is necessary for bringing about a state of affairs as something that is sufficient to bring about that state of affairs.
> (B) It treats the fact that two things regularly occur together as proof that there is a single thing that is the cause of them both.
> (C) It overlooks the fact that changing what people think is the case does not necessarily change what is the case.
> (D) It relies on the ambiguity of the term “infusion,” which can designate either a process or the product of that process.
> (E) It relies on an analogy between two things that are insufficiently alike in the respects in which they would have to be alike for the conclusion to be supported.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion (thus)** Skin should get regular Dewyfresh moisturizer to protect it from damage caused by lack of moisture.

**Evidence** Drought dries and cracks the earth, which the advertisement calls “the skin of the world.”

**Evaluate** Given that dry ground cracks, how could skin still behave differently? Soil and human skin are different materials. The ad needs the two to resemble each other in the very process it is comparing. The phrase “skin of the world” is a metaphor, not evidence that a moisturizer affects skin the way rain affects soil. Even if skin needs moisture, nothing here establishes that Dewyfresh provides the promised protection.

**Goal** Look for a criticism of an analogy between the earth and human skin that has not established the relevant similarity.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Flaw.** Necessary versus sufficient would require the ad to argue, for example, that because moisture is required for healthy skin, applying Dewyfresh guarantees beautiful skin. It does not make that formal move. Its evidence is the earth analogy.

**(B) Wrong Flaw.** A common cause would explain why two observed events happen together. The ad does not report two correlated events and infer a shared cause. It compares two different things.

**(C) Wrong Flaw.** The argument does not claim that changing beliefs changes physical reality. The opening addresses people who doubt moisturizers, but the evidence is about drought, not a change in what people believe.

**(D) Wrong Flaw.** “Infusion” is used to mean an input of moisture in both places. The conclusion does not switch from a process to its product. The questionable switch is from ground to human skin.

**(E) Correct.** Test the analogy's relevant feature. Dry soil cracks without water; does human skin respond to a moisturizer in the same way? The ad gives no reason to think so. Calling earth “skin” makes the analogy memorable but does not supply the biological similarity the conclusion needs.

## 2. PT102-S2-Q18 · Flaw · Difficulty 3 · Answer E

*Modelled on PT154-S2-Q12 (false choice), PT110-S2-Q11 (best versus only), and PT150-S2-Q4 (plans).*

> President of Central Supply Company: Profits are at an all-time low this fiscal year because of decreased demand for our products. If this situation continues, the company may have to declare bankruptcy. So it is important to prevent any further decrease in profits. Consequently, the only options are to reduce planned expansion or to eliminate some less profitable existing operations.
>
> Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the company president's reasoning?
>
> (A) It presumes without giving justification that survival of the company has been a good thing.
> (B) It does not take into account that there are alternatives to declaring bankruptcy.
> (C) It presumes without giving justification that only decreased demand can ever be the cause of decreased profits.
> (D) It does not allow for the possibility that profits will decrease only slightly during the next fiscal year.
> (E) It does not take into account that there may be other ways to stop the decrease in profits.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion (consequently)** The company's only options are to reduce planned expansion or eliminate some less profitable current operations.

**Evidence** Demand and profit have fallen. Continued decline may cause bankruptcy, so preventing further profit decline matters.

**Evaluate** Granted, the company needs to stop profits falling. Why must it choose one of those two cost cuts? It might increase revenue through a new product, revise prices, or find a third cost saving. The urgency of the goal does not prove an exhaustive list of ways to reach it. We need only one possible third way to challenge “only.”

**Goal** Find the missing alternatives to stopping profit decline, not merely alternatives to bankruptcy.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Assumption.** Whether the company's survival is good does not show that the two listed measures exhaust its ways to prevent further loss.

**(B) Bad Description.** This sounds tempting because bankruptcy is mentioned. But the president's *only options* claim concerns stopping the decrease in profits. A way to survive after profits keep falling would not be a third way to stop that fall.

**(C) Bad Assumption.** The president says decreased demand explains this year's low profits, not that decreased demand is the *only possible cause* of decreased profits. Even if another factor could lower profits, that would not identify the flaw in the supposedly exhaustive list of two measures for preventing a further decline.

**(D) Not an Objection.** Even a slight further decrease is a decrease. Its size does not show that there is another option for preventing it.

**(E) Correct.** “Other ways to stop the decrease” is exactly the omitted third route. If another route exists, reducing expansion and eliminating operations are not the *only* options. This is the False Choice flaw.

## 3. PT102-S3-Q14 · Flaw · Difficulty 1 · Answer C · Notes only

*Existing Conclusion, Evidence, Evaluate, and Goal steps are retained. Modelled on PT154-S1-Q12 (ad hominem) and PT155-S1-Q20 (keeping the target speaker straight).*

> Herbalist: Many of my customers find that their physical coordination improves after drinking juice containing certain herbs. A few doctors assert that the herbs are potentially harmful, but doctors are always trying to maintain a monopoly over medical therapies. So there is no reason not to try my herb juice.
>
> The reasoning in the herbalist's argument is flawed because the argument
>
> (A) attempts to force acceptance of a claim by inducing fear of the consequences of rejecting that claim
> (B) bases a conclusion on claims that are inconsistent with each other
> (C) rejects a claim by attacking the proponents of the claim rather than addressing the claim itself
> (D) relies on evidence presented in terms that presuppose the truth of the claim for which the evidence is offered
> (E) mistakes the observation that one thing happens after another for proof that the second thing is the result of the first

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Flaw.** The herbalist does not warn customers of frightening consequences if they reject the juice. The doctors' warning that the herbs might be harmful is the point the herbalist dismisses.

**(B) Wrong Flaw.** Customers can report improved coordination while the herbs still carry a risk of harm. Those claims can both be true, so the herbalist's evidence is not internally inconsistent.

**(C) Correct.** The doctors assert that the herbs might be harmful. The herbalist replies that doctors want to protect a monopoly, then concludes there is no reason not to try the juice. A motive to make a claim does not show that the claim is false. The potential harm has not been addressed. This is the Ad Hominem move.

**(D) Wrong Flaw.** The customer reports are not phrased to assume the juice is safe. The error occurs in dismissing the warning, not in wording the evidence as the conclusion.

**(E) Bad Description.** The report that coordination improves *after* drinking juice might invite a post hoc objection if the conclusion were that the juice caused the improvement. The conclusion is that there is **no reason not to try** it. The live objection is the possible harm raised by doctors, and the herbalist attacks the doctors instead.

## 4. PT102-S4-Q4 · Flaw · Difficulty 2 · Answer B

*Modelled on PT154-S1-Q12 (ad hominem), PT155-S1-Q20 (separating a speaker from a claim), and PT154-S2-Q12 (testing abstract wording).*

> Columnist: The country is presently debating legislation that, if passed, would force manufacturers to increase the number of paid vacation days for employees, to pay higher overtime wages, and to pay all day-care expenses for children of each employee. This legislation is being supported by members of groups that have resorted to violent tactics in the past, and by individuals who are facing indictment on tax-evasion charges. We must defeat this legislation and what it stands for.
>
> The columnist’s argument is flawed because it
>
> (A) attacks legislation by calling into question the integrity of the originators of the legislation
> (B) assails legislation on the basis of the questionable character of supporters of the legislation
> (C) attempts to discredit legislation by appealing to public sentiment for those who would be adversely affected
> (D) presupposes that legislation is bad legislation whenever it has only a small number of supporters outside the country’s national legislative body
> (E) rejects legislation on the grounds that its supporters act inconsistently in seeking to place burdens on manufacturers upon whose business success the supporters depend

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion** The proposed legislation must be defeated.

**Evidence** Some supporters belong to groups that used violence in the past; other supporters face tax-evasion indictment.

**Evaluate** Suppose every allegation about these supporters is true. What does that tell us about whether more paid vacation, higher overtime wages, or paid child care is a good law? Nothing by itself. A disreputable person can support a good proposal. The columnist offers no argument about the proposal's effects.

**Goal** Look for the move from the character of the law's *supporters* to a verdict against the law.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** “Originators” means the people who proposed or created the legislation. The stimulus identifies people supporting it. We are not told who originated it.

**(B) Correct.** The columnist assails the law because some *supporters* have questionable histories or face charges. That attacks the source of support rather than the legislation's merits. A charge is not even a conviction, but the flaw remains even if the character allegations are granted.

**(C) Bad Description.** The columnist names no people who would be adversely affected and makes no plea on their behalf. The appeal is against allegedly bad supporters.

**(D) Bad Description.** The argument says nothing about the number of supporters inside or outside the legislature. It targets their character, not their count.

**(E) Bad Evidence Match.** The columnist never claims the supporters depend on manufacturers' success or act inconsistently. That would be a different attack on them.

## 5. PT103-S1-Q5 · Flaw · Difficulty 3 · Answer A

*Modelled on PT155-S1-Q20 (burden of proof in a rebuttal), PT103-S2-Q21 (absence of evidence), and PT154-S1-Q12 (matching the conclusion to the criticism).*

> Attorneys for a criminal defendant charged that the government, in a coverup, had destroyed evidence that would have supported the defendant in a case. The government replied that there is no evidence that would even tend to support the defendant in the case.
>
> Which one of the following is the most accurate evaluation of the government’s reply?
>
> (A) It leaves open the question of whether the government had destroyed such evidence.
> (B) It establishes that the attorneys’ charge is an exaggeration.
> (C) It shows that the attorneys did not know whether their charge was true.
> (D) It demonstrates the government’s failure to search for evidence in its files.
> (E) If true, it effectively disproves the charge made on behalf of the defendant.

### Argument Breakdown

**Claim under dispute** The attorneys say the government destroyed evidence that would have helped the defendant.

**Government's reply** There is no evidence that would even tend to help the defendant.

**Evaluate** How could both statements be true? If the government destroyed the helpful evidence, none would remain now. “There is none” does not answer “Did you destroy it?” This is especially pointed because the alleged destruction would itself explain the absence the government cites.

**Goal** Find the answer saying the reply leaves the destruction allegation unresolved.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** The reply reports an absence of helpful evidence but does not establish how that absence arose. Destruction remains possible. A denial of the current evidence's existence is not a denial of the alleged earlier destruction.

**(B) Bad Description.** Calling the charge an exaggeration would require a reason to believe there was no destruction or that its scale was overstated. The reply provides neither.

**(C) Bad Description.** The attorneys could have information about destroyed material. The government's statement tells us nothing about what the attorneys know.

**(D) Bad Description.** There might be no evidence because a search found none, because none ever existed, or because it was destroyed. The reply alone does not prove a failure to search.

**(E) Backwards.** Even if the reply is true *now*, it does not disprove that helpful evidence existed and was destroyed. The alleged coverup is a possible explanation for the reply's truth.

## 6. PT103-S2-Q1 · Flaw · Difficulty 1 · Answer B

*Modelled on PT154-S1-Q12 (keep evidence aimed at the claim), PT155-S1-Q20 (rebuttal target), and PT150-S2-Q4 (identify the decisive difference).*

> Insurance that was to become effective at 9 A.M. on a certain date was taken out on the life of a flight attendant. He died on that date at 10 A.M. local time, which was two hours before 9 A.M. in the time zone where the policy had been purchased. The insurance company contended that the policy had not become effective; a representative of the flight attendant's beneficiary, his mother, countered by arguing that the policy amount should be paid because the attendant had been his mother's sole support, and she was ill.
>
> The representative's argument is flawed as a counter to the insurance company's contention because
>
> (A) the conclusion is no more than a paraphrase of the evidence offered in support of it
> (B) it appeals to the emotion of pity rather than addressing the issue raised
> (C) it makes an unwarranted distinction between family obligations and business obligations
> (D) it substitutes an attack on a person for the giving of reasons
> (E) a cause and its effect are mistaken for each other

### Argument Breakdown

**Issue** Did the policy become effective before the attendant died? The local clock read 10 A.M., but that was before 9 A.M. in the purchase time zone.

**Representative's conclusion** The insurer should pay the mother.

**Evidence** The attendant was her sole support and she is ill.

**Evaluate** Those facts make her loss especially hard. Do they settle what the policy's effective time means? No. The representative needs an argument about the contract's time zone or wording. A sympathetic beneficiary can still hold a policy that had not begun.

**Goal** Find the appeal to pity in place of a response to the coverage issue.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Flaw.** The mother's illness and dependence are not a paraphrase of “the policy should pay.” They are distinct facts, albeit irrelevant to the contract timing.

**(B) Correct.** Pity for the mother is understandable, but it does not answer the insurer's contention about when coverage began. The argument swaps an emotional reason to want payment for a reason the contract requires it.

**(C) Bad Description.** The representative makes no comparison between family obligations and business obligations. The defect is that family hardship does not determine the insurance contract.

**(D) Bad Description.** No person is attacked. The representative invites sympathy for the mother.

**(E) Wrong Flaw.** No cause-and-effect relationship is reversed. The dispute is over the time at which a policy took effect.

## 7. PT103-S2-Q4 · Flaw · Difficulty 2 · Answer C

*Modelled on PT155-S1-Q20 (opponent's claim versus rebuttal), PT154-S1-Q12 (matching the target), and PT154-S2-Q12 (testing an alleged implication).*

> Politician P: My opponent claims that the government is obligated to raise taxes to increase funding for
> schools and health care. Because raising taxes to increase funding for schools and health care would make taxpayers upset over their loss of buying power, my opponent is simply mistaken.
>
> Politician P's reasoning is questionable because it involves
>
> (A) presupposing that a claim is mistaken on the grounds that the person defending it advocates other unpopular views
> (B) assuming that a claim is false on the grounds that the person defending it is of questionable character
> (C) concluding that a view is false on the grounds that its implementation would lead to unhappiness
> (D) appealing to wholly irrelevant issues to deflect attention away from the real issue
> (E) insisting that an obligation exists without offering any evidence that it exists

### Argument Breakdown

**Opponent's claim** The government is obligated to raise taxes for schools and health care.

**Politician P's conclusion** The opponent is mistaken.

**Evidence** Raising taxes for those purposes would upset taxpayers by reducing buying power.

**Evaluate** An action can be required and still make people unhappy. Paying a debt may upset the debtor, but that feeling does not erase the debt. To rebut an obligation, P needs a reason it is not owed, not merely a cost of meeting it.

**Goal** Find the move from an unpleasant consequence of a proposal to the proposal's being false or mistaken.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Not Ad Hominem.** P does not cite other unpopular views held by the opponent. P cites a predicted consequence of the specific tax proposal.

**(B) Not Ad Hominem.** No claim about the opponent's character appears. This is not Ad Hominem.

**(C) Correct.** The view is that the government *must* raise taxes for these services. P calls it mistaken because doing so would reduce taxpayers' buying power and upset them. Unhappiness is compatible with an obligation.

**(D) Bad Description.** Taxpayers' buying power is related to a tax increase, so the issue is not wholly irrelevant. The problem is the inference from that negative consequence to no obligation.

**(E) Bad Conclusion Match.** P rejects the claim that an obligation exists; P does not insist an obligation exists.

## 8. PT103-S3-Q5 · Flaw · Difficulty 1 · Answer C

*Modelled on PT147-S1-Q6 (sampling), PT150-S2-Q1 (selected reports), and PT120-S3-Q8 (denominator/base rate).*

> Consumer: I would like to have the features contained in the latest upgrade to your computer software package, but I am leery of installing the upgrade because a friend has told me he had a problem with it. Company representative: We have distributed nearly 3,000 copies of the upgrade and we have received fewer than 100 calls saying that it has caused problems. So it is very unlikely that you will experience any problems with the upgrade.
>
> The reasoning in the company representative’s argument is most vulnerable to criticism because it fails to consider the possibility that
>
> (A) the company will issue another upgrade that corrects the problems with the current upgrade
> (B) some of the problems people have experienced with the upgrade have been quite serious
> (C) a significant number of people have experienced problems with the upgrade but have not reported them
> (D) the consumer will experience software problems if the upgrade is not installed
> (E) some of the reported problems were a result of users failing to follow instructions

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion** The consumer is very unlikely to have a problem with the upgrade.

**Evidence** Nearly 3,000 copies were distributed, and fewer than 100 problem calls reached the company.

**Evaluate** The company has counted calls, not problems. What if many customers experienced a problem but did not call? Fewer than 100 reports out of nearly 3,000 copies would not then tell us the actual failure rate. This is a reporting sample with missing cases.

**Goal** Find the possibility that problems went unreported.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** A later corrective upgrade could help later users. It does not show how many problems the current upgrade causes now.

**(B) Bad Evidence Match.** Severe problems would matter to the consumer, but the argument estimates *likelihood*. Seriousness does not show that more people have problems than the number of calls suggests.

**(C) Correct.** If many people had problems without calling, reported calls undercount actual failures. The company's inference from “few calls” to “few problems” then collapses. The reusable question is whether the method of counting captures the event the conclusion is about.

**(D) Out of Scope.** Problems without the upgrade do not tell us whether this upgrade causes problems for its users.

**(E) Bad Description.** If some reported problems came from users' mistakes, even fewer reports would count as defects caused by the upgrade. That does not expose the company's undercount.

## 9. PT104-S1-Q2 · Flaw · Difficulty 2 · Answer D

*Modelled on PT113-S2-Q3 (necessary versus sufficient), PT124-S2-Q11 (conditional requirement), and PT154-S1-Q9 (check the exact inference).*

> Although 90 percent of the population believes itself to be well informed about health care, only 20 percent
> knows enough about DNA to understand a news story about DNA. So apparently at least 80 percent of the population does not know enough about medical concepts to make well-informed personal medical choices or to make good public policy decisions about health care.
>
> The argument’s reasoning is questionable because the argument fails to demonstrate that
>
> (A) those people who can understand news stories about DNA are able to make well-informed personal medical choices
> (B) more than 20 percent of the population needs to be well informed about health care for good public policy decisions about health care to be made
> (C) one’s being able to make well-informed personal medical choices ensures that one makes good public policy decisions about health care
> (D) an understanding of DNA is essential to making well-informed personal medical choices or to making good public policy decisions about health care
> (E) since 90 percent of the population believes itself to be well informed about health care, at least 70 percent of the population is mistaken in that belief

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion** At least 80 percent of people lack enough medical knowledge for sound personal medical choices or public health policy decisions.

**Evidence** Only 20 percent know enough about DNA to understand a DNA news story. The 90 percent self-assessment is background contrast.

**Evaluate** The arithmetic gives 80 percent who cannot understand that story. Why must understanding DNA be required for every well-informed medical or policy decision? One could make an informed choice about a broken bone without understanding DNA. The argument treats a particular subject as a necessary condition for broad health-care judgment.

**Goal** Look for the unproved requirement: DNA knowledge is essential to the decisions in the conclusion.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Backwards.** This asks whether DNA understanding is *enough* for good choices. The argument needs to show that DNA understanding is *required* for good choices. Even if some DNA experts make poor choices, the inference about people without DNA knowledge remains the target.

**(B) Out of Scope.** The conclusion describes what individuals know. It does not establish a particular quota of informed people needed for policy to be good.

**(C) Bad Description.** The argument does not infer policy skill from personal medical skill. It draws both conclusions from lack of DNA knowledge.

**(D) Correct.** To infer that the 80 percent who cannot understand a DNA story also cannot make informed personal or policy decisions, DNA understanding would have to be essential to those decisions. The evidence never establishes that requirement.

**(E) Out of Scope.** The 90 percent self-report might imply that many overestimate their knowledge *if* the DNA criterion were valid. But the objection concerns whether the criterion itself is required for informed choices.

## 10. PT104-S4-Q10 · Flaw · Difficulty 1 · Answer B

*Modelled on PT113-S2-Q3 (conditional reversal), PT124-S2-Q11 (alternative route to a result), and PT154-S1-Q9 (diagram the conditional).*

> Unplugging a peripheral component such as a “mouse” from a personal computer renders all of the software programs that require that component unusable on that computer. On Fred’s personal computer, a software program that requires a mouse has become unusable. So it must be that the mouse for Fred’s computer became unplugged.
>
> The argument is most vulnerable to which one of the following criticisms?
>
> (A) It contains a shift in the meaning of “unusable” from “permanently unusable” to “temporarily unusable.”
> (B) It treats an event that can cause a certain result as though that event is necessary to bring about that result.
> (C) It introduces information unrelated to its conclusion as evidence in support of that conclusion.
> (D) It attempts to support its conclusion by citing a generalization that is too broad.
> (E) It overlooks the possibility that some programs do not require a peripheral component such as a mouse.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion** Fred's mouse became unplugged.

**Evidence** Unplugging a required component makes a program unusable; Fred's mouse-requiring program is unusable.

**Evaluate** The rule says *mouse unplugged → program unusable*. Fred has the result, so the argument runs backward to the cause. But a broken mouse, corrupted software, or another failure might also make the program unusable. We need no proof that one of those happened. Their possibility shows that unusability does not establish disconnection.

**Goal** Find the confusion of a sufficient cause with a necessary cause of the result.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Flaw.** “Unusable” need not shift between permanent and temporary meanings. A program can be unusable for several reasons under the same ordinary meaning.

**(B) Correct.** An unplugged mouse *can* cause unusability. The argument treats unplugging as though it *must* be the cause whenever such a program is unusable. This is the illegal reversal of the conditional.

**(C) Bad Description.** The general fact about required peripherals is related to the conclusion. It just does not establish that this specific peripheral was unplugged.

**(D) Bad Description.** The generalization may be perfectly true. The flaw is applying it backward, not making it too broad.

**(E) Out of Scope.** Fred's program explicitly requires a mouse. Whether other programs need no peripherals has no bearing on why this one became unusable.

## 11. PT105-S1-Q26 · Flaw · Difficulty 2 · Answer D

*Modelled on PT113-S2-Q3 (possible alternatives), PT154-S2-Q12 (false choice), and PT110-S2-Q11 (possibility versus necessity).*

> Marion knows that the bridge on her usual route is closed and that, unless she takes the train instead of driving, she can get to work on time only by leaving at least 45 minutes early. She must go to her neighborhood bank before leaving for work, however, and the bank will not be open in time for her to do so if she leaves 45 minutes early. Therefore, since she hates taking the train, Marion cannot avoid being late for work.
>
> The reasoning in the argument is flawed because the argument
>
> (A) mistakes a situation that almost certainly affects many people for one that affects a particular person alone
> (B) ignores the fact that people often know that something is the case without considering all the consequences that follow from its being the case
> (C) assumes without justification that because people generally have an interest in avoiding a given result, any particular person will have an interest in avoiding that result
> (D) treats evidence that someone will adopt a particular course of action as though that evidence excluded the possibility of an alternative course of action
> (E) overlooks the possibility that someone might occasionally adopt a given course of action without having a good reason for doing so

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion** Marion cannot avoid being late for work.

**Evidence** Driving on time requires leaving at least 45 minutes early, which conflicts with her bank visit. The train is the other route, but she hates taking it.

**Evaluate** Hating a route gives evidence that Marion probably will not choose it. It does not make the route unavailable. She could take a train she dislikes and arrive on time after visiting the bank. The argument changes a prediction about her preference into a claim of impossibility.

**Goal** Find the distinction between a course she is unlikely to take and a course she *cannot* take.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** The argument is about Marion's particular schedule, not a problem mistakenly treated as hers alone.

**(B) Bad Description.** Marion knows the bridge is closed. The flaw is not that she overlooks a consequence of a known fact; the author overlooks her ability to choose the train despite disliking it.

**(C) Out of Scope.** This choice tempts because Marion's preferences matter to the story. But it proposes that *people generally* want to avoid being late, while the argument's evidence is specific: Marion hates taking the train. Even if she wants very badly to be on time, she still has the train option. The flaw is converting dislike of that option into its impossibility, not projecting a general human preference onto Marion.

**(D) Correct.** Her hatred of trains is evidence she may choose not to take one. It does not exclude that alternative. A disfavored option is still an option, and taking it could defeat the conclusion that she cannot avoid being late.

**(E) Not an Objection.** The train is a possible route whether or not Marion has a “good reason” to take it. The issue is availability, not whether an occasional choice lacks a good reason.

## 12. PT105-S2-Q15 · Flaw · Difficulty 2 · Answer C

*Modelled on PT147-S1-Q6 (sample and target population), PT150-S2-Q1 (selection), and PT120-S3-Q8 (denominator).*

> A report of a government survey concluded that Center
> City was among the ten cities in the nation with the
> highest dropout rate from its schools. The survey data
> were obtained by asking all city residents over the age
> of 19 whether they were high school graduates and
> computing the proportion who were not. A city school
> official objected that the result did not seem accurate
> according to the schools’ figures.
>
> The school official can most properly criticize the
reasoning by which the survey report reached its result
for failure to do which one of the following?
>
> (A) take into account instances of respondents’ dropping out that occurred before the respondents reached high school
> (B) ask residents whether they had completed their high school work in fewer than the usual number of years
> (C) distinguish between residents who had attended the city’s schools and those who had received their schooling elsewhere
> (D) predict the effect of the information contained in the report on future high school dropout rates for the city
> (E) consider whether a diploma from the city’s high schools signaled the same level of achievement over time

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion of the survey report** Center City has one of the nation's ten highest dropout rates *from its schools*.

**Method** Ask all current city residents over 19 whether they graduated from high school and count those who did not.

**Evaluate** A resident's schooling may have happened in another city. If many adult residents moved into Center City after dropping out elsewhere, their answers raise the measured share of nongraduates without saying anything about Center City's schools. Conversely, former students who left the city disappear from the sample. The measured population and the claimed school population are different.

**Goal** Find the missing distinction between current residents and people who attended the city's schools.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** The survey asks whether respondents graduated from high school, so dropping out before reaching high school may sound like a counting issue. But such a respondent would still be a nongraduate. The central problem is whose schools the nongraduation reflects. Someone who left school before high school in another city is still no evidence about Center City's schools.

**(B) Bad Evidence Match.** Finishing high school early still makes one a graduate. The survey already asks whether the person graduated, not how long it took.

**(C) Correct.** The survey counts Center City residents without separating those educated in Center City from those educated elsewhere. A dropout in another city's schools cannot establish a dropout from Center City's schools. The report labels its sample as if it measured the target.

**(D) Out of Scope.** The report makes a claim about current or past dropout rates; predicting how publicity affects future rates would not validate that claim.

## 13. PT105-S4-Q8 · Flaw · Difficulty 3 · Answer B

> Linguist: Only if a sentence can be diagrammed is it grammatical. Any grammatical sentence is recognized as grammatical by speakers of its language. Speaker X’s sentence can be diagrammed. So, speaker X’s sentence will be recognized as grammatical by speakers of its language.
>
> The linguist’s reasoning is flawed because it fails to consider the possibility that
>
> (A) most people are unable to diagram sentences correctly
> (B) some ungrammatical sentences are diagrammable
> (C) all sentences recognized as grammatical can be diagrammed
> (D) all grammatical sentences can be diagrammed
> (E) some ungrammatical sentences are recognized as ungrammatical

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion** Speakers of X's language will recognize X's sentence as grammatical.

**Evidence** Only diagrammable sentences can be grammatical. Every grammatical sentence is recognized as grammatical. X's sentence can be diagrammed.

**(A) Out of Scope.** Whether most people can diagram sentences is about people's skill. The premise says *a sentence can be diagrammed*, not that ordinary speakers can draw its diagram.

**(C) Not an Objection.** This sounds close because it mentions recognized and diagrammable, the words on both ends of the argument. But it runs *recognized → diagrammable*. The author needs *diagrammable → grammatical* before the existing *grammatical → recognized* rule can be used. Even if every recognized sentence is diagrammable, a diagrammable sentence may still be ungrammatical and unrecognized.

**(D) Bad Description.** “Only if diagrammable is it grammatical” already says every grammatical sentence can be diagrammed. Repeating a premise cannot fix its reversal.

**(E) Bad Description.** Some ungrammatical sentences being recognized as ungrammatical says nothing about whether X's diagrammable sentence is grammatical.

> The reasoning in the argument is questionable because the argument
>
> (A) fails to provide an adequate definition of the word “messages”
> (B) infers that there is no extraterrestrial intelligence in neighboring star systems from the lack of proof that there is
> (C) assigns too little importance to the possibility that there is extraterrestrial intelligence beyond our neighboring star systems
> (D) neglects to mention that some governments have sent meticulously prepared messages and recordings on spacecraft

**(E) Not an Objection.** Even if most systems are uninhabited, the conclusion says *none* of the neighboring systems has intelligence. A high general probability cannot establish that absolute claim from silence.

>

> The argument is most vulnerable to the criticism that it
>

## 18. PT107-S4-Q25 · Flaw · Difficulty 2 · Answer E

## 20. PT108-S2-Q18 · Flaw · Difficulty 2 · Answer B

*Modelled on PT155-S1-Q20 (rebuttal target), PT154-S1-Q12 (matching the actual claim), and PT154-S2-Q12 (test the shifted scenario).*

> Sid: The sign says “Keep off the grass.” Micki: I know, but just one person walking across the grass doesn’t hurt it. Sid: Your statement is false. If everyone believed as you do, everyone would walk across the grass, and the grass would die.
>
> Sid’s argument is questionable in that it
>
> (A) attempts to use a statement about the consequences of actions to disprove a statement about the actions themselves
> (B) treats a statement about the consequences of an action as though it were instead about the consequences of everyone believing the statement
> (C) contradicts itself by treating a statement that the arguer does not believe as though it were a statement believed by everyone
> (D) discounts the fact that there may be circumstances under which hurting the grass is justified
> (E) attempts to undermine a statement by calling into question the character of the person making the statement

### Argument Breakdown

**Micki's claim** One person walking across the grass does not hurt it.

**Sid's conclusion** Micki's claim is false.

**Sid's evidence** If everyone believed Micki, everyone would walk across the grass and it would die.

**Evaluate** Micki spoke about *one walker*. Sid switches to a world where *everyone* walks. Trampling by everyone may kill grass while a single crossing leaves it unharmed. The result of universal adoption does not contradict the original statement about one action.

**Goal** Find the shift from the consequence of one person's walking to the consequence of everyone believing and acting on that claim.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** This is the most attractive distractor because Sid does shift the scenario. But Micki's original statement already concerns a consequence: whether one person's crossing *hurts* the grass. Sid also talks about a consequence: the grass dying after universal crossing. He does not exchange a statement about consequences for one about actions themselves. He changes *one person's action* to *everyone's actions*.

**(B) Correct.** Micki says one person's crossing does not hurt the grass. Sid argues about what happens if *everyone believes* that and crosses. The effect of universal belief and behavior cannot by itself disprove the claim about a single crossing.

**(C) Contradiction.** Sid can consider a hypothetical belief held by everyone without personally believing it. The error is using the hypothetical mass behavior to rebut Micki's one-person claim.

**(D) Out of Scope.** Micki does not claim that harming grass is justified in some circumstances; she claims one person's crossing does not harm it. Justification is irrelevant to whether her factual claim is false.

**(E) Not Ad Hominem.** Sid does not criticize Micki's character. He criticizes what he takes to be the effect of general acceptance of her statement.
