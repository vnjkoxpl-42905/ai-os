# Batch 13, part B: Strengthen, Sufficient Assumption, Evaluate

Every gap labelled Strengthen, Strengthen/Explain, Sufficient Assumption, Evaluate, Argument Evaluation or Unknown, plus PT144-S4-Q14 (filed as Miscellaneous; the stem is Sufficient Assumption). Some carry partial explanations already, so those slots hold only the missing notes or steps.

Types follow the stem, not the filing label: the two "Strengthen/Explain" slots and PT122-S1-Q8 ask for an explanation, so they're written as Paradox. PT133-S1-Q1 asks for a completion, so it's written as Most Strongly Supported.

Modelled on `models/Strengthen-SA.md` and `models/Weaken-Evaluate.md`. Labels: Conclusion / Evidence / Evaluate / Goal; Paradox slots use Fact / Evaluate / Goal.

## PT107-S1-Q3 | Strengthen | C

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Gasohol should be used more.

**Evidence:** Gasohol has a higher octane rating and lower carbon monoxide emissions than gasoline. Burning it adds no more carbon dioxide than plants remove.

**Evaluate:** This is an EXCEPT question. Four choices give another reason to prefer gasohol over gasoline. The answer gives no such reason, or gives a reason against.

**Goal:** Find the choice that doesn't favor gasohol.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Strengthens.** Cars run better on gasohol. Another advantage.

**(B) Strengthens.** Gasohol saves gasoline, which makes an energy shortage less likely.

**(C) Correct.** Burning more gasohol per kilometer is a cost of gasohol, not a benefit. It doesn't help the argument.

**(D) Strengthens.** Gasohol is cheaper at the pump.

**(E) Strengthens.** The premise says gasohol adds no more CO2 than plants remove. If gasoline adds more than plants can remove, gasohol has a real advantage over gasoline.

## PT108-S3-Q12 | Strengthen | B

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The facility shouldn't be placed under this mountain until scientists investigate whether it has any geological faults.

**Evidence:** A fault could let waste seep out or water seep in after a few thousand years. Nuclear waste stays dangerous for up to 25,000 years, so seepage would be disastrous.

**Evaluate:** The geologist makes the investigation the condition for going ahead. That only makes sense if the investigation can settle the question. If the scientists might come back unsure, waiting for them wouldn't solve anything.

**Goal:** Show the investigation would actually answer whether faults exist.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Irrelevant.** Whether civilization survives doesn't bear on whether to investigate first. If anything, it makes the danger seem less pressing.

**(B) Correct.** If the investigation gives a definite answer, it's worth waiting for. That's what the geologist's plan relies on.

**(C) Irrelevant.** What the facility was first meant for doesn't affect the fault risk.

**(D) Weakens.** A big cost increase is a reason against requiring the investigation.

**(E) Irrelevant.** Temporary storage doesn't address the long-term seepage risk the argument is about.

## PT114-S1-Q4 | Strengthen | C

### Argument Breakdown

**Opposing Point:** A chemist charged the anthropologist with fraud for not reporting a negative test for toxin T.

**Conclusion:** The test results were invalid.

**Evidence:** The powder had accidentally been tested in an acidic solution.

**Evaluate:** The anthropologist's defense depends on the acid mattering. If acid doesn't affect the test, the negative result stands. We need a link between acid and a false negative.

**Goal:** Show that an acidic solution would hide toxin T.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Target.** Field evidence supports the hypothesis that the powder contains toxin T. It doesn't show this test was invalid because of the acid.

**(B) Irrelevant.** Storage time is a different possible problem. The counterargument rests on the acid.

**(C) Correct.** If toxin T can't be detected in acid, the negative result means nothing. The test couldn't have found it.

**(D) Irrelevant.** A fresh batch being available doesn't make the acid matter.

**(E) Irrelevant.** The hypothesis is about a significant amount. A test that misses very small amounts could still detect that.

## PT122-S1-Q8 | Paradox | D

### Argument Breakdown

**Fact:** Vervet monkeys use different alarm calls depending on whether the predator is on land or in the air.

**Evaluate:** Why would the warning need to say where the danger is? One general alarm would tell the monkeys to flee. A separate call is useful only if the right response depends on where the predator comes from.

**Goal:** Find a reason the monkeys need to know whether the threat is on land or in the air.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Irrelevant.** Pitch signals how many predators there are. That doesn't explain separate calls for land and air.

**(B) Irrelevant.** Different land predators kill different numbers of monkeys. That says nothing about land versus air.

**(C) Incomplete.** This is the most popular trap. It shows the predators fall cleanly into two groups. It doesn't show why the monkeys need to tell those groups apart. If the escape were the same either way, one call would do.

**(D) Correct.** The monkeys climb trees to escape land predators and dive into foliage to escape predators in the air. The call tells them which escape to use.

**(E) Irrelevant.** What the predators eat doesn't change how the monkeys should escape.

**(D) Irrelevant.** Comparing pests to other harmful organisms doesn't address aphids.

**(E) Irrelevant.** Harming other plants more doesn't mean these aphids leave potatoes alone.

## PT159-S1-Q16 | Strengthen | A

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The chimpanzee behaviors studied are probably determined by culture rather than genetics.

**Evidence:** One East African group behaved more like the two West African groups, a different subspecies, than like the other East African groups of its own subspecies.

**Evaluate:** If genes drove the behavior, groups of the same subspecies should behave alike. One group breaks that pattern, so the author points to culture. The argument is stronger if chimps can pick up behaviors from others regardless of relatedness, because that's how culture would spread.

**Goal:** Give a way for behavior to pass between chimps without shared genes.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Chimps copy behavior whether or not they're closely related. That's a mechanism for culture to override subspecies lines.

**(B) Irrelevant.** Newcomers bring both behaviors and genes, so this can't separate culture from genetics.

**(C) Weakens.** Different environments are an alternative explanation for the behavior differences.

**(D) Irrelevant.** Each group being distinct doesn't say whether culture or genes made it so.

**(E) Irrelevant.** How close the groups live doesn't explain why the odd East African group matches distant West African groups.

## PT159-S3-Q12 | Strengthen | D

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Sleep deprivation is probably responsible for the spike in traffic accidents on the day after clocks move forward.

**Evidence:** Many people lose an hour of sleep when daylight saving time begins. The next day, accidents run 7 percent above average for that time of year.

**Evaluate:** The argument moves from a correlation to a cause. Other things change that day too. A strengthener could rule out another cause or show a detail that only sleep loss predicts.

**Goal:** Tie the extra accidents specifically to drowsiness.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Weakens.** People late for appointments may rush. That's another possible cause of accidents.

**(B) Irrelevant.** The comparison is already to the average for that time of year, so the seasonal difference doesn't matter.

**(C) Irrelevant.** How severe the accidents are says nothing about their cause.

**(D) Correct.** The extra accidents cluster later in the day, when sleep-deprived people are drowsiest. Sleep loss predicts exactly that timing.

**(E) Irrelevant.** If effects lingered for days, you'd expect the spike to last too. That doesn't help explain the one-day spike.

## PT132-S4-Q7 | Paradox | C

### Argument Breakdown

**Fact:** Over three decades, the average beak size of a captive population stayed the same, while the average beak size of a wild population of the same species shrank significantly.

**Evaluate:** Same species, different trends. Something about life in the wild changed beak size there and not in captivity. Natural selection is the obvious candidate.

**Goal:** Find a pressure in the wild that favored small beaks over time.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Incomplete.** This is the trap. If small-beaked birds were always easier to catch, every wild average would come out small. That doesn't explain a decrease over time.

**(B) Opposite.** This would make the wild average look larger, not smaller.

**(C) Correct.** A changing food supply favored small-beaked birds, so more of them survived and the average shrank. Captive birds weren't exposed to it.

**(D) Irrelevant.** The captive birds' body size says nothing about why wild beaks shrank.

**(E) Irrelevant.** Measuring some birds twice doesn't produce a steady trend.

## PT133-S3-Q12 | Paradox | A

**Fact:** Young children, whose coordination is still developing, catch faster balls more easily than slow ones.

**Evaluate:** You'd expect slow balls to be easier for children whose coordination is still developing. The answer needs something about fast balls that helps children catch them.

**Goal:** Find a feature of faster balls that makes them easier to catch.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Fast balls trigger brain regions that track objects for self-defense. That gives fast balls an advantage slow balls lack.

**(B) Opposite.** This gives slow balls an advantage, which deepens the puzzle.

**(C) Irrelevant.** The puzzle is about children, not adults.

**(D) Irrelevant.** Throwing accuracy isn't catching.

**(E) Irrelevant.** A speed limit doesn't explain why faster is easier below that limit.

## PT147-S4-Q10 | Sufficient Assumption | A

### Argument Breakdown

**Opposing Point:** Critics say that as news reporting gets more entertaining, its caliber drops.

**Conclusion:** The critics are mistaken.

**Evidence:** The greatest journalists have been the most entertaining.

**Evaluate:** To refute the critics, we need a case of maximum entertainment and high caliber together. We know the greatest journalists were the most entertaining. We don't yet know their reporting was of high caliber, since "great" could mean something else.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** The most entertaining reporting was also the highest caliber. That directly contradicts the critics.

**(C) Too Weak.** "Valuable in some sense" isn't the same as high caliber.

**(D) Too Weak.** The critics never said entertainment and news exclude each other. They said caliber drops as entertainment rises.

**(E) Irrelevant.** The worst journalists don't tell us about the top of the scale.

## PT145-S2-Q9 | Sufficient Assumption | B

## PT106-S2-Q7 | Evaluate | E

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** It is safer for a dog to get the vaccine than not.

**Evidence:** One in 50 dogs that contract the disease die from it. The vaccine is almost 100 percent effective. Vaccine complications kill one dog per 5,000 vaccinations.

**Evaluate:** The argument compares the wrong numbers. One in 50 is the death rate for dogs that catch the disease, not for all dogs. If very few unvaccinated dogs ever catch it, the real risk without the vaccine could be lower than 1 in 5,000.

**Goal:** Ask how likely an unvaccinated dog is to catch the disease.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Irrelevant.** Deaths from all causes don't compare these two risks.

**(B) Irrelevant.** The argument is about dogs.

**(C) Irrelevant.** Deaths from other diseases don't affect this comparison.

**(D) Irrelevant.** Rabies and other diseases are outside the comparison.

**(E) Correct.** If 1 in 1,000 unvaccinated dogs catches the disease, the risk of dying from it is 1 in 50,000, lower than the vaccine's risk. If most catch it, the vaccine is safer. The answer decides the argument.
