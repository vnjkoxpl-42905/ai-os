## PT111-S1-Q11 | Method | E

> Linguist: Some people have understood certain studies as showing that bilingual children have a reduced “conceptual map” because bilingualism overstresses the child’s linguistic capacities. Vocabulary tests taken by bilingual children appear to show that these children tend to have a smaller vocabulary than do most children of the same age group. But these studies are deeply flawed, since the tests were given in only one language. Dual-language tests revealed that the children often expressed a given concept with a word from only one of their two languages.
>
> The linguist’s argument proceeds by
>
> (A) offering evidence for the advantages of bilingualism over monolingualism
> (B) pointing out an inconsistency in the view that bilingualism overstresses a child’s linguistic capabilities
> (C) offering evidence that undermines the use of any vocabulary test to provide information about a child’s conceptual map
> (D) providing a different explanation for the apparent advantages of bilingualism from the explanation suggested by the results of certain studies
> (E) pointing out a methodological error in the technique used to obtain the purported evidence of a problem with bilingualism

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The studies suggesting bilingual children have a reduced "conceptual map" are deeply flawed.

**Evidence:** The vocabulary tests were given in only one language. Dual-language tests showed the children often knew a concept through a word in just one of their two languages.

**Evaluate:** The linguist does not argue that bilingualism is good, or that the children's vocabulary is fine. The attack is on how the evidence was gathered. Test a bilingual child in one language and you miss every concept they hold in the other. That is a flaw in the method of collecting the data.

**Goal:** Pointing out a flaw in how the evidence against bilingualism was gathered.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** The linguist never argues bilingualism is better than knowing one language, only that this evidence against it is flawed.

**(B) Bad Description.** No inconsistency is shown in the opposing view. The problem is in the testing, not in the view's logic.

**(C) Too Strong.** "Any vocabulary test" goes too far. The linguist's own evidence comes from vocabulary tests, given in both languages. Only the one-language tests are the problem.

**(D) Bad Description.** The studies claimed a *disadvantage* of bilingualism, not an advantage, and the linguist offers no rival explanation for one.

**(E) Correct.** The methodological error is testing in only one language. The purported evidence is the smaller vocabulary scores. The problem with bilingualism is the supposed shrunken conceptual map.

Opponent of offshore oil drilling: The projected benefits of drilling new oil wells in certain areas in the outer continental shelf are not worth the risk of environmental disaster. The oil already being extracted from these areas currently provides only 4 percent of our country’s daily oil requirement, and the new wells would only add one-half of 1 percent.Proponent of offshore oil drilling: Don’t be ridiculous! >
> The drilling proponent’s reply to the drilling opponent proceeds by
>
> (A) offering evidence in support of drilling that is more decisive than is the evidence offered by the drilling opponent
> (B) claiming that the statistics cited as evidence by the drilling opponent are factually inaccurate
> (C) pointing out that the drilling opponent’s argument is a misapplication of a frequently legitimate way of arguing
> (D) citing as parallel to the argument made by the drilling opponent an argument in which the conclusion is strikingly unsupported
> (E) proposing a conclusion that is more strongly supported by the drilling opponent’s evidence than is the conclusion offered by the drilling opponent

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Proponent) The opponent's argument is ridiculous.

**Evidence:** By the same logic, new farms should not be allowed, since no single new farm could feed the country for more than a few minutes.

**Evaluate:** The opponent says the new wells add too little to be worth it. The proponent does not dispute the 4 percent or the half of 1 percent. Instead, they copy the shape of the argument ("this adds only a tiny share, so it is not worth it") onto farms, where the conclusion is plainly silly. If the same reasoning gives an absurd answer elsewhere, something is wrong with it.

**Goal:** Citing a parallel argument whose conclusion is plainly unsupported.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** The proponent offers no evidence for drilling at all. The reply is a parallel argument, not more data.

**(B) Bad Description.** The proponent never disputes the 4 percent or the half of 1 percent.

**(C) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. It sounds sophisticated, but the proponent never says the opponent's way of arguing is usually legitimate. The farm example says it fails, full stop.

**(D) Correct.** The parallel argument is the farms. Its conclusion, that no new farms should be allowed, is strikingly unsupported, which shows the opponent's reasoning is just as weak.

**(E) Bad Description.** The proponent does not propose a better conclusion from the opponent's evidence.

## PT112-S1-Q1 | Method | A

> Joanna: The only way for a company to be successful, after emerging from bankruptcy, is to produce the same goods or services that it did before going bankrupt. It is futile for such a company to try to learn a whole new business. Ruth: Wrong. The Kelton Company was a major mining operation that went into bankruptcy. On emerging from bankruptcy, Kelton turned its mines into landfills and is presently a highly successful waste-management concern.
>
> Ruth uses which one of the following argumentative techniques in countering Joanna's argument?
>
> (A) She presents a counterexample to a claim.
> (B) She offers an alternative explanation for a phenomenon.
> (C) She supports a claim by offering a developed and relevant analogy.
> (D) She undermines a claim by showing that it rests on an ambiguity.
> (E) She establishes a conclusion by excluding the only plausible alternative to that conclusion.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Ruth) Joanna is wrong.

**Evidence:** Kelton went from mining to landfills after bankruptcy and is now highly successful.

**Evaluate:** Joanna's claim is absolute: the *only* way to succeed after bankruptcy is to stay in the same business. A single company that switched businesses and thrived breaks it. Kelton is that company. "Only" claims are made to be broken by one case.

**Goal:** A counterexample to Joanna's claim.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Kelton is the counterexample. It changed businesses after bankruptcy and succeeded, which is exactly what Joanna said cannot happen.

**(B) Bad Description.** No phenomenon gets a new explanation. Ruth just shows Joanna's rule fails.

**(C) Bad Description.** Kelton is not an analogy. It is a real case of exactly the kind Joanna was talking about.

**(D) Bad Description.** Nothing in Joanna's claim depends on a word with two meanings.

**(E) Bad Description.** Ruth is knocking down a claim, not building one by ruling out alternatives.

> It is widely believed that eating chocolate can cause acne. Indeed, many people who are susceptible to acne report that, in their own experience, eating large amounts of chocolate is invariably followed by an outbreak of that skin condition. However, it is likely that common wisdom has mistaken an effect for a cause. Several recent scientific studies indicate that hormonal changes associated with stress can cause acne and there is good evidence that people who are fond of chocolate tend to eat more chocolate when they are under stress.
>
> The argument employs which one of the following argumentative strategies?
>
> (A) It cites counterevidence that calls into question the accuracy of the evidence advanced in support of the position being challenged.
> (B) It provides additional evidence that points to an alternative interpretation of the evidence offered in support of the position being challenged.
> (C) It invokes the superior authority of science over common opinion in order to dismiss out of hand the relevance of evidence based on everyday experience.
> (D) It demonstrates that the position being challenged is inconsistent with certain well-established facts.
> (E) It provides counterexamples to show that, contrary to the assumption on which the commonly held position rests, causes do not always precede their effects.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Common wisdom has probably mistaken an effect for a cause: chocolate likely does not cause acne.

**Evidence:** Stress hormones can cause acne, and chocolate lovers eat more chocolate when they are stressed.

**Evaluate:** The author accepts the observation that chocolate binges are followed by breakouts. What changes is the explanation. Stress causes both: it brings on the acne, and it brings on the chocolate. So the correlation is real, but chocolate is a side effect of the stress, not the cause of the acne. The author adds new evidence (the studies) to support that new reading.

**Goal:** Adding evidence that supports a different explanation of the same evidence.

## PT112-S3-Q10 | Method | E

> Peter: Because the leaves of mildly drought-stressed plants are tougher in texture than the leaves of abundantly watered plants, insects prefer to feed on the leaves of abundantly watered plants. Therefore, to minimize crop damage, farmers should water crops only just enough to ensure that there is no substantial threat, from a lack of water, to either the growth or the yield of the crops. Jennifer: Indeed. In fact, a mildly drought-stressed plant will divert a small amount of its resources from normal growth to the development of pesticidal toxins, but abundantly watered plants will not.
>
> Jennifer's comment is related to Peter's argument in which one of the following ways?
>
> (A) It offers information that supports each of the claims that Peter makes in his argument.
> (B) It supports Peter's argument by supplying a premise without which Peter's conclusion cannot properly be drawn.
> (C) It supports Peter's argument by offering an explanation of all of Peter's premises.
> (D) It supports one of Peter's premises although it undermines Peter's conclusion.
> (E) It supports the conclusion of Peter's argument by offering independent grounds for that conclusion.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Jennifer) Peter is right, and here is another reason.

**Evidence:** Mildly drought-stressed plants put some of their resources into making pesticidal toxins. Well-watered plants do not.

**Evaluate:** Peter's reason for watering lightly is that insects prefer the tender leaves of well-watered plants. Jennifer's fact has nothing to do with leaf texture. It is a second, separate reason for the same advice: stressed plants defend themselves with toxins. Same conclusion, a new road to it.

**Goal:** Supporting Peter's conclusion with an independent reason.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Too Strong.** This is the most popular wrong answer. Jennifer does support Peter, but not *each* of his claims. Her toxin point says nothing about leaf toughness, Peter's actual premise.

**(B) Bad Description.** Peter's argument works fine without Jennifer's point. She adds support; she does not fill a gap he needed.

**(C) Too Strong.** She does not explain any of Peter's premises, let alone all of them.

**(D) Opposite.** Jennifer's point helps Peter's conclusion. Nothing in it undermines it.

**(E) Correct.** The toxins are independent grounds. They support "water lightly" without relying on Peter's point about leaves.

> A newspaper article on Britain's unions argued that their strength was declining. The article's evidence was the decreasing number and size of strikes, as if the reason for the unions' existence was to organize strikes. Surely, in a modern industrial society, the calling of a strike is evidence that the negotiating position of the union was too weak. Strong unions do not need to call strikes. >

>
> (A) questioning the accuracy of the statistical evidence that the newspaper article uses
> (B) detailing historical changes that make the newspaper article's analysis outdated
> (C) reinterpreting evidence that the newspaper article uses as indicating the opposite of what the newspaper concludes
> (D) arguing that the newspaper article's conclusion is motivated by a desire to change the role of unions
> (E) pointing to common interests among unions and management which the newspaper article ignores

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The article is wrong that Britain's unions are getting weaker.

**Evidence:** Calling a strike is a sign a union's negotiating position was too weak. Strong unions do not need to strike.

**Evaluate:** The article and the author look at the same fact: fewer and smaller strikes. The article reads it as weakness. The author flips it: if strikes signal weakness, fewer strikes signal strength. Same evidence, opposite meaning.

**Goal:** Reinterpreting the article's evidence as showing the opposite of its conclusion.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** The author accepts that strikes are fewer and smaller. Only the meaning is disputed.

**(B) Bad Description.** No history of changes is laid out.

**(C) Correct.** The evidence is the drop in strikes. The article says it shows declining strength; the author says it shows the opposite.

**(D) Bad Description.** The article's motives never come up.

**(E) Bad Description.** Common goals are mentioned as what strong unions can pursue, but the argument's move is reinterpreting the strike numbers.

## PT113-S2-Q13 | Method | C

> Adam: Marking road edges with reflecting posts gives drivers a clear view of the edges, thereby enabling them to drive more safely. Therefore, marking road edges with reflecting posts will decrease the annual number of road accidents. Aiesha: You seem to forget that drivers exceed the speed limit more frequently and drive close to the road edge more frequently on roads that are marked with reflecting posts than on similar roads without posts, and those are driving behaviors that cause road accidents.
>
> Aiesha responds to Adam's argument by
>
> (A) questioning Adam's assertion that reflecting posts give drivers a clear view of road edges
> (B) presenting a possible alternative method for decreasing road accidents
> (C) raising a consideration that challenges the argument's assumption that facilitating safe driving will result in safer driving
> (D) denying that the drivers' view of the road is relevant to the number of road accidents
> (E) providing additional evidence to undermine the claim that safer driving does not necessarily reduce the number of road accidents

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Aiesha, implied) Reflecting posts may not reduce accidents.

**Evidence:** Drivers on roads with reflecting posts speed more and drive closer to the edge, and those behaviors cause accidents.

**Evaluate:** Adam's chain is: posts make safe driving possible, so accidents will drop. Hidden in the middle is an assumption: if people *can* drive more safely, they *will*. Aiesha attacks exactly that. The posts make drivers feel safe, so they drive more recklessly.

**Goal:** Challenging the assumption that making safe driving easier leads to safer driving.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** Aiesha accepts that the posts give a clear view. That is the second-speaker rule: she leaves Adam's premise alone.

**(B) Bad Description.** She proposes no other way to reduce accidents.

**(C) Correct.** "Facilitating safe driving" is the clear view of the edges. "Safer driving" is what people actually do. Aiesha's facts show the first does not guarantee the second.

**(D) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. But Aiesha never says the view is irrelevant. Her point is about how drivers *respond* to the better view.

**(E) Bad Description.** Read it slowly: it has Aiesha attacking the claim that safer driving does not necessarily reduce accidents. No one makes that claim, and her evidence points the other way.

> Lydia: Red squirrels are known to make holes in the bark of sugar maple trees and to consume the trees' sap. Since sugar maple sap is essentially water with a small concentration of sugar, the squirrels almost certainly are after either water or sugar. Water is easily available from other sources in places where maple trees grow, so the squirrels would not go to the trouble of chewing holes in trees just to get water. Therefore, they are probably after the sugar. Galina: It must be something other than sugar, because the concentration of sugar in the maple sap is so low that a squirrel would need to drink an enormous amount of sap to get any significant amount of sugar.
>
> Lydia’s argument proceeds by
>
> (A) dismissing potentially disconfirming data
> (B) citing a general rule of which the conclusion is a specific instance
> (C) presenting an observed action as part of a larger pattern of behavior
> (D) drawing an analogy between well-understood phenomena and an unexplained phenomenon
> (E) rejecting a possible alternative explanation for an observed phenomenon

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Lydia) The squirrels are probably after the sugar.

**Evidence:** Sap is basically water with a little sugar, so the squirrels want one or the other. Water is easy to find elsewhere, so they would not chew holes just for water.

**Evaluate:** Two candidates: water or sugar. Lydia rules out water, so sugar wins. This is elimination: set up the possible explanations, knock one out, keep what remains.

**Goal:** Rejecting the alternative explanation to support the remaining one.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** Lydia does not dismiss any data. Galina brings up the sugar concentration, not Lydia.

**(B) Bad Description.** No general rule is applied to a specific case.

**(C) Bad Description.** This is the most popular wrong answer. But Lydia does not place the sap-drinking within a bigger pattern of squirrel behavior. She reasons about this one behavior by eliminating a motive.

**(D) Bad Description.** No analogy to a better-understood case.

**(E) Correct.** The alternative explanation is that the squirrels want water. Lydia rejects it because water is easy to find elsewhere.

> Professor Beckstein: American Sign Language is the native language of many North Americans. Therefore, it is not a foreign language, and for that reason alone, no student should be permitted to satisfy the university's foreign language requirement by learning it. **(C) Bad Description.** Sedley never questions Beckstein's authority.

**(D) Bad Description.** No facts get a new explanation.

**(E) Opposite.** Sedley rejects Beckstein's conclusion. He does not agree with it.

> Marie: I gave the cashier at my local convenience store a 10-dollar bill to pay for my purchase, and he mistakenly gave me change for a 20-dollar bill. I kept the extra 10 dollars. Since I did not trick, threaten, or physically force the cashier into giving me the extra money, it was not morally wrong for me to keep it. Julia: Nonsense. If you mistakenly hand me your coat, thinking it is my coat, then even though I did not get the coat by means of any deception, threats, or violence against you, I am not morally entitled to keep it for myself.
>
> Julia's response functions in which one of the following ways?
>
> (A) It strongly questions the application of Marie's principle to the case that Marie described, while accepting that principle.
> (B) It offers an additional reason to accept Marie's conclusion.
> (C) It challenges Marie's conclusion by claiming that the proper conclusion to draw in a relevantly similar situation would be the opposite of Marie's.
> (D) It uses Marie's criterion as a means of solving a moral problem Julia herself faces.
> (E) It proposes a radically different principle by which Marie's action might be judged, but reserves judgment as to whether Marie acted rightly.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** (Julia, implied) Marie was wrong to keep the money.

**Evidence:** If someone mistakenly hands you their coat, you are not entitled to keep it, even though you used no trickery, threats or force.

**Evaluate:** Marie's principle is that without deception, threats or force, keeping something is fine. Julia builds a case that meets all of Marie's conditions (no trickery, no threats, no force) where keeping the item is clearly wrong. The coat case is just like Marie's, and it points the other way.

**Goal:** Challenging Marie's conclusion with a similar case where the opposite conclusion is clearly right.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** Julia does not accept Marie's principle. Her coat case shows the principle itself is wrong.

**(B) Opposite.** Julia argues against Marie, not for her.

**(C) Correct.** The coat case is relevantly similar: a mistaken handover, with no trickery. And the right conclusion there is the opposite of Marie's.

**(D) Bad Description.** Julia is not solving a problem of her own.

**(E) Bad Description.** Julia does not hold back judgment. "Nonsense" makes her verdict clear.

> Tom: Critics of recent high court decisions claim that judges' willingness to abide by earlier decisions is necessary to avoid legal chaos. Mary: High courts have repudiated precedents in the past, but they were careful to do so only when the previous rulings were old and had clearly become outdated. **(D) Bad Description.** Mary does not show that Tom's own evidence contradicts his assumption.

**(E) Correct.** The two kinds are old, outdated precedents and recent ones. The difference Tom ignores is that only the recent kind makes the law look unstable.
