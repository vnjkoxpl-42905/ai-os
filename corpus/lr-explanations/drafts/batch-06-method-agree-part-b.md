# Batch 06, part B: the last NA slot and Agree/Disagree

PT119-S3-Q20 (Necessary Assumption) closes the NA set. It has steps in all three explanation styles, but its standard explanation has no choice notes, so it gets five notes. (On 2026-10-01 I misread this as "two overlapping step sets"; it is the normal three-style layout. Nothing is deleted.)

The other ten are the first ten Agree/Disagree gaps, all in the database with no explanation. PT102-S3-Q16 is filed as Dispute and PT110-S3-Q7 as Point at Issue; both are the same task.

Modelled on `models/MC-Role-Agree.md` (Agree / Disagree):
- Go through speaker 1's claims one at a time and ask whether speaker 2 takes the opposite side.
- Test every choice for both positions: can we support where each speaker stands? Name the side that is missing, in that speaker's words.
- Separate "is" from "should", and "can" from "will".

Labels: [Speaker 1]'s Position / [Speaker 2]'s Position / Evaluate / Goal. Tags: Agree vocabulary (Correct, Half Scope, Both Agree, Out of Scope, Too Strong).

## PT119-S3-Q20 | Necessary Assumption | C

**Notes only.** The existing steps in all three styles stay as they are.

> Scientist: Genetic engineering has aided new developments in many different fields. But because these techniques require the manipulation of the genetic codes of organisms, they are said to be unethical. What the critics fail to realize is that this kind of manipulation has been going on for millennia; virtually every farm animal is the result of selective breeding for desired traits. Since selective breeding is genetic engineering of a crude sort, genetic engineering is not unethical.
>
> Which one of the following is an assumption on which the scientist's argument depends?
>
> (A) The manipulation of the genetic code of organisms is never unethical.
> (B) Anything that is accomplished by nature is not unethical to accomplish with science.
> (C) The manipulation of the genetic code through selective breeding for desired traits is not unethical.
> (D) The manipulation of the genetic code through selective breeding for desired traits is important for human survival.
> (E) Science can accomplish only what is already in some sense natural, and nothing natural is unethical.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Too Strong.** "Never unethical" covers every possible case. The scientist only needs selective breeding, and genetic engineering like it, to be acceptable.

**(B) Out of Scope.** This is the most popular wrong answer, and it is easy to see why: it sounds like a defense of genetic engineering. But the scientist's argument is about a long human *practice* (selective breeding), not about what nature does. Negate B and the argument still stands.

**(C) Correct.** Negate it: "selective breeding for desired traits *is* unethical." Then pointing out that we have done it for millennia only shows we have been doing something wrong for a long time. It no longer clears genetic engineering, and the conclusion falls.

**(D) Out of Scope.** Whether selective breeding is important for survival says nothing about whether it is ethical.

**(E) Too Strong.** A sweeping claim about science and nature that the argument never uses.

## PT102-S3-Q16 | Dispute | C

> Motorcoach driver: Professional drivers spend much more time driving, on average, than do other people and hence are more competent drivers than are other, less experienced drivers. Therefore, the speed limit on major highways should not be reduced, because that action would have the undesirable effect of forcing some people who are now both law-abiding and competent drivers to break the law.

Police officer: All drivers can drive within the legal speed limit if they wish, so it is not true to say that reducing the speed limit would be the cause of such illegal behavior.
>
> The point at issue between the motorcoach driver and the police officer is whether
>
> (A) it would be desirable to reduce the speed limit on major highways
> (B) professional drivers will drive within the legal speed limit if that limit is reduced
> (C) reducing the speed limit on major highways would cause some professional drivers to break the law
> (D) professional drivers are more competent drivers than are other, less experienced drivers
> (E) all drivers wish to drive within the speed limit

### Argument Breakdown

**Motorcoach driver's Position:** Lowering the speed limit would force some competent, law-abiding drivers to break the law, so it should not be lowered.

**Police officer's Position:** Every driver can stay under the limit if they want to, so a lower limit would not *cause* anyone to break the law.

**Evaluate:** The officer answers only one of the driver's claims: that a lower limit would *cause* lawbreaking. The officer says nothing about whether professional drivers are more competent, or whether lowering the limit is a good idea. The clash is on cause.

**Goal:** Whether lowering the speed limit would cause some drivers to break the law.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Half Scope.** This is the most popular wrong answer. The driver clearly thinks lowering the limit is undesirable. But the officer never says whether it is a good idea. They only dispute the "cause" claim, so we cannot support the officer's side here.

**(B) Half Scope.** The officer says drivers *can* stay within the limit, not that they *will*. "Can" and "will" are different claims.

**(C) Correct.** The driver says a lower limit would force lawbreaking. The officer says it would not be the cause. That is the disagreement.

**(D) Half Scope.** The driver says professionals are more competent. The officer does not address competence.

**(E) Out of Scope.** Neither speaker talks about what all drivers *wish*.

## PT106-S1-Q6 | Agree/Disagree | D

> Hospital auditor: The Rodriguez family stipulated that the funds they donated to the neurological clinic all be used to minimize patients’ suffering. The clinic administration is clearly violating those terms, since it has allocated nearly one fifth of those funds for research into new diagnostic technologies, instead of letting that money flow directly to its patients.

Clinic administrator: But the successful development of new technologies will allow early diagnosis of many neurological disorders. In most cases, patients who are treated in the early stages of neurological disorders suffer far less than do patients who are not treated until their neurological disorders reach advanced stages.
>
> Which one of the following is the main point at issue between the hospital auditor and the clinic administrator?
>
> (A) Whether early treatment of many neurological disorders lessens the suffering associated with those disorders rather than completely eliminating such suffering
> (B) Whether the patients being treated at the neurological clinic are currently receiving adequate treatment for the neurological disorders from which they suffer
> (C) Whether the Rodríguez family clearly stipulated that the funds they donated to the neurological clinic be used to minimize patients’ suffering
> (D) Whether the neurological clinic is adhering strictly to the conditions the Rodríguez family placed on the allocation of the funds they donated to the clinic
> (E) Whether the Rodríguez family anticipated that some of the funds they donated to the neurological clinic would be used to pay for research into new diagnostic technologies

### Argument Breakdown

**Hospital auditor's Position:** The clinic is violating the family's terms by spending a fifth of the money on research instead of patients.

**Clinic administrator's Position:** The research leads to early diagnosis, and early treatment means far less suffering.

**Evaluate:** The administrator never says "we are following the terms." But the reply only makes sense as a defense: research *does* minimize suffering, so spending on it fits the family's condition. Underneath, they disagree about whether the clinic is following the terms.

**Goal:** Whether the clinic is following the family's conditions.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Half Scope.** Only the administrator talks about the effect of early treatment, and "rather than completely eliminating" adds a distinction neither makes.

**(B) Out of Scope.** Neither speaker discusses whether current patients get adequate treatment.

**(C) Both Agree.** Both accept the family's stipulation. They disagree about whether the clinic is honoring it.

**(D) Correct.** The auditor says the clinic is violating the terms. The administrator's reply is a defense that it is not.

**(E) Out of Scope.** What the family anticipated never comes up.

## PT107-S4-Q16 | Agree/Disagree | D

> Franklin: It is inconsistent to pay sports celebrities ten times what Nobel laureates are paid. Both have rare talents and work hard. Tomeka: What you’ve neglected to consider is that unlike Nobel laureates, sports celebrities earn millions of dollars for their employers in the form of gate receipts and TV rights.
>
> Franklin’s and Tomeka’s statements provide the most support for holding that they disagree about the truth of which one of the following?
>
> (A) Nobel laureates should be taken more seriously.
> (B) Nobel laureates should be paid more than sports celebrities.
> (C) Sports celebrities and Nobel laureates work equally hard for their employers.
> (D) There is no rational basis for the salary difference between sports celebrities and Nobel laureates.
> (E) The social contributions made by sports celebrities should be greater than they currently are.

### Argument Breakdown

**Franklin's Position:** Paying sports stars ten times what Nobel laureates earn is inconsistent, since both have rare talent and work hard.

**Tomeka's Position:** Unlike laureates, sports stars earn millions for their employers.

**Evaluate:** "Inconsistent" means there is no good reason for the gap. Tomeka gives exactly that: a reason. Revenue for employers explains the pay difference. So they disagree about whether the gap has a rational basis.

**Goal:** Whether there is a rational basis for the salary difference.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** Neither speaker talks about taking laureates seriously.

**(B) Too Strong.** This is the most popular wrong answer. Franklin finds the gap inconsistent, but he never says laureates should earn *more* than athletes. Equal pay would fix the inconsistency too.

**(C) Half Scope.** Franklin says both work hard. Tomeka does not dispute it.

**(D) Correct.** Franklin says the gap is inconsistent, meaning no rational basis. Tomeka supplies one: revenue.

**(E) Out of Scope.** Neither speaker argues about what social contributions should be.

## PT108-S2-Q1 | Agree/Disagree | D

> Jorge: It is obvious that a shift in economic policy is needed, so why not proceed with the necessary changes all at once? If one wants to jump over a chasm, one would do it with one leap. Christina: I disagree with your view, even though I agree that a shift in economic policy is needed. If one wants to teach a horse to jump fences, one should train it to jump lower heights first.
>
> Jorge and Christina disagree over whether
>
> (A) a shift in economic policy is not needed
> (B) revising current economic policy incrementally is like teaching a horse to jump fences
> (C) the faster current economic policy is revised, the less painful the initial changes will be
> (D) the economic changes should not all be made at the same time
> (E) the current economic situation is grave

### Argument Breakdown

**Jorge's Position:** Make all the needed changes in economic policy at once, the way you would jump a chasm in one leap.

**Christina's Position:** She agrees a shift is needed, but compares it to training a horse: start with lower jumps first.

**Evaluate:** Christina says it out loud: she agrees a shift is needed. So that is common ground. The disagreement is about pace: all at once or step by step.

**Goal:** Whether the changes should all be made at once.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Both Agree.** Christina explicitly agrees a shift is needed. So does Jorge.

**(B) Half Scope.** The horse analogy is Christina's. Jorge never addresses it.

**(C) Out of Scope.** Neither speaker talks about how painful the changes would be.

**(D) Correct.** Jorge wants everything at once. Christina wants it gradual.

**(E) Out of Scope.** Neither says how grave the situation is.

## PT108-S3-Q26 | Agree/Disagree | D

> Anthropologist: After mapping the complete dominance hierarchy for a troupe of vervet monkeys by examining their pairwise interaction, we successfully predicted more complex forms of their group behavior by assuming that each monkey had knowledge of the complete hierarchy. Since our prediction was so accurate, it follows that the assumption we used to reach it was in fact true.

Primatologist: Although I agree that your assumption helped you make those predictions, your conclusion does not follow. You might as well argue that since we can predict the output of some bank cash machines by assuming that these machines actually want to satisfy the customers’ requests, these cash machines must really have desires.
>
> Which one of the following is a point about which the anthropologist and the primatologist are committed to disagreeing?
>
> (A) whether the anthropologist successfully predicted the behavior of individual monkeys by use of the map of the troupe’s dominance hierarchy
> (B) whether the output of a bank cash machine can be accurately predicted on the basis of knowledge of the requests made to it by customers
> (C) whether vervet monkeys can have knowledge of the complete hierarchy of dominance relations that exists within their own troupe
> (D) whether the fact that the anthropologist’s assumption led to such successful predictions provides sufficient grounds for the claim that the vervet monkeys had knowledge of their dominance hierarchy
> (E) whether the behavior exhibited by vervet monkeys in experimental situations can be used as the basis for a generalization concerning all vervet monkeys

### Argument Breakdown

**Anthropologist's Position:** The predictions were so accurate that the assumption behind them (each monkey knows the full hierarchy) must be true.

**Primatologist's Position:** The assumption helped the predictions, but that does not prove it is true, any more than predicting cash machines proves they have desires.

**Evaluate:** The primatologist agrees the predictions worked. What they reject is the step from "useful for prediction" to "true." So the disagreement is whether the successful predictions are enough to show the monkeys knew the hierarchy.

**Goal:** Whether the successful predictions are enough to show the monkeys knew the hierarchy.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Both Agree.** The primatologist concedes the predictions were successful.

**(B) Out of Scope.** The cash machine is the primatologist's comparison. Neither speaker disputes whether machine output can be predicted.

**(C) Half Scope.** This is the most popular wrong answer. The anthropologist says the monkeys do know the hierarchy. But the primatologist never says they *cannot*. They only say the evidence does not prove it. "Unproven" is not "false."

**(D) Correct.** The anthropologist says the predictions are sufficient grounds. The primatologist says the conclusion "does not follow."

**(E) Out of Scope.** Generalizing to all vervet monkeys never comes up.

## PT110-S3-Q7 | Point at Issue | E

> Dr. Jones: The new technology dubbed “telemedicine” will provide sustained improvement in at least rural patient care since it allows rural physicians to televise medical examinations to specialists who live at great distances—specialists who will thus be able to provide advice the rural patient would otherwise not receive. Dr. Carabella: Not so. Telemedicine might help rural patient care initially. However, small hospitals will soon realize that they can minimize expenses by replacing physicians with technicians who can use telemedicine to transmit examinations to large medical centers, resulting in fewer patients being able to receive traditional, direct medical examinations. Eventually, it will be the rare individual who ever gets truly personal attention. Hence, rural as well as urban patient care will suffer.
>
> Which one of the following is a point at issue between Dr. Jones and Dr. Carabella?
>
> (A) whether medical specialists in general offer better advice than rural physicians
> (B) whether telemedicine technology will be installed only in rural hospitals and rural medical centers
> (C) whether telemedicine is likely to be widely adopted in rural areas in future years
> (D) whether the patients who most need the advice of medical specialists are likely to receive it through telemedicine
> (E) whether the technology of telemedicine will benefit rural patients in the long run

### Argument Breakdown

**Dr. Jones's Position:** Telemedicine will bring sustained improvement to rural patient care.

**Dr. Carabella's Position:** It may help at first, but in the long run rural and urban care will suffer.

**Evaluate:** Carabella grants a short-term benefit, so that is not the dispute. The key word in Jones's claim is "sustained." Carabella says the long run gets worse. So they disagree about the long run.

**Goal:** Whether telemedicine will benefit rural patients over the long run.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** Neither compares the quality of specialists' advice with rural doctors'.

**(B) Out of Scope.** Where telemedicine gets installed never comes up.

**(C) Out of Scope.** Neither speaker disputes whether telemedicine will be adopted. Carabella's worry assumes it will be.

**(D) Half Scope.** This is the most popular wrong answer. Jones talks about specialists' advice reaching rural patients, but Carabella never addresses whether the patients who need it most will get it. Her worry is about personal attention in general.

**(E) Correct.** Jones says "sustained improvement" for rural care. Carabella says rural care "will suffer" eventually.

## PT112-S3-Q4 | Agree/Disagree | B

> Mary: Computers will make more information available to ordinary people than was ever available before, thus making it easier for them to acquire knowledge without consulting experts. Joyce: As more knowledge became available in previous centuries, the need for specialists to synthesize and explain it to nonspecialists increased. So computers will probably create a greater dependency on experts.
>
> The dialogue most strongly supports the claim that Mary and Joyce disagree with each other about whether
>
> (A) computers will contribute only negligibly to the increasing dissemination of knowledge in society
> (B) computers will increase the need for ordinary people seeking knowledge to turn to experts
> (C) computers will make more information available to ordinary people
> (D) dependency on computers will increase with the increase of knowledge
> (E) synthesizing knowledge and explaining it to ordinary people can be accomplished only by computer experts

### Argument Breakdown

**Mary's Position:** Computers will make it easier for ordinary people to gain knowledge without consulting experts.

**Joyce's Position:** As knowledge has grown, the need for specialists to explain it has grown too, so computers will probably create more dependence on experts.

**Evaluate:** Both accept that computers will spread information. They split on what happens next: Mary says people will rely on experts less, Joyce says more.

**Goal:** Whether computers will increase people's need for experts.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Both Agree.** Both treat computers as spreading knowledge widely. Neither says the contribution will be negligible.

**(B) Correct.** Mary says computers will reduce reliance on experts. Joyce says they will increase it.

**(C) Both Agree.** Mary says it, and Joyce's whole argument starts from more knowledge being available.

**(D) Out of Scope.** Dependence on *computers* is not the issue. Joyce is talking about dependence on *experts*.

**(E) Too Strong.** Neither says only computer experts can explain knowledge.

## PT112-S4-Q21 | Agree/Disagree | E

> Tina: For centuries oceans and human eccentricity have been linked in the literary and artistic imagination. Such linkage is probably due to the European Renaissance practice of using ships as asylums for the socially undesirable. Sergio: No. Oceans have always been viewed as mysterious and unpredictable—qualities that people have invariably associated with eccentricity.
>
> Tina's and Sergio's statements lend the most support to the claim that they disagree about which one of the following statements?
>
> (A) Eccentric humans were considered socially undesirable during the European Renaissance.
> (B) Oceans have always been viewed as mysterious and unpredictable.
> (C) The linkage between oceans and eccentricity explains the European Renaissance custom of using ships as asylums.
> (D) People have never attributed the same qualities to oceans and eccentrics.
> (E) The linkage between oceans and eccentricity predates the European Renaissance.

### Argument Breakdown

**Tina's Position:** The link between oceans and eccentricity probably comes from the Renaissance practice of using ships as asylums.

**Sergio's Position:** No. Oceans have always seemed mysterious and unpredictable, qualities people have always tied to eccentricity.

**Evaluate:** Look at the timing each speaker implies. If the link comes from a Renaissance practice, it started in the Renaissance. Sergio's "always" puts it earlier. So they disagree about whether the link existed before the Renaissance.

**Goal:** Whether the link between oceans and eccentricity predates the Renaissance.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** Tina mentions using ships for the socially undesirable, but neither speaker debates whether eccentrics were considered undesirable.

**(B) Half Scope.** Sergio says oceans were always seen as mysterious. Tina never addresses it, so we cannot support her side.

**(C) Opposite.** This is the most popular wrong answer, and it flips Tina's claim. She says the Renaissance custom explains the link. This says the link explains the custom. Neither speaker says that.

**(D) Opposite.** Sergio says people *have* attributed the same qualities to both. And Tina never says they have not.

**(E) Correct.** Tina's explanation dates the link to the Renaissance. Sergio's "always" places it earlier.

## PT113-S2-Q11 | Agree/Disagree | D

> Mark: To convey an understanding of past events, a historian should try to capture what it was like to experience those events. For instance, a foot soldier in the Battle of Waterloo knew through direct experience what the battle was like, and it is this kind of knowledge that the historian must capture. Carla: But how do you go about choosing whose perspective is the valid one? Is the foot soldier's perspective more valid than that of a general? Should it be a French or an English soldier? Your approach would generate a biased version of history, and to avoid that, historians must stick to general and objective characterizations of the past.
>
> Mark's and Carla's positions indicate that they disagree about the truth of which one of the following?
>
> (A) The purpose of writing history is to convey an understanding of past events.
> (B) The participants in a battle are capable of having an objective understanding of the ramifications of the events in which they are participating.
> (C) Historians can succeed in conveying a sense of the way events in the distant past seemed to someone who lived in a past time.
> (D) Historians should aim to convey past events from the perspective of participants in those events.
> (E) Historians should use fictional episodes to supplement their accounts of past events if the documented record of those events is incomplete.

### Argument Breakdown

**Mark's Position:** Historians should try to capture what it was like to experience past events, the way a foot soldier knew the battle.

**Carla's Position:** Choosing one participant's view would bias history, so historians must stick to general, objective accounts.

**Evaluate:** Mark recommends the participant's-eye view. Carla rejects it in favor of the general, objective view. That is a direct "should" clash about how history ought to be written.

**Goal:** Whether historians should convey events from participants' perspectives.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** Neither disputes the purpose of history. Carla's objection is about method, not purpose.

**(B) Out of Scope.** This is the most popular wrong answer. But neither speaker says whether participants understand events *objectively*. Mark talks about direct experience, and Carla about the historian's bias.

**(C) Half Scope.** Carla does not say historians *cannot* convey a participant's view, only that they should not.

**(D) Correct.** Mark says historians should capture the participant's experience. Carla says they should stick to general characterizations.

**(E) Out of Scope.** Fiction never comes up.

## PT113-S3-Q1 | Agree/Disagree | D

> Moralist: TV talk shows are contributing to the moral decline in our country. By constantly being shown the least moral people in our society, viewers begin to think that such people are the norm, and that there is something wrong with being morally upright. TV talk show host: Well, if there is such a decline, it's not because of TV talk shows: we simply show people what they want to see. What can be wrong with letting the viewers decide? Furthermore, if restrictions were put on my show, that would amount to censorship, which is wrong.
>
> The moralist's and the TV talk show host's statements provide the most support for holding that they disagree about whether
>
> (A) TV talk shows should be censored
> (B) people's moral standards have changed
> (C) TV talk shows influence people's conception of what is the norm
> (D) TV talk shows, by presenting immoral guests, are causing a moral decline
> (E) it is wrong not to let the viewers decide what they want to see

### Argument Breakdown

**Moralist's Position:** TV talk shows are contributing to moral decline by showing the least moral people as normal.

**Talk show host's Position:** If there is a decline, talk shows are not the cause. They show people what they want to see, and restricting them would be censorship.

**Evaluate:** The moralist's main claim is causal: the shows are causing decline. The host's first move denies exactly that. The censorship point is the host's alone. The moralist never proposes restrictions.

**Goal:** Whether talk shows are causing a moral decline.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Half Scope.** The host brings up censorship, but the moralist never says the shows should be censored.

**(B) Out of Scope.** The host says "*if* there is such a decline." He neither accepts nor denies that standards have changed.

**(C) Half Scope.** This is the most popular wrong answer. The moralist says the shows shape viewers' sense of what is normal. But the host never denies that influence. He denies that the shows cause a *decline*.

**(D) Correct.** The moralist says the shows are causing decline. The host says "it's not because of TV talk shows."

**(E) Half Scope.** The host thinks letting viewers decide is fine. The moralist never takes a position on it.
