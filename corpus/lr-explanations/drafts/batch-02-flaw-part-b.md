## PT142-S1-Q5 | Flaw | C

**Modelled on:** PT154-S1-Q12 (ad hominem), PT155-S1-Q20 (rebuttal flaws).

> Critic: Fillmore, an influential television executive, argues that watching television regularly is not detrimental to very young children. Fillmore bases this on the claim, which I grant, that children can learn much that is beneficial from television. But we should reject Fillmore’s argument, because clearly it is to Fillmore’s benefit to convince parents that television is not harmful to their children.
>
> Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the critic’s reasoning?
>
> (A) It takes a necessary condition for something’s being harmful to be a sufficient condition for being harmful.
> (B) It concludes that something is true merely on the grounds that there is no evidence to the contrary.
> (C) It rejects an argument solely on the grounds that the argument could serve the interests of the person making that argument.
> (D) It is based on an appeal to the views of someone with questionable authority on the subject matter.
> (E) It bases its conclusion on claims that are inconsistent with one another.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** We should reject Fillmore's argument.

**Evidence:** Fillmore benefits if parents believe television is harmless.

**Evaluate:** Fillmore has a motive. Does a motive make his argument wrong? A car salesman who tells you the brakes work has a reason to say so, and the brakes may still work. The critic never says what is wrong with Fillmore's reasoning. In fact, the critic grants his premise.

**Goal:** Expect the ad hominem answer: rejecting an argument because of the arguer's interests.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Flaw.** No conditional about harm is reversed. The critic is not reasoning about what makes something harmful at all.

**(B) Bad Description.** The critic does not claim anything is true for lack of contrary evidence. The reason offered is Fillmore's self-interest.

**(C) Correct.** The only reason to reject Fillmore's argument is that it serves Fillmore. A self-serving argument can still be sound. The critic attacks the arguer's motive instead of the argument.

**(D) Bad Description.** The critic is not relying on Fillmore's authority; the critic is rejecting Fillmore's view. Nobody's questionable authority supports the conclusion.

**(E) Compatible.** Granting a premise while rejecting the argument is not inconsistent. You can accept that children learn from television and still deny that television is harmless. The critic's claims fit together; they just do not support rejecting Fillmore.

## PT146-S1-Q7 | Flawed Reasoning | B

**Modelled on:** PT150-S2-Q4 (comparison across settings), PT120-S3-Q23 (past vs present).

> Editorialist: The city council is considering increasing the amount of air traffic allowed at the airport beyond its original design capacity. Several council members say that this increase would not decrease safety as it would be accompanied by the purchase of the latest safety technology. But in fact it would decrease safety. Numerous studies conducted 30 years ago show that safety was reduced at every airport where the permitted level of traffic was increased beyond the airport’s original design capacity, even when those airports made use of the latest safety technology.
>
> Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the editorialist’s argument?
>
> (A) The argument draws a conclusion on the basis of a general statement that has in turn been inferred from a very limited number of particular instances.
> (B) The argument fails to consider the possibility that whether an airport can allow more air traffic than it was originally designed for without reducing safety depends largely on what the latest technology is.
> (C) The argument fails to consider the possibility that the city council members who support the increase are aware of the studies that were conducted 30 years ago.
> (D) The argument confuses an absence of evidence for the claim that the airport can safely permit air traffic in excess of its original design capacity with the existence of evidence against this claim.
> (E) The argument fails to consider that a slight increase in safety risks might be acceptable if it yields overriding benefits of another kind.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Increasing traffic would decrease safety, even with the latest technology.

**Evidence:** Studies from 30 years ago found safety fell at every airport that exceeded design capacity, even with the latest technology of that time.

**Evaluate:** "The latest safety technology" 30 years ago is not the latest technology today. Phones 30 years ago could not stream video; that says nothing about phones now. The studies tell us what the technology of that era could handle. The council members are talking about today's technology. The comparison across 30 years needs the two to be alike, and they may not be.

**Goal:** Look for the difference between then and now: the technology has changed.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Not Sampling.** The studies were "numerous" and covered "every airport" that exceeded capacity. Nothing suggests too few cases. The problem is their age.

**(B) Correct.** If safety at higher traffic depends heavily on *which* technology is in use, results with 30-year-old technology do not predict results with today's. The editorialist treats "latest" then as the same as "latest" now.

**(C) Out of Scope.** Whether council members know about the studies does not affect whether the studies support the conclusion.

**(D) Bad Description.** The editorialist does cite evidence against the claim: the studies. The weakness is that the evidence is outdated, not that the argument mistakes a lack of evidence for evidence.

**(E) Out of Scope.** The conclusion is only that safety would decrease. Whether a decrease is worth it is a separate question the editorialist never raises.

## PT146-S1-Q9 | Flawed Reasoning | A

**Modelled on:** PT134-S3-Q6 (correlation, reverse cause), PT128-S3-Q14 (causal language).

> In a recent field study of prairie plants, the more plant species a prairie plot had, the more vigorously the plants grew and the better the soil retained nutrients. Thus, having more plant species improves a prairie’s ability to support plant life.
>
> The argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that it
>
> (A) infers of two correlated phenomena, X and Y, that X causes Y without considering whether Y causes X
> (B) fails to describe the mechanism by which productivity is supposedly increased
> (C) takes for granted that the characteristics of one prairie plot could reveal something about the characteristics of other prairie plots
> (D) bases a general conclusion on data that is likely to be unrepresentative
> (E) takes an increase in number to indicate an increase in proportion

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** More plant species improve a prairie's ability to support plant life.

**Evidence:** Plots with more species had more vigorous growth and better nutrient retention.

**Evaluate:** More species goes with better growth and richer soil. The author says the species cause the good conditions. Try the reverse. Rich soil that holds nutrients can support more kinds of plants. Then the good conditions would cause the variety, not the other way around.

**Goal:** Expect the reverse-cause answer.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** The study shows a correlation between species count and a plot's ability to support plants. Good soil and growing conditions could be what allows more species to grow. The author assumes the direction runs from species to soil without considering the reverse.

**(B) Out of Scope.** An argument can establish a cause without explaining how it works. Asking for the mechanism is not the flaw.

**(C) Bad Description.** The study compares many plots, and the conclusion is about prairies in general. Nothing hinges on one plot standing in for others.

**(D) Not Sampling.** We are given no reason to think the plots studied were unusual.

**(E) Bad Description.** No proportions appear. The study counts species and measures growth.

## PT146-S3-Q5 | Flawed Reasoning | E

**Modelled on:** PT148-S1-Q2 (numbers in a table), PT144-S3-Q15 (overlapping groups).

> Resident: Data indicates that 30 percent of the houses in our town have inadequate site drainage and 30 percent have structural defects that could make them unsafe. Hence, at least 60 percent of our town’s houses have some kind of problem that threatens their integrity.
>
> The reasoning in the resident’s argument is flawed in that the argument overlooks the possibility that
>
> (A) the town has a relatively small number of houses
> (B) inadequate site drainage can make a house unsafe
> (C) structural defects are often easier to fix than inadequate site drainage
> (D) many houses in the town have neither inadequate site drainage nor structural defects that could make them unsafe
> (E) some of the houses that have structural defects that could make them unsafe also have inadequate site drainage

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** At least 60 percent of houses have a problem.

**Evidence:** 30 percent have poor drainage; 30 percent have structural defects.

**Evaluate:** Use 100 houses. Thirty have drainage problems and thirty have structural defects. If they are different houses, 60 have a problem. But what if they are the same 30 houses? Then only 30 have a problem. Adding the percentages works only if no house is on both lists. "At least 60" is really "somewhere between 30 and 60."

**Goal:** Look for overlap between the two groups.

### Choice Notes

**(A) No Impact.** Percentages work the same whether the town has 100 houses or 100,000. Size does not create or remove overlap.

**(B) No Impact.** A drainage problem already counts as a problem in the resident's math. Whether it also makes a house unsafe does not change how many houses have a problem.

**(C) Out of Scope.** Ease of repair has nothing to do with how many houses have problems now.

**(D) Compatible.** The resident's own claim leaves up to 40 percent with neither problem. "Many houses have neither" fits the argument and does not touch the addition error.

**(E) Correct.** If some houses have both problems, they get counted twice. With overlap, fewer than 60 percent of houses have a problem, so "at least 60 percent" fails.

## PT147-S4-Q16 | Parallel Flaw | D

Bank tag: Flawed Reasoning. The stem asks for the most similar flawed argument, so this is drafted as Parallel Flaw.

**Modelled on:** PT151-S4-Q23 (illegal reversal), PT113-S2-Q3 (diagram the rule).

> Voter: Our prime minister is evidently seeking a job at an international organization. Anyone seeking a job at an international organization would surely spend a lot of time traveling abroad, and our prime minister has spent more days abroad than at home so far this year.
>
> Which one of the following arguments is most similar in its flawed reasoning to the voter’s argument?
>
> (A) Kao must be a golfer. Kao is planning to run for office, and most people who run for office play golf.
> (B) Franklin will lose the coming election. The opposing candidate has better policy ideas and brings more relevant experience to the job.
> (C) Ramirez is evidently able to control the traffic signals. Just now, as Ramirez approached the curb, the traffic signal changed from red to green.
> (D) Thompson must be negotiating a personal loan. Thompson was at the bank yesterday, and people who are negotiating a personal loan go to the bank to meet with a loan agent.
> (E) McKinsey must have committed a crime at some point. After all, despite extensive background checks no one has been able to show that McKinsey has never committed a crime.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The prime minister is seeking a job at an international organization.

**Evidence:** Seeking such a job → lots of travel abroad. The prime minister travels abroad a lot.

**Evaluate:** Diagram it: job seeking → travel. The prime minister has travel. The voter concludes job seeking. That runs the rule backward. Prime ministers travel for summits, trade talks and state visits. Plenty of things lead to travel. Lottery winners are wealthy, but not every wealthy person won the lottery.

**Goal:** Find an argument with a rule "X → Y," a case of Y, and a conclusion of X.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Flaw.** Kao has the *sufficient* side (running for office) and the conclusion is the other side (golfer). The direction is right. The weakness is that "most" does not mean "all." That is a different flaw.

**(B) Wrong Flaw.** There is no conditional at all. This is a prediction from comparative strengths, not a reversed rule.

**(C) Wrong Flaw.** This infers a cause from one coincidence in timing. The voter's argument is conditional, not causal.

**(D) Correct.** Rule: negotiating a loan → go to the bank. Thompson went to the bank. Conclusion: Thompson is negotiating a loan. Same reversal, same shape: people visit banks for many reasons, just as prime ministers travel for many reasons.

**(E) Wrong Flaw.** This treats a claim not proven false (never committed a crime) as proof of its opposite. That is the unproven vs proven false flaw, not a reversal.

## PT147-S4-Q18 | Flawed Reasoning | B

**Modelled on:** PT120-S3-Q8 (one factor is not the only factor), PT150-S2-Q4 (find a relevant difference).

> Pundit: For many high school graduates, attending a university would be of no help in getting a corporate job. The attributes corporations value most in potential employees are initiative, flexibility, and the ability to solve practical problems. Many new high school graduates have these attributes already.
>
> The pundit’s argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that it
>
> (A) fails to establish that university graduates do not have initiative, flexibility, and the ability to solve practical problems
> (B) overlooks the possibility that corporations may require an attribute that potential employees can obtain only by attending a university
> (C) provides no justification for the presumption that corporations only hire employees who have initiative, flexibility, and the ability to solve practical problems
> (D) takes for granted that the only reason that high school graduates go on to attend university is to improve their job prospects
> (E) takes for granted that initiative, flexibility, and the ability to solve practical problems are attributes that can be acquired through study

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** For many high school graduates, university would not help them get a corporate job.

**Evidence:** Corporations value initiative, flexibility and practical problem solving *most*, and many graduates already have them.

**Evaluate:** "Valued most" is not "the only thing required." A corporation may rank initiative first and still refuse to hire anyone without a degree or specialized knowledge. If university supplies something employers require, it helps even graduates who already have the top three attributes.

**Goal:** Look for something else corporations want that only university provides.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Not Assumed.** The pundit need not say university graduates lack these traits. Graduates could have them too; the question is whether university adds anything for people who already have them.

**(B) Correct.** If corporations also require an attribute available only through university, such as a credential or specific training, then attending helps get the job. The pundit's evidence covers only the attributes valued *most*, not everything required.

**(C) Too Strong.** The pundit does not need corporations to hire *only* people with these traits. The argument fails because other requirements might exist, not because of how strict these ones are.

**(D) Out of Scope.** The conclusion is limited to job prospects. Other reasons for attending university do not bear on it.

**(E) Not Assumed.** The pundit points out that many graduates *already* have the traits. Whether study can produce them does not matter for those graduates.

## PT149-S4-Q14 | Flawed Reasoning | B

**Modelled on:** PT139-S4-Q18 (relative vs absolute), PT141-S4-Q3 (anti-causal).

> Columnist: Wildlife activists have proposed that the practice of stringing cable TV lines from the same poles that carry electric power lines should be banned because cable TV lines, while electrically neutral themselves, make it easier for animals to climb near electric power lines, risking electrocution. This particular argument for banning the practice fails, however, since some animals are electrocuted by power lines even where cable TV lines are all underground.
>
> Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the columnist’s reasoning?
>
> (A) It takes a sufficient condition for an argument’s being inadequate to be a necessary condition for its being inadequate.
> (B) It rejects an argument for a proposal merely on the grounds that the proposal would not completely eliminate the problem it is intended to address.
> (C) It fails to consider the additional advantageous effects that a proposal to address a problem might have.
> (D) It rejects an argument by criticizing the argument’s proponents rather than by criticizing its substance.
> (E) It rejects a proposal to address a problem merely on the grounds that other proposals to address the problem would also be effective.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The activists' argument for the ban fails.

**Evidence:** Some animals are electrocuted even where cable lines are underground.

**Evaluate:** The activists claim the cable lines make electrocution *easier*, so removing them would reduce it. The columnist answers that electrocution still happens without them. So what? Seat belts do not prevent every death, yet they reduce deaths. To refute the activists, the columnist would need to show that cable lines do not raise the rate. Showing the rate is not zero without them proves nothing.

**Goal:** Look for "it won't solve the whole problem" being treated as "it won't help."

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Flaw.** The columnist is not reversing a conditional. The error is about how much a proposal must accomplish.

**(B) Correct.** Some electrocutions without overhead cable lines show only that the ban would not end electrocution entirely. The activists only claimed the lines raise the risk. Rejecting their argument because it does not solve everything holds them to a standard they never claimed.

**(C) Out of Scope.** The columnist rejects one argument for the ban. Other benefits the ban might have are beside the point of whether *this* argument works.

**(D) Not Ad Hominem.** The columnist says nothing about the activists themselves.

**(E) Bad Description.** No other proposals are mentioned.

## PT156-S2-Q9 | Flaw | E

**Modelled on:** PT134-S3-Q6 (correlation to causation), PT148-S3-Q14 (overconfident causal wording).

> Editorialist: Evidence shows that restrictions on tobacco advertising have had a significant impact on smoking among adults. A recent survey has shown that a smaller percentage of adults now smoke than at any other time in the last two decades. The decline in the percentage of adults who smoke has been most marked during the last ten years, and, not coincidently, some of the most important restrictions on tobacco advertising came into force ten years ago.
>
> The reasoning in the editorialist’s argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that the argument
>
> (A) fails to consider whether there have been any changes over the last two decades in the percentage of the teenage population who smoke
> (B) uses evidence that describes only a percentage of the adult population to reach a conclusion about the entire adult population
> (C) reaches a conclusion about smoking among today’s adults based on statistics from ten or twenty years ago
> (D) neglects to take into account whether there have been restrictions on the advertising of other products besides tobacco in the past ten years
> (E) fails to consider the possibility that factors other than restrictions on advertising have contributed to the decline in smoking among adults

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Advertising restrictions have significantly reduced adult smoking.

**Evidence:** Adult smoking has fallen, most sharply in the last ten years, and major advertising restrictions began ten years ago.

**Evaluate:** Two things happened in the same decade. That alone does not show one caused the other. What else changed in those ten years? Higher cigarette taxes, smoking bans in restaurants, health campaigns, nicotine patches. Any of these could drive the decline. "Not coincidently" asserts the cause; it does not prove it.

**Goal:** Expect the alternate-cause answer.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** The conclusion is about adults. Teenage smoking rates do not bear on it.

**(B) Bad Description.** The percentage of adults who smoke *is* a measure of the whole adult population. Nothing is generalized from a part.

**(C) Bad Description.** The survey is recent, and it tracks changes across the last twenty years. The conclusion is about that change, not about statistics that are out of date.

**(D) Out of Scope.** Restrictions on advertising other products would not explain a drop in smoking.

**(E) Correct.** Many other factors could have lowered adult smoking in the same decade. Without ruling them out, matching timing does not show the restrictions made a significant difference.

## PT156-S2-Q11 | Flaw | D

**Modelled on:** PT154-S1-Q12 (ad hominem), PT155-S1-Q20 (rebuttal flaws).

> Professor Williams: Professor Thomas’s report characterizes our colleague Professor York as too flamboyant and confrontational in the classroom. But the argument given in this report sounds so much like the classic argument of the self-serving academic that one has to wonder if Professor Thomas simply is not the orator and entertainer Thomas would like to be, and so vents frustrations by condemning others—Professor York in particular.
>
> Professor Williams’s argument is flawed because it
>
> (A) confuses the distinction between being overly confrontational and engaging students by entertaining them
> (B) presupposes the point it is attempting to establish
> (C) mistakes Professor Thomas’s characterization of a view for an endorsement of that view
> (D) attacks Professor Thomas personally rather than addressing Professor Thomas’s argument
> (E) rejects the possibility that Professor York is in fact too confrontational.

Bank note: the stimulus contains an em dash in the original question text. It is quoted verbatim; the explanation text uses none.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion (implied):** Professor Thomas's criticism of York should be doubted.

**Evidence:** Thomas's argument sounds self-serving, so Thomas may be venting jealousy.

**Evaluate:** Suppose Thomas really is jealous. Is York therefore not too confrontational? No. A jealous critic can still be right. Williams never looks at what Thomas actually said about York's teaching. The entire response is speculation about Thomas's motives.

**Goal:** Expect the ad hominem answer.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** Williams draws no distinction between confrontational and entertaining teaching. He talks about Thomas's motives.

**(B) Bad Description.** The argument is weak, not circular. Williams does not assume Thomas is wrong in order to prove it.

**(C) Bad Description.** Williams treats the report as Thomas's own view, which it is. There is no characterization mistaken for endorsement.

**(D) Correct.** Williams attacks Thomas's possible jealousy and frustration rather than the report's reasoning. Even if Thomas is jealous, York might still be too confrontational.

**(E) Bad Description.** This tempts because Williams clearly sides with York. But Williams never denies outright that York could be too confrontational. The flaw is *how* he resists the report: by attacking Thomas.

## PT156-S4-Q14 | Flaw | B

**Lower confidence: check the B note's paraphrase of the abstract key.**

**Modelled on:** PT150-S2-Q4 (comparison across settings), PT107-S4-Q18 (match abstract wording).

> Science writer: Scientists often accept a scientific argument largely for social reasons, such as the argument’s acceptance by other scientists or the prestige of the scientist making the argument. But this is not at all to the sciences’ detriment, for similar social factors strongly influence every human endeavor.
>
> The science writer’s argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that it
>
> (A) takes for granted that the only legitimate reasons for accepting an argument are social reasons
> (B) takes for granted that whether a behavior obstructs the achievement of a particular endeavor’s goals depends on the prevalence of comparable behaviors in other endeavors
> (C) fails to consider the possibility that scientists who accept an argument largely for social reasons also generally consider relevant scientific evidence before accepting the argument
> (D) fails to consider the possibility that many scientists are unaware that they usually accept scientific arguments largely for social reasons
> (E) takes for granted that a behavior that is not detrimental to the sciences will not be detrimental to any other human endeavor

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Accepting arguments for social reasons does not harm the sciences.

**Evidence:** Social factors strongly influence every human endeavor.

**Evaluate:** "Everyone does it" is not "it does no harm here." Cheating happens in many games; it still ruins chess. Science has a particular goal: finding out what is true. Accepting an argument because of who made it could get in the way of that goal no matter how common the habit is elsewhere. The writer moves from *widespread* to *harmless*.

**Goal:** Look for the assumption that how common a behavior is elsewhere decides whether it hurts science.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** The writer never says social reasons are the only legitimate ones. The writer only says relying on them does no harm.

**(B) Correct.** Map the abstract wording. "A behavior" is accepting arguments for social reasons. "A particular endeavor's goals" are science's goals. "Comparable behaviors in other endeavors" is social influence on every human endeavor. The writer treats the fact that social influence is everywhere as settling whether it obstructs science. It does not.

**(C) Strengthens, if anything.** If scientists also weigh the evidence, social reasons are less likely to harm science. That helps the writer rather than exposing a flaw.

**(D) No Impact.** Whether scientists notice their social motives does not affect whether those motives harm science.

**(E) Backwards.** The writer reasons from other endeavors to science, not from science to other endeavors. Nothing is concluded about whether the behavior harms anything else.

## PT156-S4-Q16 | Flaw | D

**Modelled on:** PT143-S3-Q18 (untriggered conditional), PT150-S2-Q4 (plan).

> Manager: I plan to put together a successful marketing team by choosing highly skilled, independent workers who prefer not to work as part of a tightly knit group. This strategy is bound to succeed because recent research shows that when a common purpose is shared, the flexibility of loosely bound groups enables them to function better than tightly knit ones.
>
> The reasoning in the manager’s argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that the argument
>
> (A) presumes that factors that are collectively sufficient for success are also individually necessary for success
> (B) relies on research that has no apparent relevance to the manager’s proposed plan
> (C) takes for granted that flexible teams always function significantly better than inflexible teams
> (D) overlooks the possibility that people who prefer not to work in tightly knit groups may have difficulty committing to a common purpose
> (E) confuses the goals that the manager is seeking to achieve with the goals that must be held in common by the members of the marketing team

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The plan to hire independent workers who avoid tight groups is bound to succeed.

**Evidence:** *When a common purpose is shared*, loose groups function better than tight ones.

**Evaluate:** The research has a condition attached: a shared common purpose. The manager never shows his team will have one. Independent people who dislike tight groups might be exactly the people least likely to rally around a shared goal. If the condition fails, the research tells us nothing about how the team will do.

**Goal:** Look for the untriggered condition: a common purpose.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** The manager does not treat any factor as individually necessary. He relies on one research finding.

**(B) Bad Description.** The research is relevant; it is about loose versus tight groups. The problem is that it applies only under a condition the manager never establishes.

**(C) Too Strong.** The manager does not need flexible teams to be better *always*. His mistake is ignoring the research's own limit, not exaggerating its size.

**(D) Correct.** The research applies only when a common purpose is shared. If independent workers who avoid tight groups struggle to commit to a common purpose, the finding does not apply to them, and "bound to succeed" collapses.

**(E) Bad Description.** The manager does not mix up his own goals with the team's shared goals. He simply never shows there will be a shared goal.

## PT156-S4-Q18 | Flaw | C

**Modelled on:** PT141-S4-Q3 (anti-causal), PT120-S4-Q23 (plan vs status quo).

> Land developer: In a certain nation, stringent regulations prevent private landowners from building on their land if any endangered species is present on it. These regulations make the presence of endangered species a severe financial liability for many landowners and thereby discourage the landowners from protecting the endangered species on their land. Therefore, endangered species would very likely not be harmed by removal of the regulations on land development.
>
> The reasoning in the land developer’s argument is most vulnerable to criticism on which one of the following grounds?
>
> (A) It confuses a condition whose presence would be required in order for a certain result to be produced with a condition whose presence would be required to prevent that result from occurring.
> (B) It justifies a claim containing a value judgment solely on the basis of factual claims that do not in themselves entail that value judgment.
> (C) It unjustifiably overlooks the possibility that even if certain factors tend to produce a given effect, they may be likely to produce stronger countervailing effects as well.
> (D) It fails to take into account the possibility that policies that are not in the interest of land developers may well be in the interest of landowners.
> (E) It fails to account for potential reactions from private landowners who do not have any endangered species on their land.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Removing the regulations would very likely not harm endangered species.

**Evidence:** The regulations make endangered species a financial burden, which discourages landowners from protecting them.

**Evaluate:** Grant the downside: the rules give landowners a reason not to protect species. But look at what the rules do directly. They stop building wherever endangered species live. That protects habitat. Remove the rules, and bulldozers can go in. A cast is uncomfortable and makes you skip exercise, but taking it off early can still do more harm than good. The developer counts the cost of the rules and forgets their main effect.

**Goal:** Look for the net effect: the rules may protect species more than they discourage protection.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** No condition for producing a result is confused with one for preventing it. The argument weighs effects, not conditions.

**(B) Bad Description.** "Would very likely not be harmed" is a prediction, not a value judgment. The flaw is in the prediction.

**(C) Correct.** The regulations tend to discourage landowners from protecting species. They also block construction on land where those species live, a stronger protective effect. The developer looks only at the first effect, so removing the rules could harm species overall.

**(D) Out of Scope.** Developers' and landowners' interests do not decide whether species would be harmed.

**(E) Out of Scope.** Landowners without endangered species are not affected by the rules in question, so their reactions do not bear on harm to species.

## PT156-S4-Q24 | Parallel Flaw | C

Bank tag: Flaw. The stem asks for the argument whose flawed pattern most closely parallels this one, so this is drafted as Parallel Flaw.

**Modelled on:** PT113-S2-Q3 (illegal negation), PT154-S1-Q9 (diagram each conditional).

> A movie studio’s script readers discard all proposed scripts that are not submitted by agents, and they discard all scripts that are incorrectly formatted. So a script that is submitted by an agent and formatted correctly will not be discarded by the studio’s script readers.
>
> Which one of the following exhibits a flawed pattern of reasoning that most closely parallels that in the argument above?
>
> (A) To be accepted, entries in the essay contest must be postmarked by the deadline and accompanied by a completed entry form. March 21 was the deadline. Thus, an entry that is accompanied by a completed entry form but postmarked March 22 will not be accepted.
> (B) If your kitchen is well organized and you are a good cook, the meals you prepare will be delicious. The dinner Jon prepared last week was not at all tasty, so his kitchen must not be well organized.
> (C) If Bob starts exercising daily, he will improve his fitness level. His fitness level will also improve if he quits smoking. So if he refuses to exercise and continues to smoke, his fitness level is not going to improve.
> (D) The police are allowed to ticket any parked car that does not have a parking permit. They are also allowed to ticket any parked car whose parking permit has expired. Thus, the police are going to ticket Ruta’s car, for her parking permit has expired.
> (E) Most dogs that have attended obedience classes respond correctly to basic commands, but only some are able to perform advanced tricks. Thus, any dog that has attended obedience classes and performs advanced tricks will be able to respond correctly to basic commands.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Agent-submitted, correctly formatted scripts will not be discarded.

**Evidence:** Not from an agent → discarded. Incorrectly formatted → discarded.

**Evaluate:** Two separate triggers each lead to the same result. The argument negates both triggers and concludes the result will not happen. That is the illegal negation, twice. Readers could still discard a script for being too long or simply bad. Avoiding the two named reasons does not rule out every other reason.

**Goal:** Find two sufficient conditions for one outcome, both negated, with the outcome denied.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Flaw.** Postmark and entry form are *requirements* (necessary conditions). Missing one means no acceptance. That reasoning is valid, so it cannot match a flawed argument.

**(B) Wrong Flaw.** One combined rule (organized + good cook → delicious), then a failed result, then blame placed on one specific part. The contrapositive only gives "not organized *or* not a good cook." That is a different error from negating two separate triggers.

**(C) Correct.** Exercise → fitness improves. Quit smoking → fitness improves. Bob does neither, so his fitness will not improve. Same structure: two separate sufficient conditions, both absent, outcome denied. Bob could improve his fitness by eating better or sleeping more.

**(D) Wrong Flaw.** The rules say the police are *allowed* to ticket. The conclusion says they *will*. The flaw is treating permission as certainty, not negating two conditions.

**(E) Wrong Flaw.** This mixes "most" and "some" statements. The structure is quantifier-based and does not deny an outcome from two absent triggers.

## PT156-S4-Q25 | Flaw | E

> Translator: Dr. Abner, apparently scornful of my translation of an ancient poem on the grounds that it is inaccurate, says that my translation produces in most readers feelings other than those the original text was intended to produce. >
> The translator’s argument is flawed in that it
>
> (A) fails to adequately address the possibility that the original poem had more than one author
> (B) draws a conclusion purely on the basis of an appeal to popular opinion
> (C) fails to adequately address the possibility that the original text was not intended to produce any particular feelings in the reader
> (D) rejects an argument merely because of the circumstances of the person who offered it
> (E) concludes that we cannot know that certain effects were not what the author intended merely because we do not know what specific effects the author did intend

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Dr. Abner's criticism is unjustified.

**Evidence:** We do not know how the author intended readers to respond.

**Evaluate:** Not knowing exactly what someone wanted does not mean we cannot know what they did *not* want. You may not know what birthday gift your friend hopes for, but you can be sure it is not a bag of wet sand. Abner only claims the translation produces feelings *other than* the intended ones. If the translation of a solemn funeral poem leaves readers laughing, we can be confident that is not what was intended, even without knowing the exact feeling the author aimed for.

**(A) Out of Scope.** Multiple authors would not change the issue. The question is whether we can know some feelings were not intended.

**(B) Bad Description.** The "most readers" claim is Abner's evidence about how the translation affects people. The translator does not appeal to popular opinion.

**(C) Strengthens, if anything.** If the poem was not meant to produce any particular feelings, then the translation could hardly produce the "wrong" ones. That would help the translator, not expose a flaw.

**(D) Not Ad Hominem.** "As Dr. Abner should realize" is a jab, but the translator rejects the criticism on the grounds of what we can know, not because of Abner's circumstances.

**(E) Correct.** The translator moves from "we don't know what feelings the author intended" to "we can't say the translation produces unintended feelings." Some effects can be ruled out even when the specific intended effect is unknown.

>
> (A) contains a premise that presupposes the truth of the conclusion
> (B) fails to make a needed distinction between the best product currently available and the best product that could be produced
> (C) treats a cause of a product being the most expensive as an effect of its being the most expensive
> (D) presumes that because something is true of each of a company’s products it is also true of the company as a whole

> (B) rejects an argument on the grounds that those making it have a vested interest in convincing people of the truth of the conclusion
> (C) assumes that merely because something has not been proved to be true, it must therefore be false
> (D) rejects an argument on the grounds that those making it have failed to abide by what the argument recommends
