## PT109-S3-Q5 | Flaw | D

**Modelled on:** PT150-S2-Q4 (comparison), PT141-S4-Q3 (exposure and baseline), PT139-S4-Q18 (quantity distinction).

> The radiation absorbed by someone during an ordinary commercial airline flight is no more dangerous than that received during an ordinary dental X-ray. Since a dental X-ray does negligible harm to a person, we can conclude that the radiation absorbed by members of commercial airline flight crews will also do them negligible harm.
>
> A flaw in the argument is its failure to consider that
>
> (A) there may be many forms of dangerous radiation other than X-rays and the kinds of radiation absorbed by members of commercial airline flight crews
> (B) receiving a dental X-ray may mitigate other health risks, whereas flying does not
> (C) exposure to X-rays of higher intensity than dental X-rays may be harmful
> (D) the longer and the more often one is exposed to radiation, the more radiation one absorbs and the more seriously one is harmed
> (E) flying at high altitude involves risks in addition to exposure to minor radiation

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Radiation absorbed by airline flight crews does negligible harm.

**Evidence:** Radiation absorbed on *one ordinary commercial flight* is no more dangerous than radiation from *one ordinary dental X-ray*, and one dental X-ray does negligible harm.

**Evaluate:** The comparison changes its unit. A passenger takes one flight; a crew member takes many. Even if one exposure is harmless, repeated exposures might add up to harmful levels. Ask the author to compare cumulative exposure over the same period, not one flight against one X-ray.

**Goal:** Find the objection that dose depends on duration and frequency, which the one-flight comparison omits.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** Other kinds of dangerous radiation do not challenge the conclusion about the radiation crews actually absorb. This would help only if crews were exposed to one of those other kinds.

**(B) No Impact.** A dental X-ray might offset some other risk, but the evidence says the X-ray itself does negligible harm. The conclusion concerns radiation harm, not total health benefits of dentistry versus flying.

**(C) Out of Scope.** Higher-intensity X-rays may be harmful. The argument compares an ordinary dental X-ray to an ordinary flight; it does not claim that every X-ray exposure is harmless.

**(D) Correct.** The more often and longer someone is exposed, the more radiation that person can absorb. Crew members' repeated flights could cause nonnegligible harm even if the radiation from a single flight is as harmless as that from a single dental X-ray. This is the missing difference in the comparison.

**(E) Out of Scope.** Additional risks of flying may matter to airline crews' overall health, but the conclusion is limited to harm from radiation. An objection needs to make the radiation conclusion doubtful.

## PT110-S3-Q4 | Flaw | E

**Modelled on:** PT147-S1-Q6 (unrepresentative sample), PT120-S3-Q8 (rate versus cause), PT109-S4-Q1 (sampling).

> A group of 1,000 students was randomly selected from three high schools in a medium-sized city and asked the question, “Do you plan to finish your high school education?” More than 89 percent answered “Yes. ” This shows that the overwhelming majority of students want to finish high school, and that if the national dropout rate among high school students is high, it cannot be due to a lack of desire on the part of the students.
>
> The reasoning of the argument above is questionable because the argument
>
> (A) fails to justify its presumption that 89 percent is an overwhelming majority
> (B) attempts to draw two conflicting conclusions from the results of one survey
> (C) overlooks the possibility that there may in fact not be a high dropout rate among high school students
> (D) contradicts itself by admitting that there may be a high dropout rate among students while claiming that most students want to finish high school
> (E) treats high school students from a particular medium-sized city as if they are representative of high school students nationwide

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The overwhelming majority of high school students want to finish, so a high *national* dropout rate could not be due to students' lack of desire.

**Evidence:** More than 89 percent of 1,000 randomly selected students at three schools in one medium-sized city said they planned to finish.

**Evaluate:** Random selection can make a sample representative of the population *from which it was drawn*. It does not make three schools in one city representative of the nation. Imagine that other regions have very different dropout pressures or aspirations. Also, a plan to finish is not proof that a student will finish, though the answer set focuses on the first gap.

**Goal:** Look for an objection to projecting a local survey result to students nationwide.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Flaw.** Eighty-nine percent is plainly a large majority. Quibbling over the word "overwhelming" would not expose the national sampling problem.

**(B) Bad Description.** Wanting to finish and dropping out can coexist. The two conclusions concern students' desire and a possible explanation of a dropout rate, not logically conflicting outcomes.

**(C) Not an Objection.** The argument says *if* the national dropout rate is high, lack of desire cannot explain it. It does not need a high rate actually to exist to make that conditional claim.

**(D) Bad Description.** Students can want to finish and still leave school. The author does not contradict himself by discussing both possibilities.

**(E) Correct.** The respondents are from three schools in one medium-sized city, while the conclusion covers high school students nationwide. Even a large, randomly selected sample can be unrepresentative when drawn from the wrong population.

## PT111-S3-Q13 | Flaw | D

**Modelled on:** PT148-S1-Q2 (rate and total), PT120-S4-Q23 (timeframe), PT151-S4-Q23 (track the conclusion's exact claim).

> President of the Regional Chamber of Commerce: We are all aware of the painful fact that almost no new businesses have moved into our region or started up here over the last ten years. But the Planning Board is obviously guilty of a gross exaggeration in its recent estimate that businesses are leaving the region at the rate of about four a week. After all, there were never more than about one thousand businesses in the region, so if they were really leaving at such a rate, they would all have been gone long ago.
>
> The argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the ground that it
>
> (A) focuses on what is going out of a system while ignoring the issue of what is coming into the system
> (B) confuses a claim about a rate of change within a system with a claim about the absolute size of the system
> (C) argues against a position simply by showing that the position serves the interest of the Planning Board
> (D) treats a claim about what is currently the case as if it were a claim about what has been the case for an extended period
> (E) attacks what was offered as an estimate on the ground that it is not precise

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The Planning Board exaggerates when it estimates that businesses are leaving at about four a week.

**Evidence:** The region has never had more than about 1,000 businesses; at four departures per week, all would have been gone long ago.

**Evaluate:** The Board's estimate could describe the *current* departure rate. The president multiplies that rate across an unspecified long past. Even with hardly any arrivals in ten years, departures might have been slower before and increased recently. For instance, a rate of four this week says nothing about how many left each week five years ago.

**Goal:** Find the time shift from a present rate to a sustained historical rate.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** The speaker explicitly notes that almost no new businesses have arrived. The mistake is extending the departure rate backwards, not ignoring incoming businesses.

**(B) Bad Description.** A rate of four departures per week and a stock of at most 1,000 businesses are different quantities, but comparing them *would* be informative if the rate had lasted long enough. The unsupported move is assuming that it did.

**(C) Wrong Flaw.** The president does not attack the Board's interests or motives. He attacks its estimate with a numerical argument.

**(D) Correct.** "Leaving ... at the rate of about four a week" reports a rate now. The president calculates as though businesses had been leaving at that rate for an extended time. That historical premise was never supplied.

**(E) Bad Description.** The president calls the estimate a gross exaggeration, not merely imprecise. His reasoning does not depend on pretending that "about four" is exact.

## PT111-S3-Q25 | Flaw | D

**Modelled on:** PT134-S3-Q6 (causal assumption), PT128-S3-Q14 (correlation language), PT120-S3-Q8 (one factor versus only factor).

> Physician: Heart disease generally affects men at an earlier age than it does women, who tend to experience heart disease after menopause. Both sexes have the hormones estrogen and testosterone, but when they are relatively young, men have ten times as much testosterone as women, and women abruptly lose estrogen after menopause. We can conclude, then, that testosterone tends to promote, and estrogen tends to inhibit, heart disease.
>
> The physician’s argument is questionable because it presumes which one of the following without providing sufficient justification?
>
> (A) Hormones are the primary factors that account for the differences in age-related heart disease risks between women and men.
> (B) Estrogen and testosterone are the only hormones that promote or inhibit heart disease.
> (C) Men with high testosterone levels have a greater risk for heart disease than do postmenopausal women.
> (D) Because hormone levels are correlated with heart disease they influence heart disease.
> (E) Hormone levels do not vary from person to person, especially among those of the same age and gender.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Testosterone tends to promote heart disease, and estrogen tends to inhibit it.

**Evidence:** Men develop heart disease earlier than women on average. Younger men have much more testosterone than younger women, and women lose estrogen after menopause, around the age when their heart disease tends to appear.

**Evaluate:** The patterns line up, but alignment alone does not show that either hormone causes the disease pattern. Other age- or sex-related factors could produce both. We would need evidence that varying these hormone levels changes heart disease risk while relevant alternatives are controlled.

**Goal:** Find the causal leap from correlation between hormone levels and disease timing to hormone effects.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Too Strong.** The physician need not believe hormones are the *primary* explanation. The conclusion says testosterone and estrogen *tend* to influence heart disease, which allows other factors to be important or even dominant.

**(B) Too Strong.** Nothing here requires these to be the only relevant hormones. Another hormone could also promote or inhibit disease.

**(C) Irrelevant Comparison.** The speaker compares men and women at different life stages to suggest effects of hormones. The argument does not need young high-testosterone men to have higher risk than postmenopausal women.

**(D) Correct.** The observed hormone differences and heart disease patterns are correlations. To conclude that testosterone promotes and estrogen inhibits the disease, the physician assumes those correlations reflect causal influence. A shared cause or another sex-related difference could instead explain the pattern.

**(E) Too Strong.** Hormone levels could vary substantially within age and gender groups while the described average patterns still hold. The causal inference does not depend on identical levels among all people in a group.

## PT111-S4-Q8 | Flaw | E

**Modelled on:** PT141-S4-Q3 (equal outcomes mask unequal baselines), PT147-S1-Q6 (measure does not establish broad conclusion), PT134-S3-Q6 (alternate explanation).

> It is widely believed that by age 80, perception and memory are each significantly reduced from their functioning levels at age 30. However, a recent study showed no difference in the abilities of 80-year-olds and 30-year-olds to play a card game devised to test perception and memory. Therefore, the belief that perception and memory are significantly reduced by age 80 is false.
>
> The reasoning above is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that it fails to consider the possibility that
>
> (A) the study’s card game does not test cognitive abilities other than perception and memory
> (B) card games are among the most difficult cognitive tasks one can attempt to perform
> (C) perception and memory are interrelated in ways of which we are not currently aware
> (D) the belief that 80-year-olds’ perception and memory are reduced results from prejudice against senior citizens
> (E) playing the study’s card game perfectly requires fairly low levels of perception and memory

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The belief that perception and memory are significantly reduced by age 80 is false.

**Evidence:** Eighty-year-olds and thirty-year-olds performed equally well on a card game devised to test those abilities.

**Evaluate:** Does the game discriminate between high and moderately reduced ability? Suppose a score of 100 is perfect, and anyone with at least 40 units of perception and memory gets 100. A thirty-year-old with 90 and an eighty-year-old with 50 would tie despite a large decline. Equal game performance does not establish equal underlying ability.

**Goal:** Look for a floor or ceiling problem: the game may be too easy to expose the alleged decline.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** Whether the game tests *other* cognitive abilities does not help assess whether it can detect differences in perception and memory.

**(B) Strengthens, if anything.** If card games are among the hardest cognitive tasks, equal performance could be stronger evidence against the alleged decline. The author needs the game to be sensitive enough; this answer makes that more plausible.

**(C) No Impact.** Perception and memory may interact, but the alleged flaw concerns whether the game can reveal a reduction in either. Interaction alone does not explain the tied scores despite a decline.

**(D) No Impact.** The origin of the belief, even prejudice, would not by itself show that the belief is false or that the study's game can detect an actual decline.

**(E) Correct.** If perfect play takes only fairly low levels of the two abilities, both age groups could play perfectly even though eighty-year-olds' levels are significantly lower. A test with a low ceiling cannot rule out the decline the argument denies.

## PT112-S3-Q25 | Flaw | D

**Modelled on:** PT154-S2-Q12 (false choice), PT134-S3-Q6 (alternative causal stories), PT120-S3-Q8 (one factor versus only factor).

> Anthropologist: All music is based on a few main systems of scale building. Clearly, if the popularity of a musical scale were a result of social conditioning, we would expect, given the diversity of social systems, a diverse mixture of diatonic and nondiatonic scales in the world's music. Yet diatonic scales have always dominated the music of most of the world. Therefore, the popularity of diatonic music can be attributed only to innate dispositions of the human mind.
>
> The anthropologist's argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that it fails to
>
> (A) consider the possibility that some people appreciate nondiatonic music more than they do diatonic music
> (B) explain how innate dispositions increase appreciation of nondiatonic music
> (C) explain the existence of diatonic scales as well as the existence of nondiatonic scales
> (D) consider that innate dispositions and social conditioning could jointly affect the popularity of a type of music
> (E) consider whether any appreciation of nondiatonic music is demonstrated by some nonhuman species of animals

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Diatonic music's popularity can be attributed *only* to innate dispositions of the human mind.

**Evidence:** If social conditioning caused a scale's popularity, the anthropologist expects varied social systems to yield a varied mixture of scales. Instead, diatonic scales dominate much of the world's music.

**Evaluate:** Even if a purely social explanation were inadequate, why must innate disposition act alone? Similar human dispositions could make diatonic music appealing, while social learning reinforces and spreads it. Ruling out one proposed explanation as complete does not rule out a combined explanation.

**Goal:** Find the unjustified either-or: innate influence and social conditioning can both contribute.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Compatible.** Some people could prefer nondiatonic music while diatonic scales still dominate most music. Individual exceptions do not address whether social conditioning contributes to the broad pattern.

**(B) Out of Scope.** The conclusion explains diatonic music's popularity, not how innate dispositions affect appreciation of nondiatonic music.

**(C) Not an Objection.** A theory of why diatonic scales are popular need not explain the existence of every scale. The argument's issue is the exclusivity of the proposed cause.

**(D) Correct.** Innate dispositions and social conditioning might jointly influence popularity. The failure of the anthropologist's prediction for a *purely* social explanation does not show that social conditioning contributes nothing.

**(E) Out of Scope.** The argument concerns patterns in human music and the human mind. Animal appreciation of nondiatonic music would not settle whether social conditioning helps explain human preference for diatonic scales.

## PT112-S4-Q8 | Flaw | E

**Modelled on:** PT154-S1-Q12 (rebuttal structure), PT155-S1-Q20 (conclusion stronger than reply), PT134-S3-Q6 (alternate evidence).

> Archaeologist: The fact that the ancient Egyptians and the Maya both built pyramids is often taken as evidence of a historical link between Old- and New-World civilizations that is earlier than any yet documented. But while these buildings are similar to each other, there are important differences in both design and function. The Egyptian pyramids were exclusively tombs for rulers, whereas the Mayan pyramids were used as temples. This shows conclusively that there was no such link between Old- and New-World civilizations.
>
> Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the archaeologist's argument?
>
> (A) The argument equivocates with respect to the term "evidence."
> (B) The argument appeals to emotion rather than to reason.
> (C) The argument assumes the conclusion it is trying to prove.
> (D) The argument incorrectly relies on words whose meanings are vague or imprecise.
> (E) The argument presumes that no other evidence is relevant to the issue at hand.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** There was *no* early historical link between Old- and New-World civilizations.

**Evidence:** Egyptian and Mayan pyramids differ in design and function; Egyptian ones were tombs, Mayan ones temples.

**Evaluate:** Those differences may weaken one proposed piece of evidence for a link. But proving that these particular buildings were used differently would not settle whether some other evidence establishes contact. Nor does a historical link require identical building designs or uses. The conclusion "no such link" reaches well past the evidence about pyramids.

**Goal:** Look for the move from challenging one line of evidence to treating the whole historical question as settled.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Flaw.** "Evidence" is used consistently as support for a historical link. The archaeologist disputes how strong the pyramid comparison is; no second meaning of the word drives the inference.

**(B) Wrong Flaw.** The argument cites design and function, not an emotional reaction intended to replace evidence.

**(C) Wrong Flaw.** The premise is about differences between pyramids; it does not simply restate the claim that there was no historical link.

**(D) Wrong Flaw.** The terms are sufficiently clear for the argument's purpose. The problem remains even if every feature and use is defined precisely.

**(E) Correct.** Differences between the pyramids at most challenge one proposed indication of contact. The categorical conclusion assumes there is no other relevant evidence of an early link. That assumption is unsupported.

## PT113-S4-Q16 | Flaw | C

**Modelled on:** PT147-S1-Q6 (population mismatch), PT150-S2-Q4 (salient difference), PT134-S3-Q6 (overlooked alternative).

> In determining the authenticity of a painting, connoisseurs claim to be guided by the emotional impact the work has on them. For example, if a painting purportedly by Rembrandt is expressive and emotionally moving in a certain way, then this is supposedly evidence that the work was created by Rembrandt himself, and not by one of his students. But the degree to which an artwork has an emotional impact differs wildly from person to person. So a connoisseur's assessment cannot be given credence.
>
> The reasoning in the argument is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that the argument
>
> (A) ignores the fact that anybody, not just a connoisseur, can give an assessment of the emotional impact of a painting
> (B) is based on the consideration of the nature of just one painter's works, even though the conclusion is about paintings in general
> (C) neglects the possibility that there may be widespread agreement among connoisseurs about emotional impact even when the public's assessment varies wildly
> (D) presumes, without giving justification, that a painting's emotional impact is irrelevant to the determination of that painting's authenticity
> (E) presumes, without offering evidence, that Rembrandt was better at conveying emotions in painting than were other painters

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** A connoisseur's assessment of a painting's authenticity cannot be given credence.

**Evidence:** The emotional impact of art differs wildly from person to person, while connoisseurs say that emotional impact guides their assessments.

**Evaluate:** The premise ranges over people generally. The conclusion concerns trained connoisseurs. Perhaps connoisseurs share criteria and agree with one another even when the broader public reacts differently. Variation across everyone does not establish unreliable judgments within the expert group.

**Goal:** Find the gap between public variation and agreement among connoisseurs.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Not an Objection.** Anyone may report an emotional response. The question is whether trained connoisseurs' responses can inform authenticity; public access to the same kind of response does not show they cannot.

**(B) Bad Description.** Rembrandt is introduced as an example of the claimed method, not as evidence that only his work has variable impact. The central premise is about artworks' impact across people.

**(C) Correct.** Even if people generally react very differently to art, connoisseurs might agree widely about the impact relevant to attribution. If so, public variation would not justify dismissing the connoisseurs' assessments.

**(D) Not Assumed.** The speaker does not say emotional impact is irrelevant to authenticity. The complaint is that responses vary, which allegedly makes the proposed method unreliable. Those are different claims.

**(E) Out of Scope.** The example contrasts a Rembrandt with a student's work, but the argument never compares Rembrandt's emotional skill with other painters'. Its reasoning turns on variation among observers.

## PT114-S1-Q6 | Flaw | E

**Modelled on:** PT134-S3-Q6 (alternative explanation), PT155-S1-Q20 (rebuttal overreach), PT120-S3-Q8 (one aspect versus whole claim).

> Every year, new reports appear concerning the health risks posed by certain substances, such as coffee and sugar. One year an article claimed that coffee is dangerous to one's health. The next year, another article argued that coffee has some benefits for one's health. From these contradictory opinions, we see that experts are useless for guiding one's decisions about one's health.
>
> Which one of the following most accurately describes a flaw in the argument above?
>
> (A) The argument takes for granted that coffee is dangerous to one's health.
> (B) The argument presumes, without providing warrant, that one always wants expert guidance in making decisions about one's health.
> (C) The argument fails to consider the nature of expert opinion in areas other than health.
> (D) The argument presumes, without providing justification, that because expert opinion is trustworthy in one case, it must therefore be trustworthy in all cases.
> (E) The argument fails to consider that coffee may be harmful to one's health in some respects and beneficial in others.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Experts are useless for guiding health decisions.

**Evidence:** One article called coffee dangerous, while another said coffee has some health benefits.

**Evaluate:** Are the articles actually incompatible? A substance can harm health in one respect and help in another. Both experts could be right and useful: their advice may identify different effects that a decision maker should weigh. Even a genuine disagreement about coffee would be thin support for declaring all experts useless.

**Goal:** Find the reconciliation of coffee's harms and benefits that dissolves the supposed contradiction.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Opposite.** The speaker presents one article as saying coffee is dangerous and another as saying it has benefits. He does not adopt the first article's conclusion as a premise.

**(B) No Impact.** The argument says expert guidance is useless, whether or not people always want such guidance. A desire for advice is not the logical bridge at issue.

**(C) Out of Scope.** The conclusion is about health decisions, so expert opinion in other areas cannot repair or expose the coffee-based inference.

**(D) Opposite.** The author is skeptical of experts. He does not infer that all expert opinions are trustworthy because one is.

**(E) Correct.** Coffee could be harmful in some respects and beneficial in others. The articles would then report different true facts, not contradictory advice. The argument's starting point for dismissing experts would fail.

## PT114-S1-Q8 | Flaw | E

**Modelled on:** PT154-S1-Q12 (conflicting behavior ad hominem), PT155-S1-Q20 (source versus merits), PT150-S2-Q4 (plan objection).

> Pundit: The only airline providing service for our town announced that because the service is unprofitable it will discontinue this service next year. Town officials have urged the community to use the airline's service more frequently so that the airline will change its decision. There is no reason to comply with their recommendation, however, for just last week these same officials drove to an out-of-town conference instead of flying.
>
> The pundit's reasoning is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that it presumes, without providing justification, that
>
> (A) increasing the number of tickets sold without increasing ticket prices will be sufficient to make continued air service economically feasible
> (B) suspending service and losing money by continuing service are the airline's only options
> (C) the town officials paid for their trip with taxpayers' money rather than their own money
> (D) ground transportation is usually no less expensive than airplane transportation
> (E) if the town officials did not follow their own advice then that advice is not worth following

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Town residents have no reason to follow officials' recommendation to use the airline more often.

**Evidence:** Those officials drove to a conference last week instead of flying.

**Evaluate:** An official can give sound advice while failing to follow it on one occasion. The conference trip might even have had special constraints, but we need not explain it: their conduct does not tell us whether more ticket sales would help keep service. Test the recommendation on its merits.

**Goal:** Look for the assumption that a speaker's failure to practice advice makes the advice worthless.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Not Assumed.** More ticket sales might or might not make the route viable. The pundit does not assume that they will; the town officials' recommendation suggests that possibility. The pundit's flaw is dismissing their advice by pointing to their behavior.

**(B) No Impact.** The airline may have options besides suspension and continued losses. The pundit's inference about whether residents should follow the recommendation does not rely on a two-option view of the airline's choices.

**(C) No Impact.** Who paid for the officials' trip does not determine whether encouraging residents to fly is sound advice.

**(D) No Impact.** A general comparison of ground and air transport prices would not establish that the officials' advice should be ignored. The route's profitability is the relevant issue.

**(E) Correct.** The pundit uses the officials' failure to follow their own recommendation as the reason to reject the recommendation. That is a version of the ad hominem mistake: hypocrisy can be real while the advice remains sound.

## PT114-S2-Q25 | Flaw | B

**Modelled on:** PT147-S1-Q6 (unrepresentative sample), PT148-S1-Q2 (proportion versus total), PT120-S3-Q8 (base rate).

> In our solar system only one of the nine planets—Earth— qualifies as fit to sustain life. Nonetheless, using this ratio, and considering the astonishingly large number of planetary systems in the universe, we must conclude that the number of planets fit to sustain some form of life is extremely large.
>
> The argument is questionable because it presumes which one of the following without providing justification?
>
> (A) If a planet is Earthlike, then life will arise on it.
> (B) Our solar system is similar to many other planetary systems in the universe.
> (C) The conditions necessary for life to begin are well understood.
> (D) Life similar to Earth's could evolve under conditions very different from those on Earth.
> (E) Most other planetary systems in the universe have nine planets.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The universe contains an extremely large number of planets fit to sustain some form of life.

**Evidence:** One of the nine planets in our solar system qualifies, and the universe has astonishingly many planetary systems.

**Evaluate:** Multiplying one solar system's proportion by many systems assumes that our system resembles a great many others in the relevant respect. If most other systems contain no life-sustaining planets, a huge number of systems would not validate the projected ratio. The argument offers one observed system as a sample of the universe.

**Goal:** Find the assumption that our solar system is sufficiently typical of other planetary systems.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** A planet being fit to sustain life does not guarantee that life arises on it. The conclusion concerns *fitness*, not the actual origin of life.

**(B) Correct.** The argument extrapolates from our system's one-in-nine ratio to numerous other systems. That inference needs many other systems to be similar to ours in the prevalence of life-sustaining planets. Without that, the local ratio tells us little about the universe-wide count.

**(C) No Impact.** We need not understand how life begins to assess the premise's stipulated judgment that Earth is fit to sustain it. The projection across systems is the gap.

**(D) Bad Description.** The argument does not require Earthlike life to evolve elsewhere, or require life to evolve at all. It counts planets capable of sustaining *some form* of life.

**(E) Too Strong.** Other systems need not have exactly nine planets. They need a comparable frequency of life-sustaining planets for the extrapolation to work. Nine is the denominator in our system, not a required size for every system.

## PT114-S4-Q8 | Flaw | D

**Modelled on:** PT154-S1-Q12 (hypocrisy and merits), PT155-S1-Q20 (evaluate the rebuttal), PT113-S2-Q3 (prefix test).

> Politician: My opponent says our zoning laws too strongly promote suburban single-family dwellings and should be changed to encourage other forms of housing like apartment buildings. Yet he lives in a house in the country. His lifestyle contradicts his own argument, which should therefore not be taken seriously.
>
> The politician’s reasoning is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that
>
> (A) its characterization of the opponent’s lifestyle reveals the politician’s own prejudice against constructing apartment buildings
> (B) it neglects the fact that apartment buildings can be built in the suburbs just as easily as in the center of the city
> (C) it fails to mention the politician’s own living situation
> (D) its discussion of the opponent’s lifestyle is irrelevant to the merits of the opponent’s argument
> (E) it ignores the possibility that the opponent may have previously lived in an apartment building

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The opponent's argument for changing zoning laws should not be taken seriously.

**Evidence:** Although the opponent advocates encouraging housing such as apartment buildings, he himself lives in a house in the country.

**Evaluate:** Living in a country house does not settle whether current zoning rules overpromote suburban single-family homes. The opponent could even prefer his own home while correctly arguing that the city should make room for other residents' choices. The politician needs to answer the housing-policy claim, not the opponent's lifestyle.

**Goal:** Identify the ad hominem move: using a person's conduct to dismiss the merits of that person's argument.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Unsupported.** The politician may be prejudiced against apartments, but describing the opponent's residence does not establish that motive. The flaw we can verify is reliance on an irrelevant personal fact.

**(B) Bad Description.** Where apartment buildings can be built does not address whether the opponent's home undermines his argument. His proposal can be assessed without this suburban-versus-center comparison.

**(C) Irrelevant Comparison.** Knowing the politician's own living arrangement would not make the opponent's argument more or less sound.

**(D) Correct.** Whether the opponent lives in a country house has no bearing on whether zoning laws should encourage more varied housing. The criticism attacks the advocate's lifestyle while leaving the policy reasoning unanswered.

**(E) No Impact.** The opponent need never have lived in an apartment for his present claim about zoning to be sound. A past apartment residence would not be the missing defense of his argument.

## PT115-S2-Q14 | Flaw | C

**Modelled on:** PT154-S2-Q12 (false choice), PT150-S2-Q4 (plan/recommendation), PT120-S3-Q8 (one factor not only factor).

> Activist: Although the environmental bill before the legislature is popular with voters, it will have mainly negative economic consequences if it is passed, especially when we try to lure new businesses to our country. Great leaders have the courage to look beyond popularity to what is sound policy; legislators ought to do the same by not voting for this bill.
>
> The activist's argumentation is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that it
>
> (A) presumes, without providing justification, that most of the legislators are great leaders
> (B) presumes, without providing justification, that a bill is less likely to pass if it is deemed to have negative economic consequences
> (C) fails to consider whether there are noneconomic reasons for supporting the bill that outweigh the reason given for rejecting it
> (D) fails to specify whether legislators usually consider economic consequences when a bill is before the legislature
> (E) takes for granted that if a bill is popular, it will not be sound economically

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Legislators should vote against the environmental bill.

**Evidence:** It is popular but would have mainly negative economic consequences, especially for attracting new businesses. Great leaders look beyond popularity to sound policy.

**Evaluate:** The bill's economic costs matter, and popularity by itself would not justify it. But a vote requires weighing *all* important consequences. Environmental or health benefits might outweigh those costs. The speaker gives a reason against the bill and treats it as decisive without examining potential reasons for it.

**Goal:** Find the omitted noneconomic benefits that could outweigh the acknowledged economic downside.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Not Assumed.** Legislators need not be great leaders for the activist to urge them to follow the conduct attributed to great leaders.

**(B) Out of Scope.** The conclusion recommends how legislators ought to vote, not a prediction of whether the bill is likely to pass.

**(C) Correct.** The economic drawback may be outweighed by noneconomic reasons to support the bill. Without comparing the costs with potential benefits, the activist cannot conclude that sound policy requires voting no.

**(D) No Impact.** Whether legislators *usually* consider economics does not determine how they ought to vote on this bill. The gap lies in the omitted reasons for passing it.

**(E) Opposite.** The activist explicitly grants that the bill is popular while arguing that it is economically harmful. He does not infer harm from popularity.

## PT115-S4-Q21 | Flaw | B

**Modelled on:** PT147-S1-Q6 (sampling), PT120-S3-Q8 (base-rate alternative), PT148-S1-Q2 (relative versus absolute).

> In a small town, every complaint filed about a plumber's work was filed against Moore, who has a relatively small business. So there is good evidence that Moore is a poor plumber who cannot be counted on to do a good job.
>
> The argument is questionable because it fails to consider
>
> (A) the interests of Moore's customers who have not filed complaints
> (B) that there may be few if any other plumbers working in Moore's town
> (C) whether Moore's business is too small to warrant the kind of generalization drawn
> (D) the number of complaints filed in Moore's town about tradespeople other than plumbers
> (E) whether each person who was dissatisfied with Moore's work filed a complaint against Moore

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Moore is a poor plumber who cannot be counted on to do good work.

**Evidence:** In one small town, every filed complaint about a plumber's work was against Moore, despite Moore's relatively small business.

**Evaluate:** That sounds damning until we ask for the comparison group. Are there many other plumbers receiving no complaints, or does Moore do nearly all the plumbing in town? If Moore is the only plumber, every plumbing complaint would necessarily name Moore, even if his work is generally good. The distribution of complaints alone does not establish his reliability.

**Goal:** Look for the missing base rate: how many other plumbers could have received complaints?

### Choice Notes

**(A) No Impact.** Customers without complaints might matter if we knew how many there were, but their *interests* do not explain why Moore received all filed complaints.

**(B) Correct.** There may be few or no competing plumbers. If Moore performs almost all the plumbing work, all complaints naming him would be unsurprising and would not establish that his work is unusually poor. The argument needs a meaningful comparison.

**(C) No Impact.** A small business can still produce enough jobs to support a judgment. The specific gap is the number of other plumbers and jobs available for comparison, not a bare question about whether Moore's business is "too small."

**(D) Out of Scope.** Complaints about electricians, carpenters, or other tradespeople would not tell us whether Moore performs plumbing work well.

**(E) Opposite.** If more dissatisfied Moore customers failed to complain, that would make the known complaints undercount dissatisfaction and could strengthen the criticism. It does not expose why *all filed plumbing complaints* point to Moore.

## PT116-S2-Q5 | Flaw | D

**Modelled on:** PT113-S2-Q3 (necessary versus sufficient), PT151-S4-Q23 (conditional translation), PT154-S1-Q9 (contrapositive).

> Shipping Coordinator: If we send your shipment by air express, it will arrive tomorrow morning. If we send your shipment via ground carrier, it will arrive either tomorrow or the next day. Ground carrier is less expensive than air express, so which do you prefer?
> Customer: If I don't choose air express, then I will not receive my shipment tomorrow, so I clearly have no choice but to spend the extra money and have it sent via air express.
>
> The customer's response can best be explained on the assumption that she has misinterpreted the shipping coordinator to mean which one of the following?
>
> (A) Ground carrier is as reliable a shipping method as air express.
> (B) If the shipment is sent by air express, it will arrive tomorrow.
> (C) Ground carrier is not more expensive than air express.
> (D) Unless the shipment is sent by air express, it will not arrive tomorrow.
> (E) The greater the shipping cost, the faster the shipment will arrive.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion (customer):** She must pay for air express to receive the shipment tomorrow.

**Evidence she attributes to the coordinator:** Without air express, it will *not* arrive tomorrow.

**Evaluate:** The coordinator guaranteed tomorrow morning for air express. For ground carrier, the coordinator offered *tomorrow or the next day*. The customer turns "ground may take until the next day" into "ground cannot arrive tomorrow." A guarantee of tomorrow by one method does not mean that method is required for tomorrow delivery.

**Goal:** Find the conditional the customer falsely hears: no air express means no tomorrow delivery.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** Reliability of ground shipping does not explain the customer's belief that it cannot arrive tomorrow.

**(B) Not an Objection.** The coordinator did say air express would arrive tomorrow. That guarantee alone does not rule out ground carrier also arriving tomorrow.

**(C) Not an Objection.** Ground carrier is less expensive. That fact explains why the question is a choice, but not why the customer thinks ground cannot meet tomorrow's deadline.

**(D) Correct.** "Unless air express, not tomorrow" means that air express is necessary for tomorrow delivery. That is exactly the customer's mistaken interpretation. The coordinator said ground might arrive tomorrow, so the supposed necessity is false.

**(E) Not Assumed.** The customer need not believe every more expensive shipment is faster. Her mistake concerns the specific alternatives and the certainty of next-day arrival.

## PT117-S3-Q4 | Flaw | A

**Modelled on:** PT113-S2-Q3 (necessary condition mistaken), PT150-S2-Q4 (comparison across contexts), PT134-S3-Q6 (alternative route).

> Maria: Thomas Edison was one of the most productive inventors of his time, perhaps of all time. His contributions significantly shaped the development of modern lighting and communication systems. Yet he had only a few months of formal schooling. Therefore, you do not need a formal education to make crucial contributions to technological advancement.Frank: That is definitely not true anymore. Since Edison’s day there have been many new developments in technology; to make crucial contributions today you need much more extensive technical knowledge than was needed then.
>
> Frank's reasoning in his response to Maria is most vulnerable to criticism on the grounds that it
>
> (A) fails to address the possibility that technical knowledge may be acquired without formal education
> (B) does not consider whether there have been improvements in formal education since Edison's day
> (C) relies on using the term "crucial" differently from the way Maria used it
> (D) presumes that no other inventor of Edison's time could have been as productive as Edison
> (E) fails to criticize or question any of Maria's statements about Edison

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion (Frank):** Today, one needs a formal education to make crucial technological contributions.

**Evidence:** Since Edison's day, technology has developed so much that today's contributors need far more extensive technical knowledge than he did.

**Evaluate:** Frank may be right about the need for *knowledge*. But he has switched from knowledge to *formal education*. Someone could acquire deep technical knowledge through independent study, apprenticeship, work, or other routes. Edison having little schooling does not prove that today's knowledge demands can be met without schooling, but Frank's reply likewise fails to establish that schooling is required.

**Goal:** Find the distinction between needing technical knowledge and needing formal education.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Technical knowledge could be acquired without formal schooling. If so, even a much higher knowledge requirement today would not refute Maria's claim that formal education is unnecessary. Frank treats one route to knowledge as the only route.

**(B) Irrelevant Comparison.** Improvements in schools since Edison's day might make formal education more useful, but Frank's conclusion says it is *necessary*. School quality does not close the gap between knowledge and credentials.

**(C) Not Equivocation.** Both speakers use "crucial contributions" to mean consequential technological work. Their dispute is over what preparation that work requires today.

**(D) Out of Scope.** Frank does not need to assume Edison was uniquely productive. His response concerns today's knowledge requirements, regardless of other inventors in Edison's era.

**(E) Not an Objection.** Frank can grant everything Maria says about Edison and still argue that today's conditions differ. His flaw lies in the inference about formal education, not in failing to challenge the historical example.

## PT117-S4-Q9 | Flaw | D

**Modelled on:** PT134-S3-Q6 (alternate explanation), PT150-S2-Q4 (comparison difference), PT120-S3-Q8 (one factor versus only factor).

> Manager: When Sullivan was passed over for promotion, people said that the deciding factor was his being much older than the competition. But this is clearly not the case. Several recent promotions have been given to people older than Sullivan.
>
> The manager’s argument is most vulnerable to criticism because it fails to consider the possibility that
>
> (A) Sullivan was well qualified for the promotion
> (B) age is only one of a number of factors that kept Sullivan from being promoted
> (C) people often associate age with experience and good judgment
> (D) the people older than Sullivan who were promoted had no younger competitors
> (E) Sullivan’s employer tries to keep deliberations involving promotion decisions confidential

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Sullivan's older age was not the deciding factor in his failed promotion.

**Evidence:** Several recently promoted people were older than Sullivan.

**Evaluate:** The relevant question is whether age disadvantaged Sullivan *relative to his competitors for that job*. Other promotion contests may have had different age comparisons. An older person could win a contest against still older applicants while Sullivan loses against younger ones. The general record of older promotees does not settle what happened in Sullivan's particular contest.

**Goal:** Look for a difference in the other promotion contests: the older promotees may not have faced younger competitors.

### Choice Notes

**(A) No Impact.** Being well qualified does not show that age was the deciding factor; Sullivan might have lost to an even better qualified candidate.

**(B) Wrong Role.** That age was *one* factor keeping Sullivan from promotion is consistent with it being the deciding factor. The number of other factors does not rebut the manager's argument.

**(C) No Impact.** Some people associate age with good judgment, but that does not show how age worked in Sullivan's promotion decision or in the cited promotions.

**(D) Correct.** If the older promotees had no younger competitors, their promotions do not show that an older applicant would beat a younger one. Sullivan could still have lost because he was much older than the competition in his particular contest.

**(E) No Impact.** Confidential deliberations might make the true reason hard to know, but secrecy does not explain why the manager's evidence fails to rule age out.

## PT118-S3-Q6 | Flaw | C

**Modelled on:** PT154-S1-Q12 (source attack), PT155-S1-Q20 (rebuttal overreach), PT114-S1-Q8 (advice versus advisor).

> Editorial: The premier’s economic advisor assures her that with the elimination of wasteful spending the goal of reducing taxes while not significantly decreasing government services can be met. But the premier should not listen to this advisor, who in his youth was convicted of embezzlement. Surely his economic advice is as untrustworthy as he is himself, and so the premier should discard any hope of reducing taxes without a significant decrease in government services.
>
> Which one of the following is a questionable argumentative strategy employed in the editorial’s argument?
>
> (A) rejecting a proposal on the grounds that a particular implementation of the proposal is likely to fail
> (B) trying to win support for a proposal by playing on people’s fears of what could happen otherwise
> (C) criticizing the source of a claim rather than examining the claim itself
> (D) taking lack of evidence for a claim as evidence undermining that claim
> (E) presupposing what it sets out to establish

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The premier should reject the advisor's recommendation and abandon hope of cutting taxes without a significant service reduction.

**Evidence:** The advisor was convicted of embezzlement in his youth, so his economic advice is allegedly untrustworthy.

**Evaluate:** A past conviction may justify scrutinizing the person, but it says little about the specific economic prediction. Wasteful spending might be large enough to cut without reducing services. The editorial offers no calculation or policy evidence to test that claim. Rejecting the source leaves the substance unresolved.

**Goal:** Identify the attack on the claimant in place of an examination of the claim.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** The editorial does not analyze a particular implementation of spending cuts and predict its failure. It dismisses the proposal by invoking the advisor's past.

**(B) Opposite.** The editorial is not promoting an alternative proposal by stoking fear of its rejection. It is arguing against the advisor's proposal.

**(C) Correct.** The source's youthful embezzlement conviction is used to discredit the present economic claim. Whether wasteful spending can be eliminated sufficiently requires evidence about budgets and services, which the editorial does not examine.

**(D) Wrong Flaw.** The writer does not merely say there is no evidence for the advisor's claim. He offers a personal reason to distrust the advisor, then treats that as grounds to reject the claim.

**(E) Wrong Flaw.** The premise about a conviction does not restate the conclusion about tax cuts and services. The reasoning is flawed, but it is not circular.

## PT119-S2-Q24 | Flaw | D

**Modelled on:** PT155-S1-Q20 (absence of proof versus proof), PT112-S4-Q8 (one piece of evidence does not settle history), PT148-S1-Q2 (word-level distinction).

> It is now clear that the ancient Egyptians were the first society to produce alcoholic beverages. It had been thought that the ancient Babylonians were the first; they had mastered the process of fermentation for making wine as early as 1500 B.C. However, archaeologists have discovered an Egyptian cup dating from 2000 B.C. whose sides depict what appears to be an Egyptian brewery, and whose chemical residue reveals that it contained a form of alcoholic beer.
>
> The reasoning above is most vulnerable to criticism on which one of the following grounds?
>
> (A) It makes a generalization about Egyptian society based on a sample so small that it is likely to be unrepresentative.
> (B) It uses the term "alcoholic beverage" in a different sense in the premises than in the conclusion.
> (C) It presumes, without providing justification, that because one society developed a technology before another, the development in the latter was dependent on the development in the former.
> (D) It ignores the possibility that the first known instance of a kind is not the first instance of that kind.
> (E) It provides no evidence for the claim that the Babylonians produced wine as early as 1500 B.C.

The existing database explanation already has Conclusion, Evidence, Evaluate, and Goal steps. The notes below complete that explanation; the existing steps should be retained.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Bad Description.** The author is not using one Egyptian cup to generalize about what most Egyptians did. The cup establishes an instance of alcoholic beer in Egypt by 2000 B.C.; the issue is whether that instance was the world's first.

**(B) Wrong Flaw.** "Alcoholic beverage" includes wine and beer in both the evidence and the conclusion. The argument compares dates, not two meanings of the term.

**(C) Bad Description.** The author does not say Babylonian wine production depended on Egyptian beer production. Being earlier than one known Babylonian example is offered as evidence for priority, not for transmission of technology.

**(D) Correct.** The cup is older than the cited Babylonian evidence, so Egypt has the earlier *known* example of the two. Another society might have made alcohol even earlier without leaving evidence we have found. The oldest discovered instance need not be the first historical instance.

**(E) Not an Objection.** The author supplies the 1500 B.C. Babylonian wine date as background. Even if that date were well documented, the Egyptian cup would not establish that no society produced alcohol before Egypt.

## PT119-S3-Q2 | Flaw | C

**Modelled on:** PT154-S2-Q12 (answer the current proposal), PT155-S1-Q20 (weak rebuttal does not settle opposing claim), PT150-S2-Q4 (conditions can differ across times).

> Benson: In order to maintain the quality of life in our city, we need to restrict growth. That is why I support the new zoning regulations.Willett: I had heard such arguments ten years ago, and again five years ago. Each time the city council was justified in deciding not to restrict growth. Since there is nothing new in this idea of restricting growth, I oppose the regulations.
>
> Which one of the following most accurately describes a way in which Willett's reasoning is questionable?
>
> (A) It presumes that growth is necessarily good without offering support for that position.
> (B) It is based on attacking Benson personally rather than responding to Benson's reasoning.
> (C) It ignores the possibility that new reasons for restricting growth have arisen in the past five years.
> (D) It fails to take into account the variety of factors that contribute to the quality of life in a city.
> (E) It overlooks the possibility that the city council of ten years ago was poorly qualified to decide on zoning regulations.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion (Willett):** He opposes the new zoning regulations restricting growth.

**Evidence:** Similar arguments for restricting growth arose ten and five years ago, and the council was justified in rejecting them then. Willett says there is nothing new in the idea.

**Evaluate:** A familiar *proposal* can have new reasons behind it. Growth might have changed the city's housing, congestion, or infrastructure since the last decision. Even if those earlier councils were right, their decisions do not automatically settle today's case. Willett must assess the present evidence for Benson's quality-of-life claim.

**Goal:** Find the possibility that new circumstances or reasons arose after the earlier decisions.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Too Strong.** Willett need not think all growth is good. He can oppose this particular restriction while accepting that some growth has costs. His stated reason is the earlier rejections.

**(B) Bad Description.** Willett does not attack Benson's character. He attacks the age of the proposal and cites past council decisions.

**(C) Correct.** The idea of restricting growth can be old while reasons supporting it are new. Changes in the past five years could make today's regulation warranted even though earlier councils rightly rejected earlier restrictions.

**(D) Bad Description.** Many factors may affect quality of life, but Willett does not attempt a full quality-of-life analysis. The flaw in *his* argument is using the oldness of the proposal as if it ruled out new justification.

**(E) Not an Objection.** The council ten years ago may have been well qualified and correct in its own circumstances. That still would not decide whether present reasons support new zoning rules.
