## PT102-S4-Q5 | Principle-Strengthen | A

> If the ivory trade continues, experts believe, the elephant will soon become extinct in Africa, because poaching is rife in many areas. A total ban on ivory trading would probably prevent the extinction. However, the country of Zimbabwe—which has virtually eliminated poaching within its borders and which relies on income from carefully culling elephant herds that threaten to become too big—objects to such a ban. Zimbabwe holds that the problem lies not with the ivory trade but with the conservation policies of other countries.
>
> Which one of the following principles forms a logical basis for Zimbabwe’s objection to a ban?
>
> (A) International measures to correct a problem should not adversely affect countries that are not responsible for the problem.
> (B) Freedom of trade is not a right but a consequence of agreements among nations.
> (C) Respecting a country’s sovereignty is more important than preventing the extinction of a species.
> (D) Prohibitions affecting several countries should be enforced by a supranational agency.
> (E) Effective conservation cannot be achieved without eliminating poaching.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Zimbabwe objects to a total ban on ivory trading.

**Evidence:** Zimbabwe has virtually eliminated poaching inside its borders and earns income by culling herds that grow too big. Zimbabwe says the problem is not the ivory trade but other countries' conservation policies.

**Evaluate:** Zimbabwe's case is that it isn't the cause of the problem, yet a worldwide ban would cost it the culling income. So the principle has to say that a fix aimed at a problem shouldn't hurt the countries that didn't cause it.

**Goal:** If a country is not responsible for a problem, international measures to correct that problem should not harm it.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Zimbabwe isn't responsible for the poaching, and a ban would take away its culling income. This principle says a measure like that shouldn't adversely affect a country in Zimbabwe's position, which is exactly the basis for its objection.

**(B) Out of Scope.** Where the right to trade comes from never comes up. If anything, saying trade rests on agreements among nations makes a ban agreed by nations easier to defend.

**(C) Not Described.** Zimbabwe never argues from sovereignty. Its point is that it isn't the source of the problem, and it doesn't say saving the elephant matters less than anything.

**(D) Out of Scope.** Who should enforce a ban is beside the point. Zimbabwe objects to having the ban at all.

**(E) Doesn't Support.** This is the most-picked wrong answer because it sounds like Zimbabwe's record. But it says only that eliminating poaching is necessary for conservation. It gives no reason why a country that already did so should escape a ban, so it doesn't support the objection.

## PT105-S1-Q24 | Principle-Strengthen | D

> Editorial: Our society has a vested interest in maintaining a political system in which candidates are free to adhere to their principles. Yet campaigning for elected office is extremely costly, and because only the wealthiest individuals are able to finance their own political campaigns, most candidates must seek funding from private sources. In so doing, the candidates are almost invariably obliged to compromise their principles. Thus, government itself should assume the cost of candidates’ campaigns.
>
> Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the conclusion as it is drawn in the argument?
>
> (A) Candidates should not run for elected office if doing so would compel the candidates to compromise their principles.
> (B) Candidates wealthy enough to finance their own political campaigns should not be permitted to raise additional funds from private sources.
> (C) Voters should not support a candidate if that candidate is known to have accepted funding from private sources.
> (D) The government should finance a given activity if doing so will further a vested interest of society.
> (E) Private funding for political campaigns should be encouraged only if it redresses an imbalance among candidates’ financial means.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The government should pay for candidates' campaigns.

**Evidence:** Society has a vested interest in a system where candidates are free to keep their principles. Campaigns are costly, so most candidates seek private money, and that almost always forces them to compromise.

**Evaluate:** Government funding would keep candidates free of private money, which serves society's vested interest. The principle needs to link "serves a vested interest of society" to "government should pay for it."

**Goal:** If government financing of an activity furthers a vested interest of society, the government should finance it.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Conclusion.** This tells candidates not to run. The argument concludes that the government should pay, and this principle says nothing about who pays.

**(B) Wrong Conclusion.** This limits fundraising by wealthy candidates. The conclusion is about government paying for campaigns, and wealthy candidates are not the problem the editorial describes.

**(C) Wrong Conclusion.** This is advice to voters. It does nothing to show that government should cover the costs.

**(D) Correct.** Paying for campaigns frees candidates from private funders, which protects the system society has a vested interest in. This principle turns that fact into the conclusion that the government should pay.

**(E) Wrong Conclusion.** This sets a condition for encouraging private funding. The argument wants to replace private funding with public money, and this principle never mentions the government.

## PT105-S4-Q7 | Principle-Strengthen | E

> Willett: Lopez and Simmons, a married couple, have both been offered jobs at Evritech Corporation. Because Evritech has a rule against hiring more than one member of the same family, Lopez and Simmons have decided to reveal their marriage to Evritech. Their decision is foolish, however, since it will mean that one of them will have a job offer withdrawn. After all, they could easily keep their marriage secret initially and, if they want, later claim to have married after they were hired: Evritech has no policy of terminating one of two employees who marry each other.
>
> Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to support the reasoning in Willett’s argument?
>
> (A) Corporations that have rules against hiring more than one member of the same family should also prohibit their employees from marrying one another.
> (B) Corporations should adopt a policy of refusing to hire more than one member of the same family if that policy promotes overall fairness in its hiring practices.
> (C) Job applicants are no more entitled to withhold information that is requested on application forms than they are entitled to lie on such application forms.
> (D) Job candidates should refuse to accept positions in corporations whose personnel policies they cannot adhere to.
> (E) Job candidates have no obligation to reveal to a prospective employer personal information such as marital status, regardless of the employer’s policies.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Lopez and Simmons are foolish to reveal their marriage to Evritech.

**Evidence:** Revealing it means one of them loses the job offer. They could keep the marriage secret and later say they married after being hired, and Evritech has no policy of firing one of two employees who marry.

**Evaluate:** Willett's advice only works if the couple is allowed to stay quiet about their marriage. If they had a duty to tell Evritech, keeping it secret wouldn't be a sensible option. The principle has to remove that duty.

**Goal:** Job candidates have no obligation to tell an employer about their marriage.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Conclusion.** This is about what Evritech's policy should be. Willett is advising the couple about what to do under the policy that exists.

**(B) Wrong Conclusion.** This says when a company should adopt the one-family rule. It doesn't help show that the couple is foolish to disclose.

**(C) Opposite.** If withholding requested information is as bad as lying, the couple shouldn't keep quiet. That undercuts Willett's advice.

**(D) Wrong Conclusion.** This says candidates who can't follow a company's policies should turn down the job. It points toward the couple declining, not toward keeping the marriage secret.

**(E) Correct.** If candidates don't have to reveal marital status whatever the employer's policies, then the couple can keep quiet without doing anything wrong. That makes revealing the marriage, and losing an offer, the foolish choice.

## PT110-S2-Q20 | Principle-Strengthen | D

> Professor Chan: The literature department’s undergraduate courses should cover only true literary works, and not such frivolous material as advertisements. Professor Wigmore: Advertisements might or might not be true literary works but they do have a powerfully detrimental effect on society— largely because people cannot discern their real messages. The literature department’s courses give students the critical skills to analyze and understand texts. Therefore, it is the literature department’s responsibility to include the study of advertisements in its undergraduate courses.
>
> Which one of the following principles most strongly supports Professor Wigmore’s argument?
>
> (A) Advertisements ought to be framed in such a way that their real messages are immediately clear.
> (B) Any text that is subtly constructed and capable of affecting people’s thought and action ought to be considered a form of literature.
> (C) All undergraduate students ought to take at least one course that focuses on the development of critical skills.
> (D) The literature department’s courses ought to enable students to analyze and understand any text that could have a harmful effect on society.
> (E) Any professor teaching an undergraduate course in the literature department ought to be free to choose the material to be covered in that course.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The literature department should include the study of advertisements in its undergraduate courses.

**Evidence:** Advertisements have a harmful effect on society, largely because people can't discern their real messages. The department's courses give students the critical skills to analyze and understand texts.

**Evaluate:** Wigmore leaves aside whether ads are literature. The reasoning is about harm and skills: ads do damage that the department's skills could counter. The principle should link "a text that can harm society" to "the department's courses should equip students to analyze it."

**Goal:** If a text could harm society, the literature department's courses should help students analyze and understand it.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Conclusion.** This tells advertisers how to write ads. Wigmore's conclusion is about what the literature department should teach.

**(B) Wrong Approach.** Wigmore explicitly sets aside whether ads are literature ("might or might not be"). This answer argues that ads are literature, which is the issue Chan raised, and it doesn't connect to the harm Wigmore relies on.

**(C) Too Broad.** This says students should take a critical-skills course. It doesn't say that course has to cover advertisements, or that the literature department should teach it.

**(D) Correct.** Ads are texts that harm society, and this principle says the department's courses ought to equip students to analyze any such text. That leads straight to including ads in those courses.

**(E) Opposite.** If each professor is free to choose the material, the department doesn't have a responsibility to include ads. That cuts against Wigmore.

## PT111-S1-Q2 | Principle-Strengthen | D

> Lecturer: Given our current state of knowledge and technology, we can say that the generalization that the entropy of a closed system cannot decrease for any spontaneous process has not been falsified by any of our tests of that generalization. So we conclude it to be true universally. Yet, it must be admitted that this generalization has not been conclusively verified, in the sense that it has not been tested in every corner of the universe, under every feasible condition. Nevertheless, this generalization is correctly regarded as a scientific law; indeed, it is referred to as the Second Law of Thermodynamics.
>
> Which one of the following principles, if valid, most justifies the lecturer’s classification of the generalization described above?
>
> (A) Whatever is a scientific law has not been falsified.
> (B) If a generalization is confirmed only under a few circumstances, it should not be considered a scientific law.
> (C) Whatever is true universally will eventually be confirmed to the extent current science allows.
> (D) If a generalization is confirmed to the extent current science allows, then it is considered a scientific law.
> (E) Whatever is regarded as a scientific law will eventually be conclusively verified.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The generalization is correctly regarded as a scientific law.

**Evidence:** No test available with current knowledge and technology has falsified it. It hasn't been conclusively verified everywhere and under every condition.

**Evaluate:** The lecturer admits the generalization isn't conclusively verified, so the principle can't demand that. What the generalization does have is confirmation as far as current science can go. The principle must say that this much is enough to count as a scientific law.

**Goal:** If a generalization is confirmed to the extent current science allows, it is a scientific law.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Reversed.** This says scientific laws haven't been falsified. That is "law, so not falsified." The lecturer needs the other direction, "not falsified, so law." This is the most-picked trap, so check which side of the rule the conclusion sits on.

**(B) Doesn't Apply.** This tells us when something should not be a law. The generalization has been tested under far more than a few circumstances, so the principle doesn't apply, and it can't establish that something is a law anyway.

**(C) Wrong Conclusion.** This is about what will eventually be confirmed. It doesn't say anything about what counts as a scientific law.

**(D) Correct.** The generalization is confirmed as far as current science allows. Plug that into this principle and you get that it's considered a scientific law, which is the lecturer's classification.

**(E) Wrong Direction.** This starts from "regarded as a law" and predicts future verification. It doesn't tell us when to regard something as a law.

## PT112-S1-Q15 | Principle-Strengthen | D

> In one study, engineering students who prepared for an exam by using toothpicks and string did no worse than similar students who prepared by using an expensive computer with sophisticated graphics. In another study, military personnel who trained on a costly high-tech simulator performed no better on a practical exam than did similar personnel who trained using an inexpensive cardboard model. So one should not always purchase technologically advanced educational tools.
>
> Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the reasoning above?
>
> (A) One should use different educational tools to teach engineering to civilians than are used to train military personnel.
> (B) High-tech solutions to modern problems are ineffective unless implemented by knowledgeable personnel.
> (C) Spending large sums of money on educational tools is at least as justified for nonmilitary training as it is for military training.
> (D) One should not invest in expensive teaching aids unless there are no other tools that are less expensive and at least as effective.
> (E) One should always provide students with a variety of educational materials so that each student can find the materials that best suit that student's learning style.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** One should not always buy technologically advanced educational tools.

**Evidence:** In two studies, students using cheap tools (toothpicks and string, a cardboard model) did just as well as students using expensive high-tech tools.

**Evaluate:** The studies show cheap tools can be as effective as expensive ones. The principle has to say that when a cheaper tool works just as well, you shouldn't pay for the expensive one.

**Goal:** Don't buy expensive tools when cheaper tools are at least as effective.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** The two studies aren't meant to contrast civilians with soldiers. They show the same lesson in both settings: cheap tools did as well.

**(B) Out of Scope.** Nothing in the studies turns on whether the personnel were knowledgeable. The comparison is cheap tool against expensive tool.

**(C) Irrelevant Comparison.** Whether spending is as justified for civilian as for military training doesn't matter. The argument says the expensive tool isn't always worth buying, in either setting.

**(D) Correct.** In both studies a cheaper tool was at least as effective. This principle says not to buy the expensive aid in that situation, so you shouldn't always buy high-tech tools.

**(E) Out of Scope.** Learning styles and variety never come up. The studies compare cost and results, nothing else.

## PT112-S4-Q23 | Principle-Strengthen | C

> When investigators discovered that the director of a local charity had repeatedly overstated the number of people his charity had helped, the director accepted responsibility for the deception. However, the investigators claimed that journalists were as much to blame as the director was for inflating the charity's reputation, since they had naïvely accepted what the director told them, and simply reported as fact the numbers he gave them.
>
> Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the investigators' claim?
>
> (A) Anyone who works for a charitable organization is obliged to be completely honest about the activities of that organization.
> (B) Anyone who knowingly aids a liar by trying to conceal the truth from others is also a liar.
> (C) Anyone who presents as factual a story that turns out to be untrue without first attempting to verify that story is no less responsible for the consequences of that story than anyone else is.
> (D) Anyone who lies in order to advance his or her own career is more deserving of blame than someone who lies in order to promote a good cause.
> (E) Anyone who accepts responsibility for a wrongful act that he or she committed is less deserving of blame than someone who tries to conceal his or her own wrongdoing.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Journalists are as much to blame as the director for inflating the charity's reputation.

**Evidence:** The journalists naively accepted the director's numbers and reported them as fact.

**Evaluate:** The journalists didn't lie. They passed along false numbers without checking. The principle has to make reporting an unverified story as fact just as blameworthy as the original deception.

**Goal:** Anyone who reports an unverified story as fact is as responsible for its effects as anyone else.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Doesn't Apply.** This covers people who work for a charity. The journalists don't, so it can't spread the blame to them.

**(B) Doesn't Apply.** This covers people who knowingly help a liar hide the truth. The journalists were naive, not knowing accomplices.

**(C) Correct.** The journalists reported the numbers as fact without trying to verify them, and the story turned out to be false. This principle says they are no less responsible than anyone else, including the director. That is the investigators' claim.

**(D) Out of Scope.** This ranks liars by motive. The journalists didn't lie, and the claim is about how blame is shared, not about motives.

**(E) Wrong Conclusion.** This would make the director less blameworthy for accepting responsibility. It says nothing about the journalists being equally to blame.

## PT114-S1-Q3 | Principle-Strengthen | E

> An anthropologist hypothesized that a certain medicinal powder contained a significant amount of the deadly toxin T. When the test she performed for the presence of toxin T was negative, the anthropologist did not report the results. A chemist who nevertheless learned about the test results charged the anthropologist with fraud. The anthropologist, however, countered that those results were invalid because the powder had inadvertently been tested in an acidic solution.
>
> In the absence of the anthropologist's reply, which one of the following principles, if established, would most support the chemist's charge?
>
> (A) Reporting results for an experiment that was not conducted and reporting a false result for an actual experiment are both instances of scientific fraud.
> (B) Scientists can commit fraud and yet report some disconfirmations of their hypotheses.
> (C) Scientists can neglect to report some disconfirmations of their hypotheses and yet be innocent of fraud.
> (D) Scientists commit fraud whenever they report as valid any test result they know to be invalid.
> (E) Scientists who neglect to report any experiment that could be interpreted as disconfirming their hypotheses have thereby committed fraud.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The chemist charges the anthropologist with fraud.

**Evidence:** The anthropologist's hypothesis was that the powder contained toxin T. The test came back negative, and the anthropologist didn't report it.

**Evaluate:** Set the reply aside, as the stem says. All we have is a scientist who failed to report a result that went against the hypothesis. The principle has to make that failure fraud.

**Goal:** Scientists who fail to report a disconfirming result commit fraud.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Doesn't Apply.** Both cases here involve reporting something. The anthropologist reported nothing, so neither kind of fraud fits.

**(B) Doesn't Support.** This says fraud is possible even when some disconfirmations are reported. It doesn't say that failing to report one is fraud.

**(C) Opposite.** This says a scientist can leave out disconfirmations and still be innocent. That helps the anthropologist, not the chemist.

**(D) Doesn't Apply.** This is about reporting a result one knows is invalid. The anthropologist reported nothing.

**(E) Correct.** The anthropologist left out a test that could be read as disconfirming the hypothesis. This principle says that is fraud, so it supports the charge.

## PT114-S2-Q4 | Principle-Strengthen | E

> A favorable biography of a politician omits certain incriminating facts about the politician that were available to anyone when the book was written. The book's author claims that, because he was unaware of these facts when he wrote the book, he is not accountable for the fact that readers were misled by this omission. In a biographer, however, ignorance of this kind cannot be used to evade blame for misleading readers.
>
> Which one of the following principles, if established, does most to justify the position advanced by the passage?
>
> (A) An author of a biography should not be blamed for whether the book is perceived to be favorable or unfavorable by readers of the biography.
> (B) An author of a biography should be blamed for readers' misperceptions only when facts are omitted deliberately in order to mislead the readers.
> (C) An author of a biography should not be blamed for omitting facts if those facts would have supported the author's view.
> (D) An author of a biography should be blamed for misleading readers only if facts are omitted to which the author alone had access when the biography was written.
> (E) An author of a biography should be blamed for readers' misperceptions caused by omitting facts that were widely available when the biography was written.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The biographer's ignorance can't be used to escape blame for misleading readers.

**Evidence:** The omitted facts were available to anyone when the book was written. The author says not knowing them means not being accountable.

**Evaluate:** The passage's position is that ignorance is no excuse when the facts were there for anyone to find. The principle should blame a biographer for misleading omissions of facts that were widely available.

**Goal:** A biographer should be blamed for misperceptions caused by omitting facts that were widely available.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** Whether readers see the book as favorable isn't the issue. The issue is blame for misleading readers through omission.

**(B) Opposite.** This blames biographers only when omissions are deliberate. The author says the omission was not deliberate, so this lets the author off.

**(C) Doesn't Apply.** These facts were incriminating, so they wouldn't have supported a favorable view. And this principle excuses authors rather than blaming them.

**(D) Opposite.** This blames authors only for facts no one else had. The facts here were available to anyone, so the principle clears the author.

**(E) Correct.** The facts were widely available, and leaving them out misled readers. This principle says the biographer should be blamed for that, whatever the author knew.

## PT118-S4-Q3 | Principle-Strengthen | E

> Nylon industry spokesperson: Even though cotton and nylon are used for similar purposes, some people have the mistaken notion that cotton is natural but nylon is not. However, nylon's main components come from petroleum and from the nitrogen in the atmosphere. Clearly the atmosphere is natural. **Conclusion:** It's a mistake to think nylon is not natural.

**Evidence:** Nylon's main components come from petroleum and from nitrogen in the atmosphere. The atmosphere is natural, and petroleum comes from ancient plants, a natural source.

**Evaluate:** The spokesperson traces each main component back to a natural origin and concludes nylon is natural. The principle has to say that natural origins for the main components make a substance natural.

**Goal:** If a substance's main components have natural origins, the substance is natural.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** The spokesperson never discusses what nylon is used for. The argument is about where its components come from.

**(D) Wrong Conclusion.** This is about one substance being more natural than another. The spokesperson isn't saying nylon is more natural than cotton, only that it is natural.

## PT119-S2-Q5 | Principle-Strengthen | A

> Futurist: Artists in the next century will be supported largely by private patrons. Because these patrons will almost invariably be supporters of the social order—whatever it happens to be at the time—art in the next century will rarely express social and political doctrines that are perceived to be subversive of that social order.
>
> Which one of the following principles, if valid, provides the most support for the futurist's inference?
>
> (A) Art patrons tend not to support artists whose art expresses social and political views that are in opposition to their own.
> (B) Art patrons tend to be more interested in formal artistic problems than in the social and political issues of their time.
> (C) Artists are as prone to attack the contemporary social and political order in their work as they are to defend it.
> (D) Artists tend to become more critical of contemporary social and political arrangements after they are freed of their dependency on private patrons.
> (E) Art patrons tend to oppose all social change except that initiated by artists.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Art in the next century will rarely express views seen as subversive of the social order.

**Evidence:** Artists will be supported mostly by private patrons, and these patrons will almost always support the social order.

**Evaluate:** The futurist jumps from what patrons believe to what art will say. The gap is that patrons won't fund art that opposes their views. The principle needs to supply that.

**Goal:** Patrons tend not to support artists whose work opposes the patrons' views.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Patrons will support the social order, and this principle says they won't back art that opposes their views. So subversive art won't get funding, and it will rarely be made.

**(B) Doesn't Support.** If patrons don't care about social issues, their politics wouldn't shape what they fund. That weakens the futurist's link.

**(C) Doesn't Support.** If artists attack and defend the order equally, we'd expect plenty of subversive art unless something stops it. This gives no reason patrons would stop it.

**(D) Wrong Situation.** This is about artists freed from patrons. In the futurist's next century, artists depend on patrons.

**(E) Opposite.** If patrons don't oppose change started by artists, they might fund subversive art. That cuts against the conclusion.

## PT119-S3-Q14 | Principle-Strengthen | B

> Political scientist: The economies of a number of European countries are currently in severe difficulty. Germany is the only neighboring country that has the resources to resuscitate these economies. Therefore, Germany should begin aiding these economically troubled countries.
>
> Which one of the following principles most helps to justify the political scientist's reasoning?
>
> (A) Any nation that alone has an obligation to economically resuscitate neighboring countries ought to be the only nation to provide any economic aid.
> (B) Any nation that alone has the capacity to economically resuscitate neighboring countries should exercise that capacity.
> (C) Any nation that can afford to give economic aid to just a few other nations ought to aid just those few.
> (D) Only nations that alone have the capacity to economically resuscitate neighboring countries should exercise that capacity.
> (E) Only nations that can afford to give economic aid to just a few other nations ought to aid just those few.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Germany should begin aiding the economically troubled European countries.

**Evidence:** Several neighboring countries' economies are in severe difficulty, and Germany is the only neighbor with the resources to revive them.

**Evaluate:** The political scientist moves from "only Germany can help" to "Germany should help." The principle has to put "alone has the capacity" on the left and "should use it" on the right.

**Goal:** A nation that alone can revive its neighbors' economies should do so.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Doesn't Apply.** This starts with a nation that alone has an obligation, which is what the argument needs to establish. It also only limits who else should give aid.

**(B) Correct.** Germany alone has the capacity to revive its neighbors' economies. This principle says a nation in that position should use it, so Germany should begin aiding them.

**(C) Doesn't Apply.** Nothing says Germany can afford to help only a few nations. This principle doesn't match the facts.

**(D) Reversed.** "Only nations that alone have the capacity should exercise it" makes having that capacity necessary for acting, not sufficient. It tells us who may act, not that Germany must. Picked by 9 percent, so watch for "only" turning a trigger into a requirement.

**(E) Reversed.** Like D, "only" makes the condition necessary. It also depends on Germany being able to aid just a few nations, which we aren't told.

## PT120-S1-Q3 | Principle-Strengthen | C

> When a lawmaker spoke out against a research grant awarded to a professor in a university’s psychology department as a foolish expenditure of public money, other professors in that department drafted a letter protesting the lawmaker’s interference in a field in which he was not trained. The chair of the psychology department, while privately endorsing the project, refused to sign the protest letter on the ground that she had previously written a letter applauding the same legislator when he publicized a senseless expenditure by the country’s military.
>
> Which one of the following principles, if established, provides the strongest justification for the department chair’s refusal, on the ground she gives, to sign the protest letter?
>
> (A) A person should not publicly criticize the actions of a lawmaker in different cases without giving careful consideration to the circumstances of each particular case.
> (B) The chair of an academic department has an obligation to ensure that public funds allocated to support projects within that department are spent wisely.
> (C) A person who has praised a lawmaker for playing a watchdog role in one case should not criticize the lawmaker for attempting to play a watchdog role in another case that involves the person’s professional interests.
> (D) Since academic institutions accept public funds but do not pay taxes, a representative of an academic institution should not publicly pass judgment on the actions of government officials.
> (E) Academic institutions have the same responsibility as military institutions have to spend public money wisely.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The chair refuses to sign the letter protesting the lawmaker's interference.

**Evidence:** The chair had earlier written a letter praising the same lawmaker for publicizing a senseless military expenditure.

**Evaluate:** The chair's ground is consistency: having praised the lawmaker for acting as a watchdog in one case, the chair can't protest the same watchdog role now. The principle has to say that, even when the new case touches the chair's own field.

**Goal:** Someone who praised a lawmaker for being a watchdog in one case shouldn't criticize the lawmaker for doing the same in a case involving that person's own professional interests.

### Choice Notes
