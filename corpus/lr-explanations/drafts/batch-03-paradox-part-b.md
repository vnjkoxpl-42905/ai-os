## PT115-S4-Q9 | Paradox | E

**Modelled on:** PT152-S4-Q23 (count the right thing), PT148-S3-Q11 (difference-maker).

> In each of the last ten years, there have been few complaints to law enforcement agencies of telemarketing fraud. Yet, in the last ten years, fraudulent telemarketing schemes have become a more and more serious problem, bilking victims out of millions of dollars annually.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, most helps to resolve the apparent conflict described above?
>
> (A) Telemarketers convicted of fraud tend to be punished more severely than other people convicted of nonviolent crimes.
> (B) Most complaints of telemarketing fraud that authorities investigate involve the use of credit cards, which makes it extremely difficult for law enforcement agencies to track down the perpetrators.
> (C) Some fraudulent telemarketers have concocted phony investment schemes which make it unlikely that victims will detect the fraud until a year or two after the fact, by which time their swindlers are long gone.
> (D) Fraudulent telemarketers typically base their operations outside the legal jurisdiction inhabited by the people they solicit, so they must be prosecuted in higher courts.
> (E) The majority of those who have been defrauded by telemarketers have been inclined not to seek any legal remedy, out of embarrassment at being duped.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** For ten years there have been few complaints of telemarketing fraud.

**How can it be that:** Telemarketing fraud has grown into a serious problem costing victims millions every year?

**Evaluate:** Complaints are not the same as fraud. They count only victims who report. If most victims never complain, fraud can grow large while complaints stay low. Ask why a victim would stay quiet.

**Goal:** Find a reason victims of fraud do not complain.

### Choice Notes

**(A) No Impact.** Punishment after conviction does not affect how many victims complain in the first place.

**(B) No Impact.** This is about how hard complaints are to investigate. It says nothing about why there are so few complaints.

**(C) Too Weak.** Some schemes are discovered a year or two late. Over ten years, those victims would still eventually complain, and the choice covers only "some" schemes.

**(D) No Impact.** Which court prosecutes the fraud does not explain why victims do not report it.

**(E) Correct.** Most victims are too embarrassed to seek any remedy, so they never complain. Fraud grows while complaints stay low.

## PT115-S4-Q12 | Paradox | C

**Modelled on:** PT126-S4-Q5 (the riskier group gets more care), PT120-S4-Q1 (use the background fact).

> The higher a dam, the more exposed it is to forces that can cause it to collapse. Of the world's hundreds of arch dams, more than half are over 100 meters high. Yet all six of the arch dam collapses that have occurred during the past 40 years have occurred in arch dams under 100 meters high.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, most helps to resolve the apparent paradox?
>
> (A) The higher a dam must be, the greater the likelihood that it will be built as an arch dam.
> (B) Arch dams are generally more complicated and more costly to construct than other types of dams of comparable size.
> (C) The larger the structure, the more careful the attention it received in design and construction.
> (D) The basic engineering principles behind the design and construction of arch dams have been known for more than 100 years.
> (E) Arch dams under 100 meters high are less subject to collapse than are dams of other types also under 100 meters high.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** Higher dams face more forces that can cause collapse, and more than half of arch dams are over 100 meters.

**How can it be that:** All six arch dam collapses in 40 years were in dams under 100 meters?

**Evaluate:** Higher dams face more risk, yet the lower ones are failing. Something must offset the risk for tall dams. If the people building tall dams know they are risky and take extra care, those dams could be safer overall.

**Goal:** Find something that makes taller dams safer in practice despite the greater forces.

### Choice Notes

**(A) No Impact.** Taller dams being built as arch dams explains why many arch dams are tall. It does not explain why the short ones are the ones collapsing.

**(B) Irrelevant Comparison.** Comparing arch dams with other types does not separate tall arch dams from short ones.

**(C) Correct.** Larger structures get more careful design and construction. That extra care can outweigh the greater forces, so tall arch dams hold while shorter ones fail.

**(D) No Distinction.** Long-known engineering principles apply to arch dams of every height.

**(E) Irrelevant Comparison.** Comparing short arch dams with short dams of other types does not explain why short arch dams fail more than tall ones.

## PT116-S2-Q3 | Paradox | E

**Modelled on:** PT148-S4-Q3 (what are they really valuing?), PT154-S1-Q6 (name the difference).

> Cognitive psychologist: In a recent survey, citizens of Country F were asked to state which one of the following two scenarios they would prefer: (1) Country F is the world economic leader, with a gross national product (GNP) of $100 billion, and Country G is second, with a GNP of $90 billion; or (2) Country G is the economic leader, with a GNP of $120 billion, and Country F is second, with a GNP of $110 billion. Despite the fact that, under scenario 2, Country F would have a higher GNP than under scenario 1, the majority of respondents stated that they preferred scenario 1.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, would most help to explain the survey results described by the cognitive psychologist?
>
> (A) Most citizens of Country F believe their country has a higher economic growth rate than Country G.
> (B) Most citizens of Country F want their country to have a GNP higher than $120 billion.
> (C) Most citizens of Country F believe that their personal welfare is unconnected to GNP.
> (D) Most citizens of Country F believe GNP is a poor measure of a nation's economic health.
> (E) Most citizens of Country F want their country to be more economically powerful than Country G.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** Under scenario 2, Country F would have a higher GNP ($110 billion vs $100 billion).

**How can it be that:** Most citizens of F prefer scenario 1?

**Evaluate:** What does scenario 1 offer that scenario 2 does not? In scenario 1, F is the world leader, ahead of G. In scenario 2, G is ahead. If people care more about being ahead of G than about their own total, scenario 1 wins.

**Goal:** Find a preference for ranking above G over a higher absolute GNP.

### Choice Notes

**(A) No Impact.** A belief about growth rates does not decide between the two fixed scenarios.

**(B) Opposite.** Wanting a GNP above $120 billion would favor scenario 2, which comes closer.

**(C) Too Weak.** If GNP does not affect personal welfare, people might be indifferent between the scenarios. It does not explain a preference for the lower GNP.

**(D) Too Weak.** If GNP is a poor measure, people again have little reason to care either way. That gives no reason to prefer scenario 1.

**(E) Correct.** Citizens want F to be more economically powerful than G. Only scenario 1 delivers that, so they choose it even though F's GNP is lower.

## PT116-S2-Q25 | Paradox | C

**Modelled on:** PT148-S4-Q3 (what can they actually tell apart?), PT141-S4-Q15 (behavior with two effects).

> Burying beetles do whatever they can to minimize the size of their competitors' broods without adversely affecting their own. This is why they routinely destroy each other's eggs when two or more beetles inhabit the same breeding location. Yet, after the eggs hatch, the adults treat all of the larvae equally, sharing in the care of the entire population.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, most helps to explain burying beetles' apparently contradictory behavior?
>
> (A) Burying beetles whose eggs hatch before their competitors' are more likely to have large broods than are burying beetles whose eggs hatch later.
> (B) The cooperation among adult burying beetles ensures that the greatest possible number of larvae survive.
> (C) Burying beetles are unable to discriminate between their own larvae and the larvae of other burying beetles.
> (D) Many of the natural enemies of burying beetles can be repelled only if burying beetles cooperate in defending the breeding site.
> (E) Most breeding sites for burying beetles can accommodate only a limited number of larvae.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** Burying beetles try to shrink competitors' broods without hurting their own, so they destroy each other's eggs.

**How can it be that:** After hatching, adults care for all the larvae equally, competitors' included?

**Evaluate:** The behavior splits at hatching: rivals' *eggs* get destroyed, rivals' *larvae* get cared for. So look for a difference between eggs and larvae. Destroying eggs fits the goal. Caring for rivals' larvae does not, unless the beetles cannot tell whose larvae are whose. If they cannot, harming any larva risks harming their own, and their own goal is to protect their own brood.

**Goal:** Find a reason beetles cannot target competitors' larvae.

### Choice Notes

**(A) No Impact.** Hatching order and brood size do not explain why adults care for every larva.

**(B) Half Scope.** Cooperation helps the most larvae survive, but the beetles are trying to *shrink* rivals' broods. This explains why caring for everyone produces many survivors, not why beetles who want fewer rival larvae would do it.

**(C) Correct.** If beetles cannot tell their own larvae from others', they cannot harm rivals' larvae without risking their own. Equal care follows from the same goal that drives egg destruction. (Eggs, presumably, they can identify by where they laid them.)

**(D) Half Scope.** Cooperating to defend the site explains joint defense. It does not explain why beetles that destroy rivals' eggs leave rivals' larvae alone and even feed them.

**(E) Deepens Paradox.** If space for larvae is limited, a beetle has even more reason to remove rivals' larvae, not care for them.

## PT117-S2-Q3 | Paradox | C

**Modelled on:** PT154-S1-Q6 (something that changed), PT107-S4-Q19 (then vs now).

> In the past, combining children of different ages in one classroom was usually a failure; it resulted in confused younger children, who were given inadequate attention and instruction, and bored older ones, who had to sit through previously learned lessons. Recently, however, the practice has been revived with excellent results. Mixed-age classrooms today are stimulating to older children and enable younger children to learn much more efficiently than in standard classrooms.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, most helps to resolve the apparent discrepancy in the passage?
>
> (A) On average, mixed-age classrooms today are somewhat larger in enrollment than were the ones of the past.
> (B) Mixed-age classrooms of the past were better equipped than are those of today.
> (C) Today's mixed-age classrooms, unlike those of the past, emphasize group projects that are engaging to students of different ages.
> (D) Today's mixed-age classrooms have students of a greater range of ages than did those of the past.
> (E) Few of the teachers who are reviving mixed-age classrooms today were students in mixed-age classrooms when they were young.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** Mixed-age classrooms used to fail: younger children were confused and older ones bored.

**How can it be that:** Today's mixed-age classrooms work very well?

**Evaluate:** Something must be different now. The old problems came from giving one lesson to children at different levels. What teaching approach would let both ages work well together?

**Goal:** Find a change in how mixed-age classrooms run today that fixes the old problems.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Deepens Paradox.** Larger classes mean less attention per child, which should make things worse, not better.

**(B) Deepens Paradox.** Past classrooms being better equipped makes their failure harder to explain and today's success more surprising.

**(C) Correct.** Today's classrooms use group projects that engage students of different ages. That replaces the one-level lesson that confused the young and bored the old.

**(D) Deepens Paradox.** A wider range of ages should make the old problems worse.

**(E) No Impact.** Whether teachers were once in mixed-age classrooms does not explain why the classrooms now work.

## PT117-S2-Q10 | Paradox | B

**Modelled on:** PT140-S2-Q7 (follow the shares), PT154-S1-Q6 (one concrete mechanism).

> Before their larvae hatch, each parental pair of Nicrophorus beetles buries the carcass of a small vertebrate nearby. For several days after the larvae hatch, both beetles feed their voracious larvae from the carcass, which is entirely consumed within a week. Since both parents help with feeding, larvae should benefit from both parents’ presence; however, removing one parent before the hatching results in larvae that grow both larger and heavier than they otherwise would be.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, best helps to explain why removing one parent resulted in larger, heavier larvae?
>
> (A) Two beetles can find and bury a larger carcass than can a single beetle.
> (B) Both parents use the carcass as their own food supply for as long as they stay with the larvae.
> (C) Beetle parents usually take turns feeding their larvae, so that there is always one provider available and one at rest.
> (D) After a week, the larvae are capable of finding other sources of food and feeding themselves.
> (E) Two parents can defend the carcass from attack by other insects better than a single parent can.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** Both parents feed the larvae, which should help them.

**How can it be that:** Removing one parent produces larger, heavier larvae?

**Evaluate:** The carcass is a fixed amount of food, all eaten within a week. If the parents eat from it too, each parent takes food away from the larvae. One fewer parent leaves more for the young.

**Goal:** Find a way the second parent reduces the larvae's food.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Opposite.** If two beetles bury a larger carcass, two parents would mean *more* food. That makes the result stranger.

**(B) Correct.** Parents eat from the carcass themselves. With one parent gone, more of the fixed food supply goes to the larvae, so they grow larger.

**(C) No Impact.** Taking turns describes how parents share work. It does not explain bigger larvae with only one parent.

**(D) Applies to Both.** Larvae finding food after a week would be true whether one or two parents are present.

**(E) Opposite.** Two parents defending the carcass better would favor two parents, which deepens the puzzle.

> Leatherbacks, the largest of the sea turtles, when subjected to the conditions of captivity, are susceptible to a wide variety of fatal diseases with which they would never come in contact if they lived in the wild. It is surprising, therefore, that the likelihood that a leatherback will reach its theoretical maximum life expectancy is about the same whether that animal is living in captivity or in the wild.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, most helps to resolve the apparent discrepancy?
>
> (A) Fewer diseases attack leatherbacks than attack other large aquatic reptiles.
> (B) The average life expectancy of sea turtles in general is longer than that of almost all other marine animals.
> (C) Most leatherbacks that perish in the wild are killed by predators.
> (D) Few zoologists have sufficient knowledge to establish an artificial environment that is conducive to the well-being of captive leatherbacks.
> (E) The size of a leatherback is an untrustworthy indicator of its age.

## PT117-S4-Q25 | Paradox | D

**Modelled on:** PT126-S4-Q5 (the treated group started worse), PT101-S2-Q18 (low baseline).

> A certain medication that is frequently prescribed to lower a patient’s cholesterol level is generally effective. A recent study of 1,000 subjects ranging widely in age indicates, however, that the cholesterol level of someone taking the medication is typically 12 to 15 percent higher than the average for that person’s age group.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, most helps to explain how both of the claims made above could be true?
>
> (A) A recently developed cholesterol-lowering medication is more effective than the medication described above.
> (B) Another medication is prescribed to treat high cholesterol just as often as the medication described above is.
> (C) In most cases, people with high cholesterol levels are not treated with drug therapy but are put on restrictive low-cholesterol diets.
> (D) The medication described above is usually prescribed only for people whose cholesterol level is at least 30 percent above the average for their age group.
> (E) Within the population as a whole, approximately the same number of people have relatively high cholesterol levels as have relatively low cholesterol levels.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** The medication generally lowers cholesterol.

**How can it be that:** People taking it typically have cholesterol 12 to 15 percent above the average for their age?

**Evaluate:** Who takes this drug? People with high cholesterol. If they start far above average, the drug can lower them a lot and still leave them above average. Use numbers: start 30 percent above, drop by more than half that gap, finish 12 to 15 percent above.

**Goal:** Find that users started well above average.

### Choice Notes

**(A) No Impact.** Another drug being better does not explain why this one's users are above average.

**(B) No Impact.** How often another drug is prescribed says nothing about this drug's users.

**(C) No Impact.** Most high-cholesterol patients being on diets does not explain the cholesterol of the people who do take the drug.

**(D) Correct.** The drug goes only to people at least 30 percent above average. Lowering them to 12 to 15 percent above is a real effect, and it leaves them above average.

**(E) No Impact.** The balance of high and low cholesterol in the population tells us nothing about the drug's users.

## PT118-S4-Q5 | Paradox | A

**Modelled on:** PT141-S4-Q15 (breadcrumbs), PT148-S3-Q11 (difference between the groups).

> Rats fed high doses of the artificial sweetener saccharin develop silicate crystals that are toxic to cells lining the bladder. When the cells regenerate, some are cancerous and form tumors. Unlike rats, mice fed high doses of saccharin do not get bladder cancer.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, does the most to resolve the apparent discrepancy in the information above?
>
> (A) Urine proteins that react with saccharin to form silicate crystals are found in rats but not in mice.
> (B) Cells in the bladder regenerate more quickly in mice than they do in rats.
> (C) High doses of saccharin are much more likely to produce silicate crystals than lower doses are.
> (D) The silicate crystals are toxic only to the cells lining the bladder and not to other bladder cells.
> (E) High doses of other artificial sweeteners have been shown to produce silicate crystals in mice but not in rats.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** In rats, high doses of saccharin create toxic silicate crystals, and regenerating cells can become cancerous.

**How can it be that:** Mice on the same high doses do not get bladder cancer?

**Evaluate:** The rat chain starts with crystals. If mice never form the crystals, the chain never starts. Look for a difference between the species at that first step.

**Goal:** Find a difference that stops mice from forming the crystals.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** The urine proteins that react with saccharin to form crystals exist in rats but not mice. No crystals, no damage, no cancer.

**(B) Unclear Impact.** Faster regeneration in mice could mean more chances for cancerous cells, not fewer. It does not explain the difference.

**(C) Applies to Both.** Both rats and mice got high doses, so dose cannot separate them.

**(D) No Distinction.** Where the crystals are toxic would apply to both species if both formed them.

**(E) Out of Scope.** Other sweeteners are not the issue. This also runs the wrong way, giving mice crystals and rats none.

## PT118-S4-Q15 | Paradox EXCEPT | A

**Modelled on:** PT107-S4-Q19 (EXCEPT: does it change what students pay or earn?), PT113-S2-Q17 (three-and-one split).

> The cost of a semester's tuition at a certain university is based on the number of courses in which a student enrolls that semester. Although the cost per course at that university has not risen in four years, many of its students who could afford the tuition when they first enrolled now claim they can no longer afford it.
>
> Each of the following, if true, helps to resolve the apparent discrepancy above except:
>
> (A) Faculty salaries at the university have risen slightly over the past four years.
> (B) The number of courses per semester for which full-time students are required to enroll is higher this year than any time in the past.
> (C) The cost of living in the vicinity of the university has risen over the last two years.
> (D) The university awards new students a large number of scholarships that are renewed each year for the students who maintain high grade averages.
> (E) The university has turned many of its part-time office jobs, for which students had generally been hired, into full-time, nonstudent positions.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** The cost per course has not risen in four years, and these students could once afford tuition.

**How can it be that:** Many of them now say they cannot afford it?

**Evaluate:** Affordability depends on what students pay and what they have. They could be paying more through more courses, or have less money because of higher living costs, lost scholarships or lost jobs. Each working answer changes one side.

**Goal:** Four choices raise what students owe or cut what they have. The answer will do neither.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Faculty salaries rising does not change what students pay; tuition per course is flat. It does not explain why students can no longer afford it.

**(B) Helps Explain.** Students must take more courses per semester, and tuition is charged per course, so their tuition rises.

**(C) Helps Explain.** Higher living costs leave less money for tuition.

**(D) Helps Explain.** Scholarships are renewed only for high grades. Students who lose them pay more.

**(E) Helps Explain.** Part-time jobs once held by students went to nonstudents. Students lost income.

## PT119-S2-Q6 | Paradox | B

**Modelled on:** PT120-S4-Q1 (the answer beats the others, not perfection), PT152-S4-Q23 (attack the evidence directly).

> University budget committee: Athletes experience fewer injuries on artificial-turf athletic fields than on natural-grass fields. Additionally, natural-grass fields are more expensive to maintain than fields made of artificial turf. Nevertheless, this committee recommends replacing the university's current artificial-turf field with a natural-grass field.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, most helps to resolve the apparent discrepancy in the committee's position?
>
> (A) The university's current artificial-turf athletic field has required extensive maintenance since its original installation.
> (B) Most injuries sustained on artificial-turf fields take longer to heal and require more expensive physical therapy than do injuries sustained on natural-grass fields.
> (C) It is difficult for spectators at athletic events to determine whether an athletic field is artificial turf or natural grass.
> (D) Maintaining artificial-turf fields involves the occasional replacement of damaged sections of turf, whereas natural-grass fields require daily watering and periodic fertilization.
> (E) Athletes who have spent most of their playing time on natural-grass fields generally prefer not to play on artificial-turf fields.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** Artificial turf causes fewer injuries and costs less to maintain than grass.

**How can it be that:** The committee recommends replacing the turf with grass?

**Evaluate:** The committee's own evidence favors turf on both counts. To resolve this, find something that outweighs those advantages. The injury figure counts injuries, not how bad they are. If turf injuries are worse and cost more, fewer injuries might still be the worse deal.

**Goal:** Find a reason grass is better despite fewer injuries and lower maintenance on turf.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Too Weak.** The current turf has needed a lot of maintenance, but we already know grass costs more to maintain. This does not outweigh that.

**(B) Correct.** Turf injuries are fewer, but each one heals more slowly and needs more expensive therapy. That cost could outweigh the cheaper maintenance and make turf the more expensive field overall, which is the kind of reason a budget committee acts on.

**(C) No Impact.** Whether spectators can tell the difference does not affect injuries or cost.

**(D) No Impact.** This lists different maintenance tasks, but we already know grass is more expensive to maintain overall.

**(E) Out of Scope.** This is a *budget* committee, so player preference is not the kind of reason that drives its recommendation. And nothing says this university's athletes have played mostly on grass, so we do not even know the preference applies to them.

## PT119-S3-Q1 | Paradox | A

> Sambar deer are physically incapable of digesting meat. Yet sambar deer have been reported feeding on box turtles after killing them.
>

## PT119-S4-Q1 | Paradox | C

**Modelled on:** PT154-S1-Q6 (one concrete mechanism), PT144-S3-Q5 (what does each fact require?).

> Considering that Mark Twain is Samuel Clemens, I have always found it amazing that very few people know that Samuel Clemens was a writer, since almost everybody knows that Mark Twain was one.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, would most effectively resolve the apparent paradox above?
>
> (A) Most people probably have not read anything by Samuel Clemens.
> (B) Everyone who knows that Samuel Clemens was a writer also knows that Mark Twain was one.
> (C) Most people do not know that Mark Twain is Samuel Clemens.
> (D) Many people believe apparently conflicting things about authors.
> (E) Some people know that "Mark Twain" is a pseudonym for Samuel Clemens.

**Given that:** Mark Twain is Samuel Clemens, and almost everyone knows Mark Twain was a writer.

**How can it be that:** Very few people know Samuel Clemens was a writer?

**Evaluate:** Knowing "Twain was a writer" gives you "Clemens was a writer" only if you also know they are the same person. If most people do not know that, there is nothing strange about the gap.

**Goal:** Find that most people do not know Twain and Clemens are the same person.

### Choice Notes

**(A) No Impact.** Not having read the books would apply under either name, so it cannot explain knowing one fact but not the other.

**(B) No Impact.** Everyone who knows about Clemens also knowing about Twain is consistent with the gap but does not explain why so few know about Clemens.

**(C) Correct.** Most people do not know Twain is Clemens, so they cannot carry "writer" from one name to the other.

**(D) Too Weak.** Holding conflicting beliefs about authors in general does not explain this specific gap.

**(E) Too Weak.** "Some people" knowing the pseudonym fits the puzzle but does not explain why most do not know Clemens was a writer.

## PT120-S3-Q11 | Paradox EXCEPT | C

**Modelled on:** PT120-S3-Q15 (EXCEPT: routes up front), PT152-S4-Q23 (count the right thing).

> Carrots are known to be one of the best sources of naturally occurring vitamin A. However, although farmers in Canada and the United States report increasing demand for carrots over the last decade, the number of people diagnosed with vitamin A deficiency in these countries has also increased in that time.
>
> Each of the following, if true of Canada and the United States over the last decade, helps to resolve the apparent discrepancy described above except:
>
> (A) The population has significantly increased in every age group.
> (B) The purchase of peeled and chopped carrots has become very popular, though carrots are known to lose their vitamins quickly once peeled.
> (C) Certain cuisines that have become popular use many more vegetable ingredients, including carrots, than most cuisines that were previously popular.
> (D) Carrot consumption has increased only among those demographic groups that have historically had low vitamin A deficiency rates.
> (E) Weather conditions have caused a decrease in the availability of carrots.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** Carrots are a top source of vitamin A, and demand for carrots has risen.

**How can it be that:** More people are being diagnosed with vitamin A deficiency?

**Evaluate:** Routes: more people overall (more cases even at the same rate); carrots delivering less vitamin A; the extra carrots going to people who were not deficient; or fewer carrots actually eaten. Any of these lets demand rise while deficiency cases rise too.

**Goal:** Four choices fit a route. The answer will make deficiency even harder to explain.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Helps Explain.** A larger population means more diagnosed cases in total, even if the rate does not rise.

**(B) Helps Explain.** Pre-cut carrots lose vitamins quickly, so people eating more carrots may get less vitamin A.

**(C) Correct.** Popular cuisines use more vegetables, including carrots. More carrots eaten should mean less deficiency, so this deepens the puzzle instead of resolving it.

**(D) Helps Explain.** The extra carrots went to groups that rarely had deficiency, so the groups at risk saw no benefit.

**(E) Helps Explain.** Demand rose but supply fell, so people may actually be eating fewer carrots.

## PT120-S4-Q18 | Paradox | B

> It has been a staple of drama to feature an innocent young protagonist, eager to make a mark on the world, who is stymied by an indifferent or hostile society. **How can it be that:** Warblers moved to a much larger island still bred cooperatively?

## PT122-S1-Q2 | Paradox | B

**Modelled on:** PT121-S4-Q19 (explain all the data), PT148-S3-Q11 (difference between groups).

> A study of plaque buildup on teeth used three randomly assigned groups of people who brushed their teeth twice a day for a year. People in Group 1 used the same toothbrush all year. People in Group 2 used the same toothbrush all year but sterilized it each month. People in Group 3 used a new, sterile toothbrush each month. At the end of the year, people in Groups 1 and 2 had the same amount of plaque buildup as each other, while people in Group 3 had less plaque buildup.
>
> Which one of the following, if true, most helps to explain the relative amounts of plaque buildup found in the three groups?
>
> (A) The buildup of plaque on teeth, which brushing twice a day helps to prevent, is accelerated by the growth of bacteria on toothbrushes that remained unsterilized for more than a month.
> (B) The stiffness of the bristles on new toothbrushes, which the mechanical action of brushing destroys after several months, inhibits the buildup of plaque.
> (C) The people who did the study measured the amount of plaque buildup by a new method not usually employed by dentists.
> (D) Before they joined the study, some of the people in Group 3 had been in the habit of brushing their teeth only once a day.
> (E) The people in Group 2 and Group 3 brushed their teeth as vigorously as did the people in Group 1.

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** Group 1 kept one brush; Group 2 kept one brush but sterilized it monthly; Group 3 got a new sterile brush monthly.

**How can it be that:** Groups 1 and 2 had the same plaque, while Group 3 had less?

**Evaluate:** Two results must fit. Sterilizing did nothing (1 = 2), so germs on the brush are not the cause. What is different about Group 3? Their brushes were *new*. Something about a new brush, other than being germ-free, must reduce plaque.

**Goal:** Find a property of new brushes, not sterility, that reduces plaque.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Opposite.** If bacteria on old brushes speed up plaque, sterilizing should have helped Group 2. But Groups 1 and 2 ended up the same.

**(B) Correct.** New bristles are stiff, and brushing wears that stiffness down over months. Only Group 3 always had stiff bristles. Groups 1 and 2 brushed with worn brushes, sterilized or not, so they matched.

**(C) Applies to Both.** A new way of measuring plaque applies to all three groups and cannot separate them.

**(D) Opposite.** If some of Group 3 used to brush less, they should have *more* plaque, not less.

**(E) No Impact.** Equal vigor rules out one difference but does not explain the result.

## PT122-S4-Q25 | Paradox EXCEPT | B

> A study conducted over a 6-month period analyzed daily attendance and average length of visit at the local art museum. The results showed that when the museum was not featuring a special exhibition, attendance tended to be lower but patrons spent an average of 45 minutes longer in the museum than when it was featuring a special exhibition.
>
> Each of the following, if true, could help to explain the differing average lengths of visits to the museum except:
>
