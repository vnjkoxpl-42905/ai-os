  [step 1 id=d9b0a365-ab76-41ff-b451-3a338496b16a] Evidence (for instance)
Logicians don't need to be logical when they discuss logic, just like physicians don't have to be healthy to discuss health.
  [step 2 id=fc848127-25d4-473d-8483-fd17e81006ed] Evaluate
The author is trying to support the idea that sometimes it's okay if you don't practice what you preach. The evidence is an analogy, in which the author is assuming in both cases that it's okay if you don't practice what you preach. It's okay for a doctor to tell her patient to stop smoking cigarettes, even though she herself hasn't been able to quit smoking. It's okay for a logician to be discussing logic, even though they are being illogical as they do so. Any time an argument is based on an Analogy, we should be asking ourselves whether the two things feel fair to compare. In this case, it does seem perfectly fine for a doctor to treat my health conditions, even if they don't live a healthy lifestyle. But it doesn't seem as fine for a logician to be illogical while discussing logic. How will I emerge with a better understanding of logic, if the logician teaches it to me in some illogically? To put it another way, what a physician does outside her clinic to her own body doesn't really affect how she treats people inside her clinic. The former is her life. The latter is her job. But what a logician does within a discussion of logic seems like it is his job, not his life. If he can't be logical while discussing logic, then he's messing up being a logician.

  [note A id=a97b10c9-de1c-47e4-be9b-a95dfa7610e7] This answer identifies the critical disanalogy that undermines the argument. The argument treats two cases as parallel exceptions to "practice what you preach," but they are fundamentally different. A logician who is illogical in discussions of logic is failing at the core competency of their profession. Logic is not just what logicians preach — it is the tool they must use to do their work. Being illogical when discussing logic means being incompetent at the very activity that defines the profession. By contrast, a physician's personal lifestyle is separate from their professional competence. A doctor who smokes, overeats, or never exercises can still diagnose diseases, perform surgery, and prescribe effective treatments. The physician's failure to practice what they preach involves personal behavior, not professional skill. The argument treats these as equivalent exceptions, but one involves professional incompetence while the other involves a personal shortcoming that does not affect job performance. This disanalogy destroys the argument's reasoning.
