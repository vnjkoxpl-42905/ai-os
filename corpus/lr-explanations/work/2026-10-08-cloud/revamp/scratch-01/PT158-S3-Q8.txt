  [step 2 id=db12ab11-af52-46c2-a683-ab4d5b3b4340] Evaluate
Whenever we're dong Flaw and we see conditional logic (only), we immediately suspect we're dealing with the #1 Famous Flaw, Necessary vs. Sufficient, in which an author provides a conditional logic rule but thinks about the rule in the style of an illegal negation or reversal. The word "only / only if" always signifies a necessary condition, so the rule the professor gave is this:
```
Give A → Conc supported w/ good evidence
```
    An illegal negation would sound like this: "My paper didn't get an A. Thus, its conclusions must not have been supported with good evidence." An illegal reversal would sound like this: "My conclusions were supported with good evidence. Thus, my paper will get an A." And that's just what the author did. He is thinking that having well supported conclusions is sufficient, like as long as he had well supported conclusion he should be getting an A. But the professor said that well supported conclusion were required, not that they were all you need. There might be other requirements too. For example the professor might say, "I go on dates only with men who are at least 5' 8" in height." Does that mean that the professor is going to be willing to go on a date with every single man whose 5'8" or taller? Of course not. She's saying that's a requirement, not a guarantee.

  [note C id=c6682ca3-987b-4577-91cf-6217ea53ce46] This answer precisely identifies the student's logical error. The professor said she would give A's "only to" papers with reliable statistical evidence. The word "only" establishes a necessary condition: you need reliable statistical evidence to get an A. It does not establish a sufficient condition: having reliable statistical evidence does not guarantee you an A. The student takes this necessary condition — one requirement among potentially many — and treats it as a sufficient condition, assuming that meeting it alone should produce an A. But the professor could have additional unstated criteria: quality of writing, depth of analysis, originality, methodological rigor, or any number of other standards. The student's paper met one necessary condition while potentially failing others, making the B grade perfectly consistent with the stated criteria. The student's complaint is built entirely on a logical confusion between what is required and what is enough.

  [step 2 id=23b8e89d-ea1f-487b-9ac4-5f64f3644e2c] Evaluate
Classic logic trap. "A's only to papers with X" does not mean "papers with X automatically get A's." It means X is required — a necessary condition — but there could be ten other conditions too. Maybe the paper had good stats but terrible writing, or missed an entire section, or was three pages short. The student heard "you need this to get an A" and interpreted it as "this is all you need for an A." That is like saying "this restaurant serves food only to paying customers" and concluding that paying automatically gets you food. No — you also need to order, wait your turn, and not be banned from the premises.
