## PT116-S3-Q6 | Agree/Disagree | E
explanation_id: a34f24a2-710f-4c34-89c5-6550494f64a2

### Argument Breakdown

**Antonia's Position:** The stock market is the best place to invest your money. It is often volatile, but it gives you the chance to make a large profit quickly.

**Maria's Response:** Maria agrees that the market offers large, quick profits, but says you're just as likely to take a huge loss. Savings bonds are better, because they provide "a constant, reliable income over many years."

**Evaluate:** Go claim by claim. Does Maria deny that the market can bring a large profit quickly? No, she says "I agree." Does she deny that the market is volatile? No. Her "just as likely to take a huge loss" says the same kind of thing, more sharply. So the two of them share the facts.

Where they split is the choice those facts lead to. Antonia looks at a risky market with a big, fast upside and calls it "the best place to invest." Maria looks at the same tradeoff and picks the steady income of savings bonds instead. Antonia never uses the words "reliable income," but calling a volatile investment the best one, because of its quick profits, is exactly a vote for the risky upside over safer options.

**Goal:** The answer should name the tradeoff: whether a steady, reliable return is better than a riskier shot at a large, quick profit. Maria says yes. Antonia's "best place to invest" says no.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Both Agree.** Antonia says both halves of this, and Maria says "I agree" to the profit half without disputing the volatility. There's no Disagree position anywhere in the conversation.

**(B) Out of Scope.** Neither speaker says bonds can produce a large return. Maria praises bonds for being "constant" and "reliable," which is a claim about steadiness, not size. With no one taking either side, there's nothing to disagree about.

**(C) Unsupported Agree Position.** Who says the stock market provides a constant, reliable income? Nobody. Maria gives that feature to savings bonds and warns that the market can bring "a huge loss." Antonia calls the market volatile. If anything, both would reject C, so it can't be the point where they split.

**(D) Unsupported Disagree Position.** This is the most tempting wrong answer, because Maria clearly holds it: bonds are safer. Now ask the second question. Can we support the claim that Antonia thinks bonds are not safer? Antonia admits the market "is often volatile." Antonia prefers the market for its profit potential, not because it's safe. Antonia can agree that bonds are safer and still call the market the better investment. That's the difference between D and E. D asks which investment is safer, and both speakers could accept the same answer. E asks whether safety is worth giving up the quick profit, and that's where they part.

**(E) Correct.** Check both sides. Maria: "it is better to invest in savings bonds, which provide a constant, reliable income," after conceding the market's quick profits. That's the Agree position. Antonia: the volatile market is "the best place to invest" because of the chance at a large, quick profit. That's the Disagree position. When two speakers accept the same facts, look for the disagreement in what they do with those facts, here which side of the tradeoff each one picks.

## PT123-S3-Q7 | Agree/Disagree | B

### Argument Breakdown

**Antonio's Position:** "One can live a life of moderation by never deviating from the middle course." The cost is that such a person loses the joy of spontaneity and misses the opportunities that come from occasionally taking great chances or going too far.

**Marla's Response:** Someone who never risks going too far "is actually failing to live a life of moderation." One must be moderate even in one's moderation.

**Evaluate:** Take Antonio's first claim and ask whether Marla argues the opposite. Antonio says never leaving the middle course is a way to live a life of moderation. Marla says the person who never risks going too far is failing to live a life of moderation. Those can't both be right. That's the explicit clash.

Now look at Antonio's second claim, the cost. Does Marla deny that the strictly moderate person loses spontaneity and opportunities? No. Her point actually fits with it: a truly moderate life leaves some room for going too far. So the two speakers aren't fighting about whether occasional risk is good. They're fighting about the word "moderation": Antonio thinks a moderate life means always staying in the middle, and Marla thinks it requires occasionally stepping out.

**Goal:** The answer should name the definition: whether a life of moderation requires never deviating from the middle course, or what living moderately actually takes.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Unsupported Disagree Position.** This is the most tempting wrong answer, because Antonio clearly values taking chances: never doing it means missing out on joy and opportunities. Now look for the other side. Does Marla say taking great chances is undesirable? No. Her claim is that never risking going too far is a failure of moderation, which, if anything, puts her on the same side as Antonio. Both speakers can hold A, so it isn't their disagreement.

**(B) Correct.** Antonio: never deviating from the middle course is enough for a life of moderation. Marla: never risking going too far is "failing to live a life of moderation." Each is a claim about what moderation requires, and they give opposite answers. The move to carry forward: when two speakers use the same key word and reach opposite verdicts, check whether they're defining it differently. The answer often names the definition rather than either speaker's verdict.

**(C) Out of Scope.** Neither speaker mentions any virtue besides moderation. Spontaneity is described as a source of joy, not as a virtue someone might embrace alongside moderation, and no one discusses whether that's possible.

**(D) No Disagree Position.** Neither speaker gives a frequency. Antonio praises those who are "occasionally willing" to take chances, and Marla says one must sometimes risk going too far. Both point to "occasionally," so there's no number or rate they dispute.

**(E) Both Agree.** Antonio counts the loss of spontaneity as a cost of the strict middle course, so he would favor some spontaneity. Marla's "moderate even in one's moderation" fits a little spontaneity too. Neither speaker takes the side that moderate spontaneity is undesirable.

### Argument Breakdown

**Opposing Point:** Recent speculations say a hardware store will be opening in the shopping plaza.

**Conclusion:** No hardware store will be opening in the shopping plaza.

**Evidence:** If somebody were going to open a store there, they would already have started publicizing it. There has been no such publicity.

**Evaluate:** "Contrary to recent speculations" tells you two things at once. Someone else has a view (the speculation), and the author is about to reject it. The rest of the sentence is the author's answer: no hardware store is coming.

Run the support test. Why should we believe no store is opening? Because an opening would already have been publicized, and there's been no publicity. That's a tight conditional argument:

Store opening → Publicity already
No publicity
So: no store opening

The two later sentences answer "why?" for the first sentence. Nothing answers "why?" for them, so they're the premises, and the first sentence is the conclusion.

**Goal:** Match the author's half of the first sentence: a hardware store will not be opening in the shopping plaza.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Opposing Position.** This is the "recent speculations" part of the first sentence, the view the author rejects. The conclusion is the author's answer to it, not the report of what other people guessed.

**(B) Correct.** This drops the "contrary to recent speculations" framing and keeps the author's claim: no store is coming. When the conclusion sentence opens with a rebuttal phrase like "Contrary to," "Despite what many think" or "Although some argue," the answer usually keeps only the author's side of it.

**(C) Premise.** This is the conditional premise. Ask "why should we believe the store isn't coming?" and this is half the answer. It's a reason for the conclusion, not the conclusion itself. It also says nothing about whether a store is actually opening.

**(D) Out of Scope.** The argument never discusses whether opening a store there would be wise. It's about whether a store will open, not whether one should.

**(E) Premise.** This is the "But there has been no such publicity" sentence. Paired with the conditional in C, it proves the conclusion, so it's the evidence, not the point the evidence supports.

Inspire curiosity → Broaden

## PT131-S3-Q9 | Main Conclusion | D

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** There is no good reason to suppose that promoting creativity is a proper goal of an employee training program.

**Evidence:** Many jobs require little or no creativity, and in those jobs creativity is more likely to be disruptive than innovative. Furthermore, even if creativity were in demand, there is no evidence that it can be taught.

**Evaluate:** The first sentence is an opinion about what a training program should aim for, which is the kind of claim that needs support. Does it get any? Run the test. Why should we believe there's no good reason to promote creativity in training? First, because many jobs don't need it and it can be disruptive there. Second ("Furthermore" adds another reason), because even where creativity is wanted, nobody has shown it can be taught. Both later sentences answer "why?" for the first, so the first sentence is the conclusion.

Notice how careful the manager's wording is. The conclusion doesn't say creativity is bad or that training should avoid it. It says there's "no good reason to suppose" it's a proper goal. The second premise is careful too: "no evidence that it can be taught," not "it can't be taught."

**Goal:** Match the first sentence, keeping its cautious strength: there is no good reason to think employee training programs should promote creativity.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Premise.** This restates the first reason: in jobs needing little creativity, using it is likely disruptive. It answers "why?" for the first sentence, so it's support, not the conclusion. (The stimulus says "more likely to be disruptive than innovative"; A softens that to "can be," but the role is the bigger problem.)

**(B) Too Strong Premise.** This is the most tempting wrong answer. It sounds like the "no evidence that it can be taught" premise, but it changes the claim. "There's no evidence it can be taught" leaves open that it can be, and we just haven't shown it. B says training programs "are not able to" teach it, which is a claim the manager never makes. Even an accurate version of this would still be a premise. It's one of the two reasons for the conclusion, not the conclusion itself.

**(C) Premise.** This is the first half of the first reason. Many jobs need little creativity, so promoting it in training may be pointless. It supports the conclusion and isn't supported by anything.

**(D) Correct.** "Should promote creativity" matches "promoting creativity is a proper goal," and "no good reason to suppose" is kept word for word. The move: once the support test points to the first sentence, check that the answer keeps its exact strength. Here, that means "no good reason to suppose," not "should not."

**(E) Distorted Premise.** The manager says "even if creativity were in demand," which imagines the case without granting it. E turns that into a flat claim that creativity "is in demand." And even fixed, this would be the second premise, not the conclusion.
