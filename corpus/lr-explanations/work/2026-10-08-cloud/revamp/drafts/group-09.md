## PT108-S3-Q6 | Paradox | C
explanation_id: 7aca97bc-3629-4277-bbd4-5597bdda2c4c

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** A power plant opened and began discharging its wastewater into the river.

**Why is it that:** Shortly afterward, anglers downstream from the plant caught fewer bass overall?

**Evaluate:** There's no real contradiction here. The stem only wants explanations for a drop, and four answers will supply one.

So what does "the overall number of bass caught" depend on? Two things: bass in the water, and people fishing for them. That gives two roads to a smaller catch.

1. Fewer bass downstream. They were eaten, moved away, died or lost their habitat.
2. Less fishing downstream. Fewer anglers show up, or they fish less.

Most people think only of road 1, because wastewater feels like it must be hurting the fish. Road 2 works just as well. If half the anglers stop coming, the catch drops even if the bass are doing fine.

**Goal:** Eliminate the four answers that put the drop on either road. The correct answer does neither. Expect it to push the other way: more bass, or more fishing, which should mean more bass caught, not fewer.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Explains.** More predators in the river means fewer bass left to catch. Road 1.

**(B) Explains.** If warm water drives the bass elsewhere, there are fewer bass downstream to catch. Road 1.

**(C) Correct.** Better access means more anglers fishing for bass downstream. More people fishing the same stretch should raise the overall catch, not lower it. This answer points the wrong way, so it can't explain a decrease. The transferable move: on an EXCEPT Paradox, ask of each answer, "If this is true, which way does the number move?" Four move it down. This one moves it up.

**(D) Explains.** This is the one to be careful with, because it talks about anglers instead of fish, and that can look off topic. But anglers are half of the catch. If noise makes the spot less popular, fewer people fish there, and fewer bass get caught even if the bass population is unchanged. Road 2.

**(E) Explains.** Destroying part of the bass's habitat leaves fewer bass in that stretch of river. Road 1.

## PT117-S3-Q2 | Paradox | C
explanation_id: 4e0d31b1-37db-42db-a39a-40e976a4732d

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** In captivity, leatherbacks are exposed to a wide variety of fatal diseases they would never meet in the wild.

**How can it be that:** A leatherback's chance of reaching its theoretical maximum life expectancy is about the same in captivity as in the wild?

**Evaluate:** Captivity has an extra danger the wild lacks. If that were the whole story, captive turtles should do worse. They don't. So something has to balance the scale.

What could? Either the wild has a danger of its own that captivity lacks, or captivity has some benefit that offsets the diseases. Picture two piles of risk: captivity's pile has the diseases in it, so the wild's pile needs something about as heavy.

**Goal:** Find a difference between captivity and the wild that favors captivity: a threat only wild leatherbacks face, or a protection only captive ones get. An answer that talks about other animals, or that makes captivity sound even worse, won't do it.

### Choice Notes

**(A) No Distinction.** This compares leatherbacks with other large aquatic reptiles. The puzzle is captive leatherbacks versus wild leatherbacks, and this fact is the same for both groups.

**(B) No Distinction.** How long sea turtles live compared with other marine animals says nothing about captive versus wild leatherbacks. A fact true of every leatherback can't explain why two groups of them come out even.

**(C) Correct.** Here is the weight on the wild side. Most leatherbacks that die in the wild are killed by predators, a threat captive turtles don't face. So each setting has its own major killer: disease in captivity, predators in the wild. That's how the odds can come out about the same. The move to keep: when two groups "should" differ but don't, look for a second difference that cancels the first.

**(D) Deepens Paradox.** If few zoologists know how to build a healthy environment for captive leatherbacks, captivity sounds even worse. That makes the equal odds more surprising, not less. It's tempting because it's about captivity, but it adds weight to the wrong side.

**(E) Out of Scope.** Whether size reveals age has nothing to do with how often turtles in either setting reach their maximum life expectancy. Even if it raised doubts about how ages are estimated, those doubts would apply to both groups and wouldn't explain why the two come out even.

## PT117-S3-Q8 | Method | A
explanation_id: a0084eb1-010f-486a-9dd2-51acfc8f4d80

### Argument Breakdown

**Rossi's Conclusion:** Children should have the right to vote.

**Rossi's Evidence:** It is undemocratic for people to live under a government in which their interests are not represented. And children's interests are sometimes different from their parents'.

**Smith's Response:** Smith grants that children's interests can differ from their parents' (deficits run up now will hurt their generation later). But Smith argues that children's ideas of what can or should be done are too simple and their time horizons too different from adults', so they can't be given the responsibility of voting.

**Evaluate:** The stem asks only about Rossi, so Smith is background here. How does Rossi get to the conclusion? Rossi's first sentence isn't about children at all. It's a broad rule about people in general: if your interests aren't represented, that's undemocratic. Then comes a fact that brings children under the rule: their interests sometimes differ from their parents', so, on Rossi's view, the parents' votes don't fully represent them. Put the two together and children need a vote of their own.

That's the shape: a general rule, a specific case that fits it, and a conclusion about the case.

**Goal:** Find the answer that says Rossi applies a general principle. Check each answer as a description of Rossi's argument, not Smith's.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** "It is undemocratic for people to live under a government in which their interests are not represented" covers everyone, not only children. Rossi's argument leans on that principle and applies it to one group. When an argument opens with a broad "it is wrong / unfair / undemocratic to..." and then reasons about a particular case, "appeals to a general principle" describes it.

**(B) Not in Stimulus.** Rossi speaks first and doesn't question anyone's honesty or motives. There's no opponent in Rossi's argument at all.

**(C) Wrong Speaker.** Rossi argues from what democracy requires, not from what would happen if children voted. If anyone comes close to weighing the effects of the proposal, it's Smith, who worries about what children would do with the vote. This answer is tempting because it fits the dialogue, but it describes the wrong speaker.

**(D) Not in Stimulus.** This is the most tempting wrong answer, maybe because Rossi's argument is short. But Rossi does give a rationale for the policy. The policy is "children should have the right to vote." The rationale is the principle (unrepresented interests are undemocratic) plus the fact (children's interests can differ from their parents'). That's a reason, not just a description.

**(E) Not in Stimulus.** This describes equivocation. The term Rossi uses twice is "interests," and it means the same thing both times: what is good for a person. No shift in meaning.

## PT119-S4-Q2 | Method | B

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** You can rely on Barr Motor Company to produce more winning automobiles than its competitors.

**Evidence:** Pride in their work explains why Austin Stables produces more winning racehorses than any other stable. That kind of pride isn't found only in horse racing. Barr has shown similar pride for generations.

**Evaluate:** Austin Stables never comes up in the conclusion, so why mention it? Because the ad wants us to carry a lesson from one business to another. Pride made Austin beat its rivals. Barr has the same pride. So Barr will beat its rivals too.

That's an analogy: two cases share a trait, and what is true of the first is claimed for the second. Notice the ad never shows Barr winning anything. The whole case for Barr rests on the comparison.

**Goal:** Find the answer that says the ad uses an analogy (Austin Stables) to conclude that Barr outperforms its competitors. Check both halves of each answer: the move it names and the claim it says the move supports.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Not in Stimulus.** The ad never mentions repeat customers. It talks only about pride and winning.

**(B) Correct.** The analogy is Austin Stables, and the conclusion, more winning cars than its competitors, is a claim that Barr is superior to its competitors. Both halves match. When a Method stimulus brings in a second case that the conclusion never mentions, check whether the argument is borrowing a lesson from it. That's the analogy.

**(C) Wrong Conclusion.** This is the most tempting wrong answer. The ad does say Barr has a tradition of pride, but it only asserts it ("For generations we at Barr Motor Company have demonstrated similar pride"). Nothing in the ad proves it. And the tradition of pride is a premise, not where the ad is headed. The ad's point is that Barr will produce more winners.

**(D) Opposite.** The ad leans on pride as the reason for Austin's success. Downplaying pride would undercut the ad's own comparison.

**(E) Not in Stimulus.** The ad says Barr's pride is "similar." It never compares how old the two traditions are.

## PT123-S3-Q2 | Paradox | A
explanation_id: 024b58e6-5d5c-4e51-b8ff-f32dc5ce5e85

### Argument Breakdown

**Given that:** Jimmy replaced an old gas water heater with a new, pilotless one rated as highly efficient.

**How can it be that:** Jimmy's gas bills went up afterward?

**Evaluate:** A gas bill is the amount of gas used times the price of gas. So there are two routes to a bigger bill, and neither requires the new heater to be inefficient.

1. More gas used somewhere in the house: more people, a new gas appliance, more heating.
2. A higher price per unit of gas.

The efficient heater might be saving gas while something else uses more, or while each unit costs more.

**Goal:** Four answers will put a rise in usage or price after the switch. Eliminate those. The correct answer won't give Jimmy's household more gas use or a higher rate.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** This says the new heater accounts for a smaller share of the household's gas than the old one did. Read carefully: that's a percentage, not an amount. It tells us nothing about whether the household used more gas or paid more for it, so it doesn't explain the higher bills. If the share fell because the heater uses less gas, it makes the increase more puzzling. If the share fell because other uses grew, the explanation would be those other uses, which this answer never names. Either way, it adds no route to a bigger bill. The move on an EXCEPT Paradox: sort each answer into a route that produces the result, and the one that fits no route is the answer.

**(B) Explains.** Doubling the household means more showers, more laundry and more hot water. More gas used means a bigger bill. Route 1.

**(C) Explains.** Jimmy started using a gas dryer at the same time. That's a new source of gas use the old bills never included. Route 1.

**(D) Explains.** Higher rates mean each unit of gas costs more, so the bill can rise even if usage falls. Route 2.

**(E) Explains.** Unusually cold weather led to heavy gas usage. More gas used, bigger bill. Route 1.
