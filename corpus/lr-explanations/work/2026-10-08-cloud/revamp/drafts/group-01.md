## PT158-S2-Q10 | Flaw | E
explanation_id: 5b6e6a77-8a8e-4229-9ebb-3626cb21bf9e

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Swahili culture was, to some extent, influenced by Oromo culture.

**Evidence:** In the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, the Swahili built tombs with large pillars and paneled facades. Those structures are widespread among the Oromo, and unknown among every other people the Swahili had contact with.

**Evaluate:** The evidence is a curious fact: two cultures share an unusual style of tomb, and nobody else around them has it. The historian's explanation is that the style moved from the Oromo to the Swahili.

Given that both groups had these tombs, how could we still argue that the Oromo didn't influence the Swahili? Turn the arrow around. Maybe the Swahili built them first and the Oromo copied them. The shared style shows a connection. It doesn't show which way the connection ran.

So what would the historian need to believe for the arrow to point from Oromo to Swahili? That the Oromo had the style first. Nothing in the argument tells us that. We only get dates for the Swahili tombs.

**Goal:** The flaw is assuming the direction of influence. Look for an answer saying the historian takes for granted that the Oromo built these tombs before the Swahili did, or one saying the argument ignores the possibility that the Swahili influenced the Oromo.

### Choice Notes

**(A) No Impact.** "Fails to address" means ask whether this possibility would weaken. Suppose the Oromo used the structures for something other than burial. The Swahili could still have borrowed the pillars and facades from them. The conclusion is about influence on style, so the use of the buildings doesn't touch it.

**(B) Wrong Flaw.** This is the "earlier, so it caused it" flaw. The historian never says which group came first. That missing fact is the actual problem, so an answer that says the argument relied on a time order describes something the argument didn't do.

**(C) Wrong Flaw.** The conclusion is modest ("to some extent"), but that isn't a flaw. A careful conclusion from strong premises would make the argument better, not worse. And the premises don't strongly support anything broader anyway.

**(D) Not Assumed.** This is the tempting one, because a third source is a classic alternate explanation. "Takes for granted" means negate it and ask whether the argument falls apart. Negated: some third civilization created the first tombs of this kind. The Swahili could still have picked up the style from the Oromo, who got it from that third group. Swahili culture would still have been influenced by Oromo culture, so the historian doesn't need to assume D.

**(E) Correct.** Negate it: the Oromo did not start building these tombs before the Swahili. Now the Swahili may have had the style first, and the Oromo may have copied it. The conclusion loses its footing. When an author explains a shared feature by saying one group influenced the other, ask who had it first. The influence can only run from the earlier group to the later one.

## PT158-S2-Q14 | Flaw | E
explanation_id: c5e8fdf8-96fa-4637-bbf5-61dfd7ae2727

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** There are celestial objects in this galaxy that generate light but are not stars.

**Evidence:** Planets do not generate light; they only reflect it. Besides stars, this galaxy has many other celestial objects that are not planets.

**Evaluate:** Sort the galaxy into three bins: stars, planets, and everything else. We know planets don't generate light. We know the third bin isn't empty. The author concludes that something in that third bin generates light.

Why would it? The only thing we've been told about the third bin is that it isn't made of planets. That would matter only if planets were the only objects that don't generate light. Picture a moon or an asteroid. It isn't a star and it isn't a planet. It could reflect light without generating any, just like a planet does.

The author treats "not a planet" as if it meant "generates light." That's a Necessary vs. Sufficient error. The premise says being a planet is enough to not generate light. It doesn't say being a planet is required for that.

**Goal:** The stem already supplies "fails to consider the possibility that," so we want a possibility that would weaken the argument. Predict: the other non-star objects might not generate light either. In other words, planets aren't the only objects that don't generate light.

### Choice Notes

**(A) No Impact.** This is the closest-sounding trap, since it swaps "generate" for "reflect." Ask whether it weakens. If no other objects reflect light, we still know nothing about whether they generate it. The argument's question is about generating light, so A leaves it untouched.

**(B) Out of Scope.** The conclusion is only about this galaxy. How big a share of the universe this galaxy holds doesn't affect whether something in it generates light.

**(C) No Impact.** Light-generating objects outside this galaxy don't help or hurt a conclusion about objects inside it.

**(D) No Impact.** Stars and planets can differ in a hundred ways. The question is whether the objects that are neither stars nor planets generate light, and D says nothing about them.

**(E) Correct.** Run the test. If planets aren't the only objects that don't generate light, then the "many other celestial objects" could all be dark too. Then nothing tells us any non-star generates light, and the conclusion collapses. When an argument moves from "X is not a member of the group that lacks a feature" to "X has the feature," check whether that group was the only one lacking it.

## PT158-S2-Q18 | Flaw | E
explanation_id: 9dc92652-6d3d-474d-b725-2d5369d40bbd

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Choosing a specialist or a general practitioner for physical therapy will not affect one's chances of major improvement.

**Evidence:** For patients treated less than six weeks, about 31 percent showed major improvement, whether they saw a general practitioner or a specialist. For longer treatment, about 50 percent showed major improvement, again with either kind of practitioner.

**Evaluate:** The data are about whole groups of patients. The conclusion is about "one's chances," meaning yours, with your particular injury. Equal group averages can hide big differences inside the groups.

Here's a made-up case. Suppose half of all patients have knee injuries and half have back injuries. Specialists get major improvement in 60% of knee patients and 20% of back patients. General practitioners get 20% of knee patients and 60% of back patients. Both groups average 40%. But if you have a bad knee, the specialist triples your chances.

So the argument assumes that matching overall rates mean every patient does equally well with either practitioner. That assumption is the gap.

**Goal:** Look for an answer saying the two kinds of practitioner might do better with different kinds of patients or injuries, so the choice could matter for an individual even though the overall rates match.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Misdescribes Argument.** "Presumes" means the argument would need this. But the argument never uses data about "any improvement." Both of its figures are already about major improvement. So it never moves from any improvement to major improvement, and A describes a step that isn't there.

**(B) Wrong Flaw.** Complaints that an argument "provides no information" about something are rarely the flaw. Here the missing information is about which injuries need short or long treatment. The argument compares practitioners within each length of treatment, so how cases get sorted by length doesn't touch the comparison.

**(C) No Impact.** The evidence comes from records of improvement, not from patients' opinions of their practitioners. Nothing in the argument depends on patients reporting favorably, so a reporting bias has nothing to distort.

**(D) Wrong Flaw.** The figures are percentages within each group, so the groups don't need to be the same size to be compared. A survey of 200 patients with one kind of practitioner and 300 with the other can still show 31 percent in both.

**(E) Correct.** "Overlooks the possibility" means ask whether it weakens. It does: if specialists excel at one type of injury and general practitioners at another, a patient with the first type has better chances with a specialist. That's exactly the knee and back case. When an argument moves from equal results across a whole group to "it won't matter for you," look for a way the parts of the group could differ.

## PT158-S2-Q2 | Flaw | A
explanation_id: e9dbeec3-2277-4bd3-861a-a723ee66e523

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Smith's proposed ordinance does not even merit consideration.

**Evidence:** Four years ago, Smith owned an appliance store famous for using bait-and-switch, the very tactic the ordinance would prohibit.

**Evaluate:** This is a rebuttal. Smith proposes something, and the council president rejects it. When a rebuttal is the flaw, check two famous flaws first. Does the speaker attack the person instead of the idea (ad hominem)? Or does the speaker treat a weak case for a claim as proof the claim is false (unproven vs. proven false)?

Look at the reason the president gives. It's not about the ordinance at all. It's Smith's past. Smith may be a hypocrite, but that tells us nothing about whether banning bait-and-switch is a good idea. Someone who once ran a store on that tactic might even know better than anyone why it should be banned.

So the president dismisses the proposal because of who proposed it. That's ad hominem.

**Goal:** Look for the formal version: the president rejects the ordinance because of its source rather than its content. A more specific version would say the president dismisses a proposal because the person who made it once did the thing it targets.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Match it piece by piece. "Dismisses the proposed ordinance" matches "does not even merit consideration." "Because of its source" matches the attack on Smith's past. The president never says a word about what the ordinance would do. When a speaker answers a proposal by talking about the person who made it, look for "source rather than content."

**(B) Wrong Flaw.** The president doesn't call the ordinance false, and Smith's old store isn't a fact that conflicts with the ordinance. The past and the proposal can both stand, so nothing is being disproven by a counterexample.

**(C) Wrong Flaw.** The argument never talks about sales methods at all. A distinction between deceptive and legitimate tactics wouldn't fix an argument whose only reason is Smith's history.

**(D) Circular.** The conclusion (the ordinance doesn't merit consideration) and the premise (Smith's store used bait-and-switch) are different claims. The problem is that the premise is irrelevant, not that it repeats the conclusion.

**(E) Wrong Flaw.** Nothing is generalized here. The president reasons about one person and one proposal, not from a few cases to all cases.
