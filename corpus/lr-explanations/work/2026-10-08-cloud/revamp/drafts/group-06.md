## PT148-S4-Q2 | Agree/Disagree | C
explanation_id: dfdb259f-4a76-4cd7-aa1a-45bdf5097d30

### Argument Breakdown

**Bell's Claims:** Bell makes four claims.

1. The media commentators are wrong to criticize Klein's policies.
2. Klein's policies are unpopular.
3. Klein's policies avoided an impending catastrophe.
4. Klein is just the person we need making important decisions in the future.

**Soltan's Responses:** Go through Bell's claims one at a time and ask: does Soltan argue the opposite?

1. No comment. Soltan never mentions the commentators.
2. No disagreement. Soltan says Klein "will not have the political support" to make future decisions, which fits with her policies being unpopular.
3. No disagreement. Soltan grants that the policies "have been effective". That sits comfortably with Bell's claim that they avoided a catastrophe.
4. Disagrees. Bell wants Klein making the important decisions ahead. Soltan concludes "she should step down", because she won't have the support to make those decisions.

**Evaluate:** Soltan opens by agreeing with Bell ("Klein's policies have been effective") and then turns with "but politics matters." That turn is where the clash lives. Both speakers look at the same future. Bell says Klein is the right person to make its decisions. Soltan says she won't be able to make them, so she should go. Someone who thinks Klein is "just the person we need making important decisions in the future" can't also think she should step down before those decisions arrive.

**Goal:** Find the answer about whether Klein should stay in her role: Bell's claim 4 against Soltan's conclusion. Expect answers built from claims 2 and 3, which are points of agreement or no comment, not disagreement.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Both Agree.** Can we support a Disagree position? Soltan says it outright: the policies "have been effective". Bell says they "avoided an impending catastrophe", which is about as effective as a policy gets. Neither speaker denies it, so this is common ground.

**(B) Both Agree.** Bell says the policies are unpopular. Soltan never denies that, and "politics matters" plus the lack of "political support" suggests Soltan sees the same unpopularity. No one takes the opposite side.

**(C) Correct.** Run both positions. Soltan's Agree position is explicit: "So she should step down." Bell's Disagree position comes from claim 4. If Klein is "just the person we need making important decisions in the future", Bell wants her to stay. The move to remember: a speaker's conclusion often clashes with the other speaker's claim even when the two use different words. Translate each into what it says Klein should do, and the disagreement shows.

**(D) Both Agree.** Soltan says "important decisions will need to be made", and Bell talks about Klein "making important decisions in the future". Both assume those decisions are coming. They differ only on who should make them.

**(E) Unsupported Disagree Position.** Bell says the policies avoided an impending catastrophe, so Bell holds the Agree side. Soltan never discusses a catastrophe at all, and "have been effective" leans toward agreement. Nobody holds the Disagree side.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** It is good to have national leaders voted out of office after a few years.

**Intermediate Conclusion:** Reforms are generally undertaken early in a new government.

**Evidence:** If leaders don't act quickly on a problem and it becomes an issue later, they have to deny there is a problem, deny anything could have been done, or admit responsibility for letting the problem persist.

**Evaluate:** Before reading, set the posture for Main Conclusion: the conclusion usually comes earlier than its support. So the first question for the opening sentence is whether it sounds like the pundit's opinion. "It is good to" is a judgment, so it's a strong candidate.

The next words settle it: "The reason is that..." A reason has to be a reason for something, and the only thing before it is the first sentence. Now run the chain with "why should we believe that?"

Why is it good to vote leaders out after a few years? Because reforms happen early in a new government, so fresh governments keep reforms coming.

Why do reforms happen early? Because a leader who waits and then faces the problem later is stuck. They have to deny the problem, deny anything could have been done, or take the blame for it. Acting early avoids that bind.

The first sentence sits at the top. Nothing in the passage is offered as a reason for anything above it.

**Goal:** Restate the first sentence: leaders should be replaced after a few years. "It is good to do X" and "X should be done" are close enough to count as a match.

### Choice Notes

**(A) New Idea.** The pundit never mentions motivation, and never says new leaders will try harder because old ones were voted out. A conclusion that was never stated can't be the main conclusion. This one also adds a condition ("leaders who fail to solve problems") the first sentence doesn't have.

**(B) Premise Distortion.** This borrows from the last sentence, but it changes it. The pundit says a leader who waited would have to deny the problem or deny anything could be done, "otherwise" admit responsibility. That's a description of the leader's options, not a claim about leaders who "stay in power too long". And even an accurate version of the last sentence would be support for the reform claim, not the top of the argument.

**(C) Intermediate Conclusion.** This is the second sentence, and it's the most tempting trap because it is supported (the last sentence explains it). But run the test the other way. Why believe it's good to vote leaders out? Because reforms come early. That makes C a reason for the first sentence, so it sits below the main conclusion. "Most likely" also overstates "generally".

**(D) New Idea.** The pundit never says any leader should be given more time. If anything, the argument points the other way: replace leaders after a few years so reforms keep happening.

**(E) Correct.** "It is good to have national leaders voted out of office after a few years" becomes "National leaders should be removed from office every few years by the voting in of new leaders." Same action (voting in new leaders), same timing (every few years), and "it is good" matches "should". The move: once the support test shows which claim everything else props up, match that claim's meaning, not its exact wording.

### Argument Breakdown

**Opposing Point:** Many people with Internet access long for emotional connection to a global human community, and that longing often leads them to use the Internet to learn about other cultures.

**Conclusion:** Learning about other cultures probably will not satisfy their longing.

**Evidence:** The Internet is used primarily for acquiring information rather than developing feelings of interconnectedness.

**Evaluate:** The first two sentences describe a plan: people want connection, so they go online and learn about other cultures. They aren't the columnist's opinion; they set up the thing the columnist is about to judge. "However" marks the turn. The columnist's verdict is that this plan probably won't work.

Then "for" introduces the reason. Read it as a support test: why should we believe learning about cultures won't satisfy the longing? Because the Internet is mainly for information, not for feeling connected. That answers the question, so the claim between "However" and "for" is the conclusion.

Keep the strength in view. The columnist says "probably will not", not "cannot".

**Goal:** Find the answer that says learning about other cultures online probably won't give these people the emotional connection they want.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Background.** This is the first sentence. It reports what people express. The columnist doesn't argue for it; it's the starting situation the conclusion reacts to.

**(B) Background.** This is the second sentence: the longing leads people to learn about other cultures online. Again, why should we believe it? The columnist gives no reason. It's the behavior the conclusion judges, not the judgment.

**(C) Premise.** This restates the claim after "for", the reason the plan probably fails. It answers "why believe the conclusion?", so it can't be the conclusion itself.

**(D) Correct.** Line it up with the claim after "However". "People desiring connection to a global human community" is "their longing". "Learning about other cultures through the Internet" is the activity the second sentence describes. "Probably will not provide emotional connection" matches "probably will not satisfy their longing", and it keeps "probably". The move: when the conclusion uses a pronoun like "their", fill in who it refers to before matching, and D does exactly that.

**(E) Premise Plus Overreach.** This is the most tempting trap because it sounds like the whole story. Look at what it does. The first half ("they tend to engage in behavior that results in the acquisition of knowledge about other cultures") is the second sentence, a premise. The second half turns the columnist's prediction about this one plan into a general rule about any such behavior, which "rarely produces genuine emotional connection". The columnist's reason is specific to the Internet being a tool for information, so it doesn't support a claim about every way of learning about other cultures. And the conclusion says the plan "probably will not satisfy" the longing, not that the behavior "rarely" produces connection.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** The sole reason sexual reproduction has become the rule among animals and plants is that natural selection has favored some entire species over others.

**Evidence:** Sexual reproduction's evolutionary advantage is that it increases genetic variation in a species, which helps the species as a whole. But that increased variation is not advantageous for any individual member of the species.

**Evaluate:** "It follows that" marks the last sentence as the conclusion, so the first two sentences are the support. The claim in the stem is the second one.

Why would the biologist need it? The conclusion says the *sole* reason sexual reproduction spread is selection between whole species. To get to "sole", the biologist has to shut the other door: maybe sexual reproduction spread because it helped individuals. The claim in the stem does exactly that. If increased variation helps no individual, the benefit exists only at the species level, so selection among species is what's left.

Notice what "However" is doing. It doesn't turn against the first sentence or introduce the biologist's opponent. It sets "good for the species" beside "not good for any individual", and the conclusion uses both halves.

**Goal:** The claim is a premise that supports the conclusion.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Run the support test on the conclusion: why should we believe species-level selection is the sole reason? Because the benefit is real for the species and absent for every individual. The claim in the stem is half of that answer. The move: when "However" appears in the middle of the support, ask whether it marks the author's turn against a view or just a contrast between two premises. Here it's the second.

**(B) Opposing Role.** This is the trap "However" sets up. A claim that came after "However" can feel like it pushes back on something. But it doesn't push back on the conclusion; it's the reason the conclusion can say "sole". Remove it and the argument gets weaker, which is the opposite of casting doubt.

**(C) Opposing Role.** The biologist never questions this claim. It's asserted plainly and then used, with "It follows that", to reach the conclusion.

**(D) Wrong Phenomenon.** There is a phenomenon the argument explains: sexual reproduction has become the rule among animals and plants. The claim in the stem isn't that phenomenon. It's a fact about who benefits from genetic variation, used to support the explanation.

**(E) Wrong Explanation.** The explanation the biologist proposes is in the conclusion: natural selection favoring entire species. The claim in the stem is a reason to accept that explanation, not the explanation itself.

### Argument Breakdown

**Opposing Point:** Banking industry visionaries foresee a bright near future in which customers can do all their financial business by computer or telephone from home.

**Conclusion:** That future may be more of a paradise for banks than for their customers.

**Evidence:** As banks eliminate branch offices and customer-service employees, customers will have to serve as their own tellers and pay more transaction fees for their efforts.

**Evaluate:** The first sentence is attributed to "Banking industry visionaries". When a passage opens with someone else's view, expect the author to respond, and "But" is the response.

The conclusion uses referential language, so fill it in before matching. "That" refers to the bright day the visionaries foresee: customers doing all their banking from home. So the conclusion says home banking may be better for banks than for customers.

Now the support test. Why believe banks come out ahead? Because banks shed branches and staff while customers do the tellers' work and pay more fees. That's a reason for the second sentence, so the second sentence is the top.

Two features to carry into the answer: it's a comparison (banks versus customers), and it's hedged ("may be").

**Goal:** Find an answer that says home banking may benefit banks more than it benefits customers.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Opposing Point.** This is the visionaries' forecast from the first sentence. The author doesn't argue for it; the author responds to it with "But".

**(B) Correct.** Substitute and compare. "Enabling bank customers to transact all their financial business by means of computers or telephones from their own homes" is what "that" refers to. "May be more beneficial to banks than to their customers" matches "may be more of a paradise for banks than for their customers", keeping both the comparison and the "may". The move: replace a pronoun in the conclusion with what it refers to, then look for the answer that says the full thing.

**(C) Premise.** This is the last sentence nearly word for word, and it's the most tempting trap because it's the most concrete claim in the passage. Run the support test. Why should we believe home banking favors banks? Because customers become their own tellers and pay more fees. C is the "because", so it supports the conclusion rather than being it. Ask the reverse: does the "paradise for banks" claim give us any reason to believe customers will pay more fees? No.

**(D) Half the Comparison.** The passage suggests banks gain from closing branches, but the author's claim is comparative: banks gain more than customers do. D drops the customers entirely, and it's also an unstated step rather than the claim the author makes.

**(E) Never Stated.** The author never says home banking is what allows banks to close branches. The passage only says branches are being eliminated "as" this happens. Even if E is a reasonable background assumption, it isn't the claim the passage argues for, and it says nothing about who benefits more.

## PT157-S3-Q22 | Role | C

### Argument Breakdown

**Opposing Point:** Philosophers usually treat emotions as nonrational.

**Conclusion:** Emotion is not nonrational.

**Intermediate Conclusion:** Emotion only seems nonrational because language lacks the ability to convey adequate conceptions of emotion.

**Evidence:** The words we use for emotions name only very general kinds of inner experience: excitement, calm, joy, and so on.

**Example:** So, for one case, there is no language for describing just how one joy differs from another.

**Evaluate:** The passage opens with what philosophers "usually" think, and "But" turns against it. "Emotion is not nonrational" is the philosopher's rebuttal and the main conclusion.

Everything after the colon backs it up. Why believe emotion isn't nonrational? Because it only seems that way, since language can't convey emotion well. Why believe language can't convey emotion well? Because emotion words name only very general kinds of experience.

The last sentence starts with "Thus, for example", which can look confusing. "Thus" says the sentence follows from the one before it. "For example" says it is one case of that sentence. Put them together: if emotion words are only general, then naturally we have no words for how one joy differs from another. It takes the general claim about emotion words and shows it in a single emotion. That's an illustration, and it sits at the bottom of the chain.

**Goal:** The joy claim is a specific example of the general claim about emotion words, and through that claim it supports the conclusion.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Phenomenon.** The thing the argument explains is that emotion *seems* nonrational. The joy claim isn't an instance of emotion seeming nonrational. It's an instance of language being too general, which is part of the explanation. A has the right word ("example") attached to the wrong claim.

**(B) Main Conclusion Trap.** "Thus" invites this answer, and the claim is the last sentence. Run the support test on it. Does "there is no language for describing how one joy differs from another" give us a reason to believe emotion isn't nonrational? It does, but only indirectly, by illustrating the claim about general words. And the passage's rebuttal ("But emotion is not nonrational") is what everything else serves. A last sentence introduced by "for example" is support, not the top of the argument.

**(C) Correct.** Check each part. "A specific instance": one particular emotion, joy. "Illustrating a general claim": that emotion words name only very general kinds of experience. "Thereby indirectly supporting the conclusion": the general claim supports the point about language, which supports the conclusion that emotion isn't nonrational. The move: when the chain has several levels, find the claim directly above the one in the stem, and expect the right answer to describe that link.

**(D) Concession.** A concession would grant something to the philosophers who treat emotion as nonrational. The joy claim grants them nothing. It's part of the case against them.

**(E) One Level Off.** The proposed explanation for why emotion seems nonrational is that language "lacks the ability to convey adequate conceptions of emotion". The joy claim is one example within that explanation, two steps below it, not the explanation itself.
