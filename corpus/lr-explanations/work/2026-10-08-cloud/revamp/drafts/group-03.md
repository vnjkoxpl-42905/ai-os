### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** Some people propose one test for nonaddictiveness: if at least some habitual users can stop using a substance, it's nonaddictive.

The expert rejects that as the sole test. It would label some prime examples of addictive substances nonaddictive.

Any adequate set of criteria must include the medical experts' view: a substance is addictive only if withdrawal from habitual use causes most users extreme psychological and physiological difficulty.

Addictive → most users have extreme difficulty withdrawing

Contrapositive:

NOT (most users have extreme difficulty withdrawing) → NOT addictive

**Evaluate:** The last sentence is the one with real force. "Only if" puts the requirement on the right: being addictive requires that most users struggle badly when they stop. So the rule can prove two things. If a substance is addictive, most users have extreme difficulty withdrawing. If most users don't have that difficulty, the substance isn't addictive.

Notice what the rule doesn't do. It never says that difficulty is enough to make something addictive, and it's about most users, not one person.

The first part of the passage also gives a useful hint about how the test is built. "Some users can quit" isn't enough to call a substance nonaddictive. What matters is what happens to most users.

**Goal:** Expect an answer that runs the rule forward or backward without reversing it. Something like "if a substance is addictive, it can't be that most users quit easily," or "if most users quit with little difficulty, the substance isn't addictive."

### Choice Notes

**(A) Reversal.** This reads the rule backward. The expert says addictiveness requires extreme difficulty, not that extreme difficulty proves addictiveness. A also drops "most users" and talks about a single person, so even the trigger doesn't match.

**(B) Out of Scope.** The passage says the "some users can quit" test would wrongly call some addictive substances nonaddictive. That's the inadequate test. It says nothing about how many substances an adequate definition would count, or how many are counted now.

**(C) Correct.** Start from the rule: addictive → most users have extreme difficulty withdrawing. If most users have extreme difficulty, then it can't also be true that most users quit with "little or no" difficulty. So an addictive substance requires that quitting easily is "not true for most habitual users," which is exactly what C says.

The "some habitual users can cease" part is the passage's own example. Some users quitting easily doesn't decide anything; the question is whether most do. The move to carry forward: when an answer uses "only if," diagram it and check it against the rule. C puts the same requirement on the right, so it's the original rule restated.

**(D) Wrong Trigger.** This is the tempting one, because "without significant difficulty" sounds like the contrapositive. Look at what it describes: one person who used the substance "throughout life." That person never withdrew, so we learn nothing about withdrawal, and one user isn't most users. The contrapositive needs facts about most users when they stop.

**(E) Unsupported.** The expert disagrees with one proposed test and endorses a requirement medical experts hold. That suggests addiction can be defined well enough to be useful. Nothing supports "impossible to define with precision."

### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** The style manual gives three rules.

1. For quotations from older works: if archaic spellings or punctuation occur infrequently and don't interfere with comprehension, they're preserved.

Infrequent + doesn't interfere → preserve

2. If they occur frequently, the editor may modernize them. The editor then explains it in a note in the text or, when similar modernizing was done in more than one quotation, in a general statement in the preface.

Frequent → may modernize (with a note, or a preface statement)

3. For quotations from modern works: obvious typographical errors may be corrected without explanation.

Obvious typo in a modern work → may correct without explanation

**Evaluate:** These are rules, so ask what triggers each one and what each one gives. Rule 1 needs two conditions together. Rule 2 needs only frequency, and it gives permission ("may"), not an obligation. Rule 3 is about modern works and typos, not archaic styles.

Watch the gaps. If something is infrequent but does interfere, Rule 1 isn't triggered, and no rule says what happens next. If the editor modernizes one quotation, nothing says the preface option kicks in.

**Goal:** The answer should trigger one rule fully and stay within what that rule gives. The best candidate is Rule 2: frequent archaic spelling or punctuation in an older work means the editor may modernize it.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Untriggered Rule.** The answer doesn't say the word is from an older work or archaic. If it's an obvious typo in a modern work, Rule 3 lets the editor correct it with no explanation at all. But knocking out Rule 1 doesn't create a duty to modernize. The only rule that allows modernizing is Rule 2, and it's triggered by frequency, not interference, and it says "may." C changes a permission into "should" and attaches it to the wrong trigger.

The extra fact, that it "interferes with reader comprehension," doesn't hurt: a rule still applies when we're told more than its trigger. And D keeps the modality right with "may." ### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** Early urban societies couldn't have been maintained without large-scale farming nearby, because foraging and similar methods can't support populations that dense.

Early urban society → large-scale farming nearby

Large-scale farming requires irrigation.

Large-scale farming → irrigation

Irrigation was unfeasible far from rivers or lakes until more recent times.

Far from rivers or lakes (early times) → NOT irrigation

**Evaluate:** Three requirements, and they link. An early urban society needs large-scale farming nearby. Large-scale farming needs irrigation. So an early urban society needs irrigation nearby.

Early urban society → large-scale farming → irrigation

Now apply the last fact through the contrapositive. Far from a river or lake, in early times, there was no irrigation. No irrigation means no large-scale farming, and no large-scale farming nearby means no early urban society.

Far from rivers or lakes (early times) → NOT irrigation → NOT large-scale farming → NOT early urban society

Keep the time limit. Irrigation was unfeasible far from water only "until more recent times," so the inference is about early societies.

**Goal:** Expect something like "in early times, urban societies couldn't be maintained far from rivers or lakes."

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** The passage is about what urban societies required, not where most people lived. Early peoples could have lived anywhere in small foraging groups that needed no irrigation.

**(B) Too Broad.** This is the most tempting wrong answer, because it sounds like the chain. But the chain is about urban societies, which need dense-population farming. B talks about any society. A small society far from water could forage, so farming without irrigation isn't required for it. B also drops "early." In more recent times, irrigation far from rivers is feasible.

**(C) Correct.** This is the chain run backward from the fact. Far from rivers or lakes in early times, irrigation was unfeasible. No irrigation means no large-scale farming, and without nearby large-scale farming an early urban society couldn't be maintained. The move: when requirements link head to tail, chain them, then use a fact that denies the last requirement to knock out everything before it.

**(D) Contradicted.** Urban societies need large-scale farming, and the passage says large-scale farming requires irrigation. Being near a river or lake makes irrigation possible. It doesn't remove the need for it.

**(E) Out of Scope.** Rural societies aren't discussed. We know foraging can't support dense urban populations, but nothing compares how rural societies got their food.
