## PT105-S4-Q14 | Necessary Assumption | D
explanation_id: 352458de-28ad-43f0-a977-cf5751c9f621

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** We urgently need a major tax cut for upper-income citizens.

**Evidence:** Unemployment is at its highest level ever, and legislators need to act quickly. The citizen predicts that the tax cut would bring a large increase in investment, which would create new jobs. Without the tax cut, investment will not grow.

**Evaluate:** This is a plan, so ask what has to be true for the plan to work at all. The whole chain runs through one step: tax cut, then more investment, then new jobs. But the tax cut only puts money in upper-income citizens' pockets. What if there's nothing they can do with that money that raises investment?

Picture it. The money arrives, and the only things available are spending on goods that create no new investment, or paying down debts. Then the "correspondingly large increase in investment" can't happen, and the jobs never appear. The citizen is taking for granted that the money can actually flow into investment.

**Goal:** Look for a defender of the middle step: the tax cut money can (or will) be used in ways that increase investment. Then negate it. If upper-income citizens could not use the money to raise investment, the plan should fall apart.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Out of Scope.** The citizen says unemployment is the highest ever, not that the recession is the worst ever. Negate it: "this is not the worst recession in the country's history." The argument for the tax cut doesn't change, since it rests on unemployment and on what the tax cut would do.

**(B) Too Strong.** This is the most tempting wrong answer because it sounds like the mechanism the citizen relies on. But it's a general sliding rule about every group and every size of tax cut. Negate it: "a bigger tax cut does not make a group more likely to invest." The citizen still could be right that this particular cut would lead to more investment. The argument needs this tax cut to be usable for investment, not a rule that bigger cuts always mean more investing.

**(C) Irrelevant Comparison.** Who has invested more money in the past, upper-income or lower-income citizens, isn't the question. Negate it: "upper-income citizens have not invested more in total than lower-income citizens." They could still invest the money from this tax cut, so the argument survives.

**(D) Correct.** Negate it: "upper-income citizens could not use the money gained from the tax cut in ways that increase investment." Now the plan's middle step is impossible. No new investment, no new jobs, and the reason for the urgent tax cut disappears.

Notice how weak D is. It only says the money "could" be used this way. That's exactly why it's necessary: weak answers negate into strong denials, and a strong denial of the plan's mechanism wrecks the argument. When an argument depends on a cause producing an effect, look for the answer that says the effect is at least possible.

**(E) Out of Scope.** The citizen is predicting what this tax cut will do, not relying on history. Negate it: "in the past, tax cuts for certain groups have not tended to create new jobs." The citizen's chain doesn't run through past tax cuts at all. This one could still produce investment and jobs, so E isn't required.

## PT119-S3-Q20 | Necessary Assumption | C
explanation_id: 3dbbe7ec-5548-455f-b9e4-fa006c62eaf5

### Argument Breakdown

**Opposing Point:** Critics say genetic engineering is unethical because it requires manipulating the genetic codes of organisms.

**Conclusion:** Genetic engineering is not unethical.

**Evidence:** Selective breeding is genetic engineering of a crude sort, and people have been doing it for millennia. Virtually every farm animal is the result of selective breeding for desired traits.

**Evaluate:** The scientist's move is "you already accept this, and it's the same kind of thing." Selective breeding is a form of genetic engineering, so genetic engineering gets the same ethical status.

But what status is that? Look at the premises again. They tell us selective breeding is old and widespread. They never say it's ethical. A long history doesn't settle that. Suppose a critic replied, "Fine, selective breeding is genetic engineering too, and it's been wrong all along." The scientist's evidence would then put genetic engineering in bad company, not good company.

So the argument only works if the comparison case is itself acceptable. The scientist is assuming the critics would have no ethical complaint about selective breeding.

**Goal:** Predict a defender of the comparison: selective breeding for desired traits is not unethical. Negate it ("selective breeding is unethical") and the evidence stops clearing genetic engineering.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Too Strong.** "Never unethical" covers every possible manipulation of a genetic code, in any form, for any purpose. Negate it: "manipulating the genetic code is sometimes unethical." The scientist can live with that. The argument is about genetic engineering as a technique, compared with selective breeding, and it doesn't need to defend every conceivable use.

**(B) Out of Scope.** This is the most tempting wrong answer because it sounds like a general defense of genetic engineering. But the scientist's comparison isn't to nature. Selective breeding is something people do to animals on purpose. Negate B: "some things nature accomplishes are unethical to accomplish with science." The argument never relied on nature, so it's untouched.

**(C) Correct.** Negate it: "the manipulation of the genetic code through selective breeding for desired traits is unethical." Now the scientist's evidence backfires. Genetic engineering is like selective breeding, and selective breeding is unethical, so pointing to farm animals no longer shows genetic engineering is acceptable. The conclusion loses its support.

This is the transferable move for arguments by comparison: "X is fine because X is just like Y" assumes Y is fine. When the premises describe Y without ever approving of it, the approval is the missing piece.

**(D) Out of Scope.** The argument is about ethics, not importance. Negate it: "selective breeding is not important for human survival." Selective breeding could still be ethical, and that's all the comparison needs.

**(E) Too Strong.** Two sweeping claims: science can do only what is natural, and nothing natural is unethical. The scientist needs neither. Negate it and the comparison between selective breeding and genetic engineering still stands exactly as it did.

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** When a bean dish's quality matters more than cooking it quickly, the beans should not be presoaked.

**Evidence:** Presoaking dried beans overnight reduces cooking time. Cooking without presoaking yields plumper beans.

**Evaluate:** Line up the words. The conclusion is about quality. The evidence about skipping the soak is about plumpness. Those aren't the same idea. Nothing in the argument says plumper beans make a better dish.

Imagine plumpness made no difference to how good the dish is, or even made it worse. Then skipping the soak would cost you time and buy you nothing in quality. The recommendation would have no reason behind it.

**Goal:** Predict a link between the new term in the conclusion and the evidence: plumper beans make for a better-quality dish. Negate it ("plumper beans do not enhance the quality of a dish") and the recommendation loses its only support.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** This is the link, almost word for word. Negate it: "plumper beans do not enhance the quality of a dish." Now the only benefit of skipping the soak has nothing to do with quality, so there's no reason to skip it when quality is what matters.

The move to carry forward: when the conclusion uses a term (quality) that the evidence never mentions, and the evidence uses a term (plumper) that the conclusion never mentions, the assumption usually connects the two.

**(B) Too Strong.** "No dishes" at all? Negate it: "some dish's quality improves with faster cooking." The argument can live with that. Its conclusion only covers dishes where quality outweighs speed, and it never needed faster cooking to be worthless for every dish in the world.

**(C) Out of Scope.** The argument never weighs appearance against taste. Plumper beans could improve a dish through either one, or through texture. Negate it and the link from plumpness to quality can still hold.

**(D) Out of Scope.** The argument is about whether to presoak the beans. Other ingredients never come up. Negate it ("some other ingredient needs to be presoaked") and the advice about the beans is unaffected.

**(E) Too Narrow.** This ties plumpness to taste, and to a sliding scale where every increase helps. The argument doesn't need either. Plumper beans could improve a dish's quality through texture or look even if they tasted the same. Negate E and A can still be true, so the argument survives.

## PT147-S4-Q6 | Necessary Assumption | B
explanation_id: 45d92455-5b00-4472-bb31-9a00e8dc6c24

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** Ample food and relatively little exercise can skew the results of research using laboratory animals.

**Evidence:** Laboratory animals have ample food and get relatively little exercise. Such studies often rely on the assumption that the animal subjects are healthy. For example, caloric-restriction studies take for granted that their subjects weren't overfed to begin with.

**Evaluate:** Follow the chain the scientist needs. Lab conditions, then something wrong with the animals, then a broken study assumption, then skewed results. The premises give us the first link (the conditions exist) and the third (the studies assume healthy subjects). What connects them?

The scientist never says the conditions are bad for the animals. Suppose eating freely and moving little left lab animals perfectly healthy. Then the studies' assumption of healthy subjects would be true, and there would be nothing for the conditions to skew. The whole worry depends on the conditions being capable of harming the animals' health.

**Goal:** Predict the missing link: ample food plus little exercise can make lab animals unhealthy. Notice the conclusion only says "can skew," so the assumption only needs "can be unhealthy," not "always is."

### Choice Notes

**(A) Reversal.** This is the most tempting wrong answer because it uses the right ingredients. But it runs the relationship the other way: a restricted diet and plenty of exercise are enough to make animals healthy. The scientist needs the opposite direction: ample food and little exercise can make them unhealthy. Negate A: "restricted diet and plenty of exercise don't guarantee health." Lab conditions could still be unhealthy, so the argument stands.

**(B) Correct.** Negate it: "laboratory conditions with ample food but little exercise cannot be unhealthy for the animals." Then the animals' health isn't threatened by those conditions, the studies' assumption of healthy subjects holds, and the conditions can't skew anything. The conclusion collapses.

B also matches the strength of the conclusion. "Can skew" needs only "can be unhealthy." When the conclusion is a weak possibility, expect the necessary assumption to be weak too.

**(C) Out of Scope.** The argument is about laboratory animals. Whether animals elsewhere live the same way doesn't affect whether lab conditions can skew lab results. Negate it and nothing changes.

**(D) Out of Scope.** What some studies take into account doesn't decide whether the conditions can skew results in the studies that rely on healthy subjects. Negate it ("no animal studies consider these differences") and, if anything, the scientist's worry gets stronger.

**(E) Out of Scope.** How steady the animals' eating habits are says nothing about whether those habits are healthy. Negate it and the argument is unaffected.
