## PT157-S2-Q7 | Most Supported | D
explanation_id: 601ad138-a674-4637-b665-78264592329e

### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** Mu mesons from cosmic rays travel to Earth at speeds approaching the speed of light. Lab mu mesons are nearly at rest, and they typically decay in much less time than the trip from just outside the atmosphere to our detectors takes.

If fast mu mesons decayed as fast as lab ones, we would detect only about one one-hundredth of the number we actually detect.

Then the blank: "Apparently, mu mesons moving at speeds near the speed of light _______."

**Evaluate:** The fourth sentence is the engine. It's a conditional, and the stimulus has already told us its result is false. We don't detect one one-hundredth of what we detect. We detect what we detect.

Decay as fast as lab mesons → detect about 1/100 of the actual number

We don't get the right side, so we can't have the left side. The fast mesons do not decay as fast as the lab mesons.

Put it in plain terms. Lab mesons, nearly at rest, fall apart before they could finish that trip. Yet roughly a hundred times more fast mesons reach the detectors than the lab rate allows. Something about the fast ones keeps them around longer, and the one difference the stimulus gives us between the two groups is speed.

You don't need any physics here. Swap "decay" for "fade" and the logic is the same.

**Goal:** The blank should finish the contrapositive: mesons near the speed of light decay more slowly than mesons nearly at rest. Expect the wrong answers to explain a shortage of detections, when the real puzzle is a surplus.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Wrong Direction.** A longer trip means more time to decay, so fewer mesons would arrive. The puzzle is that more arrive than the lab rate predicts. This makes it worse.

**(B) Wrong Direction.** If the mesons were hard to detect, we'd count fewer. We already count about a hundred times more than the lab rate allows, so detection trouble can't be what the evidence points to.

**(C) Wrong Direction.** Fewer mesons out there would mean even fewer detected. The stimulus is built to explain why we see so many, and nothing in it is about how many are generated.

**(D) Correct.** This is the contrapositive put into words. If fast mesons decayed at the lab rate, we'd detect about 1/100 of what we do. We detect far more, so they don't decay at the lab rate. "Decay more slowly than mu mesons almost at rest" is that conclusion, framed by the speed difference the stimulus sets up. The move to keep: when a stimulus states a conditional and then tells you its result didn't happen, the answer is usually the negated trigger.

**(E) Contradicts Stimulus.** The first sentence says these mesons are "generated by cosmic rays." Nothing later questions that. The evidence is about decay, not origin.

## PT157-S3-Q21 | Must be True | E
explanation_id: acf5a37a-c0a7-4910-8f91-92c7d1fddb64

### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** "Moral excellence can be achieved only by repeatedly overcoming inclinations to do the wrong thing." Excellence requires overcoming:

Moral excellence → repeatedly overcame inclinations to do wrong

Overcoming those inclinations is often difficult, even for a morally virtuous person.

"The only way to become a morally virtuous person is through the achievement of moral excellence." Virtue requires excellence:

Morally virtuous → moral excellence

**Evaluate:** Two rules with a shared term, so ask the first conditional question: can we chain them? Yes. Virtue requires excellence, and excellence requires overcoming.

Morally virtuous → moral excellence → repeatedly overcame inclinations to do wrong

Now pause and infer one more step. Can you overcome an inclination you never had? No. So every morally virtuous person has had inclinations to do the wrong thing.

Contrapositive, for the traps: no overcoming → no excellence → no virtue.

**Goal:** Expect an answer that runs from virtue to the far end of the chain: every morally virtuous person has repeatedly overcome wrong inclinations, or has had them. Expect traps that reverse the arrows or negate both sides.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Contradicts Stimulus.** The stimulus says overcoming wrong inclinations is often difficult "even for a morally virtuous person." Virtuous people have to resist, which means acting wrongly is still an option for them. "Incapable" has no support.

**(B) Too Strong.** We know every virtuous person has achieved moral excellence. That's an arrow from virtue to excellence. It says nothing about what share of morally excellent people are virtuous. Excellence could be common and virtue rare.

**(C) Contradicts Stimulus.** Someone with no inclination to do wrong has nothing to overcome. Excellence requires repeatedly overcoming those inclinations, so this person can't have achieved moral excellence. The answer says the opposite.

**(D) Reversal.** This is the most tempting answer. It sounds like "the only way to become virtuous is through excellence." But that rule is virtue → excellence. D says not virtuous → not excellent, which is the same as excellence → virtue. That's the arrow reversed. The stimulus never says excellence is enough for virtue, so a person could achieve excellence and still not be virtuous.

**(E) Correct.** Chain the two rules: virtue requires excellence, excellence requires repeatedly overcoming inclinations to do wrong. You can't overcome an inclination you never had, so every morally virtuous person has been inclined to do something wrong. This is the move to repeat: chain the rules, then read the far right side in plain English and ask what it presupposes.

## PT157-S3-Q23 | Most Supported | E

### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** Superstring theory "purports, unlike more established physical theories, to explain the nature and existence of gravity."

Problem one: testing it would take a particle accelerator 100 trillion kilometers long.

Problem two: it "has had no success in adequately explaining why the force of gravity is not stronger or weaker than it is."

**Evaluate:** No conditionals and no quantities to chain, so look at what the author treats as a judgment. The stimulus doesn't just report that superstring theory hasn't explained gravity's strength. It calls that a "problem."

Why would that be a problem? Because the theory claims to explain gravity. A theory that promises to explain a force and can't say why the force is as strong as it is falls short of its own promise. That's the value judgment hiding in the word "problem."

Notice what the stimulus does not give us. It says nothing about how well established theories explain anything, and the "unlike" clause tells us those theories don't even claim to explain gravity's nature and existence.

**Goal:** Expect a general principle that the superstring case illustrates: a theory that claims to explain a force has a shortcoming if it can't account for that force's strength. Expect traps that compare superstring theory with the established theories, or that turn "a problem" into "can't be true."

### Choice Notes

**(A) Unsupported Assumption.** "No success" describes the result, not the effort. Superstring theorists may have tried hard and failed. A assumes they haven't attempted it, and nothing says trying would bring success.

**(B) Out of Scope.** The only contrast with established theories is what each claims to explain. The stimulus never rates how well established theories explain physical phenomena, so "better explanations" has nothing behind it.

**(C) Unsupported.** This is the most tempting wrong answer, because it reads "unlike" as "the others did better." But "unlike more established physical theories" only says those theories don't purport to explain the nature and existence of gravity. It tells us nothing about whether any of them has had success with gravity's strength. To pick C, you'd need a fact the stimulus never gives.

**(D) Too Strong.** The giant accelerator is a "problem" for testing the theory. Whether a theory can be tested and whether it is true are different questions. A theory could be true and very hard to test. "Cannot be true" is far beyond anything said.

**(E) Correct.** Superstring theory claims to explain gravity, and its failure to explain gravity's strength is called a "problem." E generalizes exactly that: a theory that purports to explain the nature of a force is "deficient" if it can't account for the strength of that force. "Deficient" is a fair paraphrase of "has a problem." The move to keep: when a stimulus labels something a problem or a flaw, the author has made a value judgment, and a Most Supported answer can state the principle behind it.

### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** Compound screens are built from small tiles joined seamlessly, each tile its own miniature screen. Sets with compound screens are just a few inches thick.

For a noncompound screen in a set that thin, the larger the screen, the dimmer it is.

However, each tile in a compound screen is small enough to be quite bright. And an unlimited number of tiles can be joined "without making the resulting screen any less bright or the set any thicker."

That's the inference. A thin set can now have a screen that is both very large and bright.

**Goal:** Expect an answer that says thin sets (a few inches) can now have screens that are bright and very large. Watch for answers that add claims about demand, about every possible disadvantage, or about how thickness changes brightness, because the report covers none of those.

**(A) Unsupported.** The report praises what compound screens can do at large sizes. It never says they're a poor fit for small screens. A few bright tiles would make a small bright screen.

**(C) Unsupported.** This is the most tempting wrong answer, because it uses the noncompound comparison. So a set just a few inches thick can have a screen that is both bright and very large. Every piece of D traces back to a sentence in the report. ### Argument Breakdown

**Opposing Point:** Economists make two claims. First, part-time workers' higher turnover shows they are much more likely than full-time workers to be dissatisfied with their jobs. Second, lower-paid part-time workers threaten to take jobs from full-time employees.

**Evidence:** "But" turns against the economists. Job efficiency is positively correlated with job satisfaction, so companies are unlikely to replace satisfied employees with dissatisfied ones.

**Evaluate:** Cerrato uses the economists' first claim against their second. Line them up.

If part-timers are the dissatisfied ones, then putting a part-timer in a full-timer's place means swapping a (more likely) satisfied worker for a dissatisfied one. Cerrato says companies are unlikely to make that swap, because dissatisfied workers tend to be less efficient. So the threat in the second claim shouldn't materialize, at least for the dissatisfied part-timers.

The "Therefore" blank needs the conclusion of that reasoning. It's a Most Supported completion, so expect it to be scoped to what the evidence covers: dissatisfied workers, and an "unlikely" rather than a "never."

**Goal:** Predict: dissatisfied part-time workers are unlikely to take the jobs of (satisfied) full-time workers. In other words, the economists' threat claim doesn't fit their own dissatisfaction claim.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Opposite.** This repeats the economists' threat claim, which Cerrato's "But" is set up to reject. The evidence points the other way: companies don't swap satisfied workers for dissatisfied ones.

**(B) Out of Scope.** Profits and wage levels across companies never come up. Cerrato's evidence is about satisfaction, efficiency and replacement.

**(C) Correct.** Companies are unlikely to replace satisfied employees with dissatisfied ones. Part-timers are, by the economists' own claim, the likely dissatisfied group, and full-timers the more satisfied one. So dissatisfied part-timers are unlikely to take full-timers' jobs. Notice how carefully C is scoped. It talks about "dissatisfied" part-time workers, so it doesn't need every part-timer to be unhappy, and it says "unlikely," matching Cerrato's "unlikely." **(E) Unsupported.** When companies choose to hire part-timers is never discussed. Cerrato's evidence is only about whether they would replace satisfied employees with dissatisfied ones, and "only when" is a strong claim with nothing behind it.

## PT158-S2-Q6 | Most Supported | A

### Argument Breakdown

**Statements:** Dark honey "tends to" have more antioxidants than light-colored honey.

The most healthful strains are all unusually high in antioxidants:

Among the most healthful strains → unusually high in antioxidants

"However," certain strains produced by bees harvesting primarily sage nectar are among the most healthful strains, even though they are among the lightest-colored.

**Evaluate:** Two of these sentences command attention. The middle sentence is a rule with "all." The last sentence is a fact that triggers it: certain sage-nectar strains are among the most healthful.

Apply the rule to the fact. Those sage-nectar strains are among the most healthful, so they are unusually high in antioxidants.

What about the "tends to" sentence? It's a general trend, and the sage honey is an exception to it: light-colored, yet high in antioxidants. A trend allows exceptions, so there's no contradiction. It just isn't the sentence that drives the inference.

**Goal:** Expect: certain (some) sage-nectar honey strains are unusually high in antioxidants. Watch for answers that turn the exception into a new trend, or that jump from "among the most" to "the most."

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Certain sage-nectar strains are among the most healthful. All of the most healthful strains are unusually high in antioxidants. So some sage-nectar strains are unusually high in antioxidants. "Some" matches "certain strains," and the answer doesn't overclaim about all sage honey. The move to keep: when a stimulus gives you an "all" rule and a fact that meets its trigger, apply the rule and look for the result in the answers.

**(B) Out of Scope.** The stimulus talks about one nectar source, sage. It says nothing about what most plants produce.

**(C) Hasty Generalization.** A few light-colored sage strains are among the most healthful. That's an exception, flagged by "However." It doesn't show light honey in general tends to be more healthful than dark. The only trend we're given runs the other way for antioxidants.

**(D) Contradicts Stimulus.** Those sage strains are among the most healthful, and every one of the most healthful strains is unusually high in antioxidants. So they are unusually high, not low. D is what you'd guess from color alone, which the rule overrides.

**(E) Too Strong.** This is the most tempting wrong answer, because the light sage honey really is high in antioxidants. But "among the most healthful" and "unusually high" don't make it the single highest. A dark honey could easily top the list, and the "tends to" sentence even leans that way. Nothing supports the superlative.
