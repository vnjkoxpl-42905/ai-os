## PT120-S4-Q17 | Parallel Flaw | A
explanation_id: c8f0c599-a68f-44d8-b43c-61ed3d475627

### Argument Breakdown

**Conclusion:** No one has ever pushed Alex to do more than what comes easily.

**Evidence:** People who have never been asked to do more than they can easily do never do all they can. Alex clearly has not done all that Alex is capable of.

**Evaluate:** Diagram the rule first, because everything turns on its direction.

Never pushed → doesn't do all they can

The fact we get about Alex lands on the right side: Alex doesn't do all Alex can. The argument then runs the arrow backward and concludes the left side. That's the reversal (mistaking a necessary condition for a sufficient one).

Why doesn't it work? The rule says every never-pushed person underperforms. It doesn't say every underperformer was never pushed. Alex might have been pushed hard and still coasted because of illness, boredom or a bad year. The rule allows all of that.

**Goal:** Find the same three pieces with the same mistake:

If A, then B.
X is B.
So X is A.

The topic and wording don't matter. Check each answer for a conditional, a fact that matches its right side, and a conclusion that claims its left side.

### Choice Notes

**(A) Correct.** Diagram it:

Has a dog → knows the true value of companionship

Alicia knows the true value of companionship, so the answer concludes Alicia has a dog. That's the fact on the right side and the conclusion on the left, the exact backward move Alex's argument makes. And it fails for the same reason: Alicia could have learned about companionship from friends or family without owning any dog. The transferable move: diagram the rule, then ask which side the fact lands on and which side the conclusion claims. Fact right, conclusion left is the reversal.

**(B) Wrong Flaw.** Discovers something new → has examined all the possible solutions. Fran has never discovered something new, so the answer concludes Fran has never explored all the possible solutions. The fact negates the left side and the conclusion negates the right side. That's a flaw too (the negation flaw), but it's a different one. Alex's argument starts from the right side; this one starts from a denied left side.

**(C) Valid.** Doesn't face sufficient challenges → doesn't accomplish everything they can. Jill does accomplish everything Jill can, which denies the right side. Flip the arrow into its contrapositive and you get exactly the conclusion: Jill faces sufficient challenges. This is good reasoning, so it can't match a flawed argument. Notice how close its topic is to the original. A copied subject is not a copied structure.

**(D) Valid.** "By definition" matters here. A definition runs both ways: anything that is a closed plane figure bounded by straight lines is a polygon. The object on the chalkboard fits that description, so it must be a polygon. No flaw to match.

**(E) Different Structure.** This is the closest look-alike, because it also seems to move from the right side of a rule back to the left. Look at the details. The rule's right side is being lax about keeping property secure, but Jon is only lax about property Jon can afford to lose, so the fact doesn't even match the right side. The rule's left side is never having lost something one cannot afford to lose, but the conclusion jumps to never having lost anything at all. The answer adds two mismatches that the original doesn't have. A shows the reversal cleanly with nothing extra.
