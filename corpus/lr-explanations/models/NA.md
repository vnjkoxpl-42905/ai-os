## 2. What the best ones do

- **Decide link or defender out loud, then commit.** The Evaluate says which kind of argument this is. Abstract terms and conditionals point to a link; real-world, causal, plan and prediction arguments point to a defender. Then it builds the matching thing: a diagram (PT150-S2-Q12, PT148-S1-Q25) or "Given that ... how could we still argue that ...?" objections (PT153-S3-Q15, PT143-S3-Q25). Step labels stay Conclusion > Evidence > (Intermediate Conclusion) > Evaluate > Goal.
- **The Goal lists candidate answers, not the task.** The best Goals list two to four answers in the stimulus's own words, and one of them is the key or close to it: "the helmet is not more properly part of the heritage of the Italian people" (PT154-S4-Q4); "the drop in participation ... would not be the same across all demographic groups" (PT150-S3-Q7); "changing personal behavior would not lead to the government changing its policies" (PT146-S2-Q23).
- **Negation is written out as a sentence, then judged.** The pattern: "If we negate this ... [negated sentence in quotes, often 'Yo, author --' or 'Hey, author --']. Does that weaken? Yes, because ..." The note spells out the negation of quantifiers: some becomes none (PT127-S1-Q13), at least one becomes none (PT145-S4-Q26), a double negative becomes all (PT152-S2-Q23).
- **Rank the negations when two answers both hurt.** PT127-S1-Q13 (A vs C), PT148-S1-Q5 (A vs B), PT113-S3-Q14 (E vs B) and PT146-S2-Q23 (A vs C) all say the same thing: the correct answer is the one whose negation hurts most, and it does not need to destroy the argument (PT140-S3-Q19, PT127-S1-Q19).
- **Worked numbers and analogies carry the hard gaps.** Examples: gene 80 to 85 (PT122-S1-Q10), 2 s / 1 s / 4 s (PT143-S3-Q19), 35/35/30 (PT150-S3-Q7), a 0.5% rising chance (PT140-S2-Q6), $50,000 lotto (PT122-S1-Q10), Tom and the traveling team (PT143-S3-Q25).
- **Conditional answers get a diagram and a contrapositive, not a negation.** "When correct answers are conditional, they're usually written in contrapositive form" (PT148-S1-Q5, PT150-S2-Q12, PT147-S1-Q7). "Putting the Conclusion on the left side of the arrow is always wrong" (PT148-S1-Q25).
- **Rules for strength, stated once.** "If an answer is phrased very strongly, then negating it always gives a very weak idea" (PT146-S2-Q23). "Most" is almost always wrong unless the conclusion is about "most" (PT140-S2-Q6, PT154-S4-Q4). "Some" is lovably weak (PT152-S2-Q23).
- **Named traps with a one-line example.** Illegal opposite or "Flip a Fact" ("tall children love their moms" does not give "short children don't", PT119-S2-Q10). Only Thing Mentioned is not Only Thing (earrings, PT145-S4-Q26). Premise booster (PT118-S3-Q19). Inference instead of assumption (PT148-S1-Q17, A). Attacking a concession (PT147-S1-Q7, B).
- **Phrases worth reusing:** "the lovable ruling-out 'not / no'", "Whenever we're doing Necessary Assumption and we see an answer ruling out an idea with 'not', we slow down and negate it", "Which answer, when negated, most weakens", "a thought the author must have been having in order to arrive at her conclusion", "authors don't assume things that hurt their arguments".

## 3. What the weaker candidates got wrong

- **The author disowns the key or the question.** PT144-S4-Q20 calls the correct answer "very flawed" and "technically written wrong". PT109-S3-Q25 ("a surprising correct answer to me ... If I were hating on this answer") and PT152-S4-Q13 ("this answer seems to bother me") undercut the key. PT109-S4-Q16 calls it "a weird question".
- **The Goal predicts nothing, or the wrong gap.** PT144-S4-Q20 ("does not lend itself to an obvious prephrase"), PT143-S1-Q3 ("go to the answers a little fuzzy"), and PT139-S1-Q19 give generic shapes only. PT109-S4-Q16's Goal names a different assumption from the key.
- **Speculation from outside or from the author's own life.** PT147-S4-Q21 (natural selection vs a supernatural designer), PT119-S3-Q15 (the author's Twitter use; modern literature "probably more respectful of women and minorities"), PT119-S2-Q10 (asides on how residency really works; labeled as not needed, but they take space).
- **Politically loaded examples in a teaching brand.** PT145-S2-Q24 (gun control, abortion, party voters), plus a political slogan as the illegal-opposite example in PT145-S2-Q24 C, PT143-S1-Q3 C and PT127-S1-Q13 D. The logic is fine; the examples should be neutral (PT143-S1-Q3's blue and red M&Ms already is).
- **Thin notes on the top trap, or pasted openings.** PT104-S1-Q21 D (21%) gets two sentences. PT127-S1-Q19 A and B (21% and 9%) share one pasted diagram. PT151-S4-Q4 opens A, B and C with the same sentence. PT142-S4-Q4 A to C share a template. PT105-S2-Q20 gives the correct answer of an EXCEPT one line.
