### What the best MBT/MSS explanations do

- **Scan for the indicator, then ask the two conditional questions out loud.** "Can I chain it? Can I apply it to a fact (usually via the contrapositive)?" appears almost word for word in PT103-S1-Q19 and PT155-S1-Q15, and it drives both the Evaluate and the Goal. When there is a Most claim and a conditional, the best ones say which one commands attention and why (PT145-S2-Q23: a lone Most is inert; PT122-S4-Q19).
- **Rewrite rules into "X requires Y" before chaining.** PT155-S1-Q15, PT140-S3-Q17 and PT148-S3-Q17 turn "unless", "only when", "essential", "cannot until" into requirements, so the chain reads left to right and the contrapositive is easy to state in plain English.
- **The Goal names the answer's content and its strength.** Strong: "look for something like 'the symptoms relate to collagen'" (PT153-S2-Q5); "Pulham has to be higher than 38%" (PT155-S4-Q21); "an answer expressing this stalemate" (PT148-S3-Q17). The better ones also predict where the traps will go ("expect illegal negations and reversals", PT140-S3-Q17; the list of overconfident directions in PT142-S4-Q11).
- **Pivot endings get a named move and a worked mini-example.** "Reconcile the Pivot" with the Tom/Yankees example (PT142-S4-Q11), and the rebuttal reading plus "Flip the Causal Difference-Maker" (PT151-S3-Q14). Both warn that the right answer will be watered down.
- **Quantity inferences are taught with head counts, not only symbols.** PT148-S1-Q22 (15 ballet lovers, minimum 17 cake lovers), PT145-S2-Q23 (100 Velyena mines vs 11 gold mines), PT155-S4-Q21 (100 + 100 students, 47%). The rule is stated so it can be memorized ("Most A are B, Most B are not A: more B than A"), then the numbers make it believable.
- **Weak wording is argued for, not just noticed.** The best correct notes say how little evidence the answer needs: "no guarantee" needs one data point (PT102-S4-Q2, the 175/UCLA example); "sometimes" needs one case (PT151-S3-Q14, PT108-S2-Q17); "can be altered" (PT107-S1-Q6).
- **Recurring trap names, each with a one-line test.** One-claim answer ("feels like the first sentence on steroids", PT148-S3-Q17; PT147-S4-Q23 C); relative vs absolute (PT107-S1-Q6 A, PT140-S3-Q17 B); only mentioned ≠ only (PT103-S1-Q19); unknown Most group ("never conclude Most X without a Most X premise", PT145-S2-Q23); untriggered rule ("we don't have any knowledge triggered by...", PT155-S1-Q15, PT149-S4-Q24).
- **The popular trap gets the longest note and a counterexample.** PT122-S4-Q19 B (30%), PT140-S3-Q17 A and B (21% and 23%), PT146-S2-Q17 C (29%), PT148-S1-Q22 C (19%) each build a concrete scenario where the premises are true and the trap is false.
- **When the key is not strictly provable, they say so and say why it still wins.** PT155-S1-Q15 (allowed outside vs outside), PT107-S1-Q6 (volume dial is not causal, but the stimulus supports it), PT142-S4-Q7 (correlation to "reasonable to conclude"). That honesty is part of teaching Most Supported.
- **Reusable phrases:** "pause and infer"; "X requires Y"; "remove the overlap and join the one-offs"; "we don't have any knowledge triggered by X"; "if it said ___, it would be correct" (PT149-S4-Q24 E); "this is the first sentence on steroids".

### What the weaker MBT candidates got wrong

- **Goal hands off to the answers.** PT149-S3-Q21 ("re-read the paragraph ... then use the answers to guide us"), PT146-S2-Q17 and PT147-S4-Q23 admit they cannot predict. Acceptable when the stimulus is a scatter, but it costs P every time.
- **Pasted notes instead of question-specific reasons.** PT154-S2-Q22 repeats the same Most rule in B, C and E, and C borrows B's closing sentence, which does not address oil paintings. PT127-S1-Q17 gives D (22%) and E the same one-line note. PT107-S4-Q23 pastes the one-claim paragraph into B and C.
- **Unsupported test-taking folklore.** PT149-S3-Q21 and PT107-S1-Q21 both claim the tempting wrong answer is deliberately placed in slot (A). That is not a reasoning move and should not be copied.
- **Diagrams flattened into unreadable lines.** PT111-S3-Q18 and PT108-S3-Q23 are strong underneath, but the conditional chains now read as word salad ("inspired → good → sophistic → underst performances show listeners m. roots").
- **The popular trap gets the shortest note.** PT127-S1-Q17 D (22%): one line pasted from E. PT145-S4-Q13: A, B, C are one line each and E defers to "the explanation for (C)".

### What the best MBF explanations do

- **Open Evaluate with the two-pattern rule, then look for each.** "The correct answer almost always contradicts a conditional or contradicts an inference we can make by combining claims" (PT127-S1-Q25, PT113-S3-Q15, PT147-S1-Q20). Then they actually derive the inference or state the contradiction shape, rather than stopping at the rule.
- **Define a contradiction precisely and separate it from a bad inference.** "Trigger, but don't deliver" (PT153-S3-Q16); "a contradiction provides the trigger but denies the outcome; reversals and negations are illegal but not contradictions" (PT115-S2-Q24); "Bad Inference ≠ contradiction" (PT124-S2-Q10). This is the single most important transfer line for the family.
- **Every wrong-answer note runs the same test: what would we need to have read to contradict this?** PT110-S3-Q22, PT121-S4-Q18, PT148-S1-Q10 and PT122-S2-Q21 phrase it as "To contradict this, we would need to have read that ___. We never heard that." PT113-S3-Q15 and PT117-S4-Q18 phrase it as "Can I prove the opposite?" Either way, it replaces verdict-only "out of scope" notes.
- **Teach the strength asymmetry.** Weak claims ("some", "commonly", "could") are hard to contradict; strong claims ("no", "never", "invariably", "no techniques") fall to one counterexample. PT110-S3-Q22 states it, PT149-S3-Q12 and PT148-S1-Q10 apply it to soft stems, PT122-S2-Q21 applies it to a causal passage.
- **Predict the disguise.** PT153-S3-Q16 predicts filler around the trigger ("hates pasta"); PT147-S1-Q20 says D matches the prediction "but they disguised it well"; PT142-S4-Q23 predicts two requirements in tension before reading the choices.
- **Locate answers by position on a chain.** PT115-S2-Q24: "something is the 1st idea but not the 3rd" contradicts; "the 4th but not the 3rd" does not. That turns five choices into one quick check.
- **Run numbers on quantified "or" and Most claims.** PT127-S1-Q25 (30% + 30% non-overlapping; 49% + 2%) and PT113-S3-Q15 ($1 million at 20% vs 13%) make the math visible, and PT127-S1-Q25's NFL/Mars example shows why a Most "or" claim can be satisfied by one side alone.
- **Tell the student what one word would make a wrong answer right.** PT153-S3-Q16 B ("if it said some popular novelists"), PT105-S4-Q21 A. This shows exactly where the line is.
- **Reusable phrases:** "trigger, but don't deliver"; "can't prove it ≠ can disprove it" (PT102-S4-Q15); "we can't contradict something we never talked about"; "to contradict this, we would need to know ___"; "could I infer that the opposite must be true?".

### What the weaker MBF candidates got wrong

- **Verdict-only notes, especially for "out of scope" answers.** PT134-S2-Q18 (notes of one sentence each), PT129-S2-Q23, PT102-S4-Q15 (A to D are one or two lines). An out-of-scope note is only useful when it says what contradiction would have been needed.
- **Pasted notes.** PT152-S2-Q24 D and E are identical (and both wrong, below); PT122-S2-Q16 B, C, E share one note; PT105-S4-Q21 D and E share one note; PT108-S3-Q18 opens A, B, C, E with the same boilerplate.
- **Slips that invert the logic of a note.** PT152-S2-Q24 says "we can contradict this" where it means "can't"; PT116-S3-Q17's Goal lists a wrong second contradiction pattern; PT122-S4-Q5's Evaluate treats a Most claim as All. In a family built on the difference between can't-prove and can-disprove, these are serious.
- **Popular trap under-served.** PT102-S4-Q16 B (39%) gets two sentences; PT147-S1-Q20 E (17%) and PT102-S4-Q26 A (16%) get one line.
- **Different register.** PT112-S4-Q7 uses heavy em-dashes and a voice unlike the rest of the corpus ("i.e., which answer is ruled out"); accurate, but not a style model.
