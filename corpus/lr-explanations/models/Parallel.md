### 2. What the best ones do

- **Decide recipe or slogan first, and say why.** Conditional, quantifier and repeated terms mean diagram; one premise or no formal language means state the moral (PT146-S1-Q23 declines to diagram despite "only" and "never"; PT108-S2-Q16 gives both).
- **Write the prediction as a numbered ingredient list: number of premises, claim type (conditional, most, some, fact), conclusion type and strength** (PT151-S2-Q19, PT144-S4-Q22). Then name which ingredients are essential and which are only filters (PT151-S2-Q19 on the AND outcome).
- **Cut on the conclusion first, by type and strength.** "Can't avoid" vs. "can't rule out" (PT151-S2-Q19 D); "never" and "cannot" vs. "unlikely" (PT150-S3-Q23 A, C); possible or certain vs. probable (PT112-S4-Q9 A, E).
- **Run one test on every choice and write out what a matching answer would have said.** PT149-S3-Q23 writes the proper conclusion for each choice before showing the actual one; PT106-S3-Q26 rewrites trap A into its matching form.
- **Put the original and the choice side by side.** Tables (PT149-S1-Q25) or a running tally of wrong moves (PT152-S1-Q23: denies trigger, confirms trigger, confirms outcome) make the break visible without prose.
- **Give the popular trap the longest note and say what it gets right before what breaks.** PT111-S4-Q23 A (34%, within one person vs. across people); PT117-S3-Q16 D (22%); PT152-S2-Q19 C (28%, Mars example).
- **Say out loud when the key is imperfect and which mismatch doesn't matter.** PT117-S3-Q16 (some vs. many is logically irrelevant); PT106-S3-Q26 (weak conclusion match, best gist); PT150-S3-Q23 (three premises packed into one).
- **Teach the translation rule the question depends on.** "Unless" into the trigger (PT112-S4-Q9); negative to positive quantifiers (PT152-S2-Q19); a recommendation is an implicit comparison (PT117-S4-Q21).
- **Flag surface bait as a signal.** Same topic or copied wording is usually the trap: PT102-S2-Q22 C and D (government subsidy topic), PT151-S2-Q19 A ("the only conclusion one can draw").

### 2. What the best ones do

- **Name the flaw first, then pick the tool.** For conditional or quantified stimuli, say the recipe alone can carry you even if the flaw is fuzzy (PT153-S3-Q11, PT122-S4-Q24). For story flaws, write the objection as the prediction: "couldn't both be wrong?" (PT117-S2-Q22), "does Katelin even know that?" (PT153-S2-Q25).
- **Prove the flaw with small made-up numbers or a familiar example.** Dorm sizes (PT148-S1-Q24), a 100-person breakdown (PT142-S4-Q25), major sizes (PT146-S3-Q25), Clark Kent (PT153-S2-Q25), student-loan-to-mortgage (PT143-S3-Q14).
- **Sort wrong answers into three kinds: valid, different flaw, or right flaw with the wrong structure.** PT107-S4-Q21 (A, D, E valid; B wrong conclusion); PT124-S2-Q8 (each wrong answer gets its own named flaw); PT155-S1-Q8 (A and D are the legal contrapositive plus a different slip).
- **Rank flaw match over structure match, and say so.** PT155-S1-Q8 B ("replicating the flaw is the most important part"); PT142-S1-Q25 B (negation vs. reversal: same family, weaker match).
- **Test the popular trap against the objection, not just the recipe.** PT116-S3-Q22 E (timeline blocks reverse causation); PT117-S2-Q22 A ("at least one wrong" already allows both); PT149-S4-Q25 E ("only viable candidates" closes the gap).
