### What the best ones do

- **They predict the rule and put the conclusion's judgment on the right side.** They write "If [evidence fact], then [conclusion judgment]", often at two levels of abstraction (PT146-S1-Q1, PT155-S4-Q9). When there are several premises, they predict one rule per premise (PT150-S3-Q14 does this too; PT138-S3-Q12).
- **They filter by the outcome first, then check the trigger.** Is the conclusion's language on the right side? If not, the choice is out. If it is, does the stimulus establish the trigger? (PT154-S2-Q8, PT144-S2-Q23). Their stated rule: "flexible on the trigger, stubborn on the outcome" (PT154-S2-Q8, PT101-S2-Q9).
- **When they see pros and cons, they switch to weighing tradeoffs.** They name the upside and the downside, then predict "[the side the author picks] matters more than [the other]". They also explain why an answer with no "should" in it can still win (PT107-S4-Q7, PT108-S2-Q24, PT147-S1-Q14, golden PT138-S3-Q1).
- **They translate conditional wording before judging it.** "Only if" and "only" go on the right; "unless" means "if not"; "if and only if" is a biconditional; negating an AND gives an OR. Then they show that the correct answer is often written in contrapositive form to hide it (PT103-S2-Q23, PT150-S2-Q5, PT144-S3-Q23, PT138-S3-Q12).
- **They give the most-picked trap the longest note and say why it tempts.** Examples: PT144-S3-Q23 B at 30% (affected is not opposed; many years is not never), PT137-S3-Q9 D at 22% (a confiscation rule is not an admissibility rule), PT138-S3-Q12 D at 24% (relative vs absolute), PT144-S2-Q23 D at 36% (relevance is not justification).
- **When the principle is in the stimulus, they say the application already concludes the rule's right side, so the job is to establish the missing trigger** (PT137-S4-Q8, golden PT138-S2-Q5, PT103-S2-Q23).
- **They check what kind of conclusion is being proved.** A consistency claim (PT112-S3-Q20), a comparison (PT151-S3-Q6), a conditional conclusion (PT108-S2-Q24) or an intermediate conclusion (PT155-S4-Q9) each shape what the rule must output.
- **They use one concrete example that carries the logic.** Examples: the Al Capone diary handed over by his daughter (PT137-S3-Q9), frozen broccoli (PT138-S3-Q12), the fisherman scene (PT155-S1-Q16), "Eric is a jerk" (PT108-S2-Q24).
- **They are honest when the key is imperfect.** They say what is off and why it still beats the others (PT148-S1-Q4, PT153-S3-Q2 "only wins because all the other answers lose", PT148-S4-Q8 "affirms a Necessary Assumption").

- **Incomplete notes.** PT150-S2-Q16's E note only diagrams the argument and never tests E. PT153-S3-Q2's E note stops after one sentence.
- **Popular traps under-served.** In PT113-S2-Q22, A drew 18% and C drew 28%, but each gets the same short "wrong topic" treatment as the 3% answers. In PT148-S4-Q8, the note calls D "by far the most attractive" at 6% while C at 16% gets a brief note.

- **Choice notes that describe the wrong choice** (see fidelity, PT101-S2-Q25).

### What the best ones do

- **When the principle is in the stimulus, they diagram it with the judgment on the right, then sort answers by conclusion direction.** Any answer concluding something the rule cannot output is gone unread. Only after that do they check the trigger (PT115-S2-Q11, PT152-S2-Q21, PT139-S4-Q6).
- **They turn a multi-part trigger into a checklist and tick it for every answer.** The note names which item each wrong answer is missing (PT140-S1-Q25, PT139-S4-Q6, PT126-S4-Q9).
- **They get the negation right.** Negating "Y and Z" gives "~Y or ~Z"; "not X unless Y or Z" needs both missing. They also explain the payoff: with two rules, the OR rule is usually the one tested (PT115-S2-Q11, PT139-S4-Q6, PT152-S2-Q21).
- **When the principle is implicit, they abstract it to a topic-free slogan before reading the choices.** Then they name the exact relationship each trap swaps: before vs after, cause vs similarity, parent vs cousin, "even if" vs "as long as" (PT118-S3-Q18, PT121-S1-Q4, PT144-S2-Q26, PT153-S3-Q25).
- **They rewrite the popular trap into the version that would have been right.** This shows the student exactly which element broke (PT153-S3-Q25 A; PT103-S3-Q25 C).
- **They say what the stem wording changes.** "Conforms" or "underlies" is not "if valid, justify", so an answer that would prove the conclusion can still be wrong (PT101-S2-Q22 A). "Violate" and "conflict" mean the trigger applies and the outcome is defied (PT120-S1-Q7, PT142-S4-Q21). "Cannot always be followed" asks for a case where compliance is impossible (PT120-S1-Q24).
- **For analogies, they test each principle against both halves** (PT148-S4-Q6).
- **They use a worked example where a meaning match is the hard step.** Examples: the wolf timeline (PT135-S4-Q23), $10M plus $2M (PT144-S2-Q26), the water gun (PT120-S1-Q24).
- **They catch quantifier and scope slips inside otherwise good answers.** Examples: one person vs people generally, many vs most, "those who feel most strongly" vs "everyone" (PT126-S1-Q21); "never" vs "balance" (PT102-S2-Q24).

### What the weaker candidates got wrong

- **Wrong type for the family.** Several shortlisted slots are really Flaw (PT134-S2-Q23), Most Supported (PT159-S3-Q14, PT136-S2-Q9), principle-Weaken (PT149-S4-Q17) or principle-Paradox (PT142-S4-Q12). They are not usable as Conform models.
- **Pasted openings with no test.** PT109-S1-Q18 repeats one paragraph under B, C and E. PT149-S4-Q17 pastes A, B and E. PT130-S1-Q21 gives A and B the same one line.
- **The popular trap gets a one-liner.** PT135-S4-Q23 A (28%): "If conclusion, then premise." PT139-S1-Q18 C (22%): one sentence. PT152-S4-Q8 C (11%): "This principle nicely justifies the government's action."
- **Damaged or distracting text.** "- paragraph" artifacts appear in PT119-S3-Q8 and PT117-S2-Q12. PT105-S4-Q24 has dropped spaces and a missing Goal header.
- **Outside or charged examples.** PT105-S4-Q24 uses a real-world Israel/UN example. PT121-S1-Q4 cites drone strikes under a named president. PT136-S2-Q9 argues from what dreams are "often" like.
