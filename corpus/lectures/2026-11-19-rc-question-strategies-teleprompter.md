# RC Question Strategies
### Teleprompter script. Read straight down. Lines in [brackets] are stage directions.
### Runs about 45 minutes. Uses the Gilliam passage from the last lesson as the example.

---

## PART 1. The one rule above every question type (4 min)

Before any strategy, one rule.

Read the whole question stem. Translate it.
Every time.

Here's why I'm so stubborn about this.

In LR, the question type tells you almost everything.
"It's a Strengthen." Great. You know the job.

RC doesn't work that way.
The type is just the umbrella.
Underneath it, the stem gives you extra information that narrows what the right answer can be.

Listen to this stem.

"The author would be most likely to regard a policy..."

A lot of people stop there.
"Author agreement. Got it."

But keep reading.

"...a policy in which all forest fires started by lightning were allowed to burn until they died out naturally, as..."

Now that's a different question.
It's not "what does the author believe?"
It's "what would the author think of this one specific policy?"

If you read half the stem, there's usually an answer that's true about the passage.
And it's wrong. Because it doesn't answer the real question.

[Slow down.]

For students missing only one or two on RC, about half of those misses come from misreading the stem.
That's the last mistake standing between a great score and a perfect one.

So, the process for every question, same as last time:

One. Translate the stem. All of it.
Two. Make your Mission. Answer it yourself, from your translation.
Three. Test each whole answer against the question you actually translated.

And one more thing about the Mission.
It is not a prediction you're trying to match.
It's a qualifying round.
It proves you can answer the question, so you've earned the right to judge other people's answers.

Now. The question families.

## PART 2. Main Point (5 min)

One question. Only one.

"What did the author want me to take away from this passage?"

That's it. Ask that, and don't do anything else.

What Main Point is not:

It's not a summary of everything.
It's not a little piece of every paragraph.
It's not more likely to be in the first paragraph or the last.
And it's not guaranteed to be written out anywhere.

All those shortcuts are what make Main Point hard.
"This was in paragraph three, so it's good."
Drop them. You already have a test that works every time.

Can't get comfortable with "takeaway"? Try this.

The author has ten seconds in an elevator.
Doors open in the lobby.
What do they say?
That's the Main Point.

Two rules.

If the author has an opinion, it belongs in the Main Point.
They wrote this so you'd hold that opinion.

And watch out for premises.
In RC, the Main Point works more like an LR conclusion than a premise.
The building blocks aren't the takeaway. The result of the building is.

Make your Main Point at the end of every passage.
On comparatives, do it for Passage A before you start Passage B.

[Pause.]

Stuck between two answers? Use the Conclusion Test.

"Because answer one, we know that answer two."
Then flip it.
"Because answer two, we know that answer one."

Whichever one sounds right in the "we know that" spot is probably your Main Point.
The other one is acting like a premise.

**Gilliam, Question 1.** "The author is primarily concerned with..."

Ten-second pitch: "Here's why Gilliam made the art he made, and what it was."
A says the motivation behind and nature of an artist's work. That's the pitch.
D says his views were rare. True, but that's one sentence of the passage. It's a building block, not the takeaway.

## PART 3. Tone (5 min)

Tone frustrates people who want a citation for everything.

The answer says "detached indifference."
They go hunting for the word "detached."
It isn't there.

Tone comes down to two skills.

**Skill one: vocabulary.**

You have to really know what "detached" means, and "indifference."
And then put them together. The pair means something neither word means alone.

Tone answers use harder vocabulary than most of the test.
So put tone answer choices in your vocab tracker. Both the pair and each word.

**Skill two: reading the vibe.**

Think about talking to someone you like.
Later your friend asks, "What was their vibe?"
You know. Instantly.
They never said "my vibe is positive." You felt it.

You do that every day. Do it with the author.

Two ways to get better at it.

First, adjectives and adverbs.
They're the color. They change how you see every noun and verb around them.

"It was just one word." Sure.
So is a minus sign in front of a whole equation.
What matters is how much it changes, not how big it is.

If it helps, highlight adjectives and adverbs for a few sections just to train your eye.

Second, get yourself out of the way.
The "what if I can't do this" voice? Turn it off.
Put that attention on the author instead.
Read with expression in your head, like you're saying it out loud.
If you're giving it expression, you have to pick up the feeling underneath.

And to be clear: don't go looking for snark.
Be locked in enough that if it shows up, you notice.

**Gilliam, Question 4.** His attitude toward his contemporaries' art.
Direction: negative.
Strength: he's impatient, he calls it conservative. He's not mocking anybody.
"Derisive condescension" has the right direction but too much strength.
"Open dissatisfaction" has the right direction and the right strength. That's B.

Tone answers have a direction and a strength. Match both.

## PART 4. Purpose (4 min)

Purpose asks why.
Why did the author write this passage?
Or why this paragraph, this sentence, this word?

Purpose, motivation, goal. Same thing.

"How am I supposed to read the mind of a stranger I'll never meet?"

You do it all the time.

On a first date, you're reading their goal from what they do.
Little digs about your outfit? You know what they're going for.
They remember details from last time? You know that goal too.
Nobody announced it. You read it from their actions.

Same with an author.

Say paragraph two goes: critic one says this, and critic one is wrong. Critic two says that, and critic two is wrong.

What's the purpose?
To defend the view against its critics.

Notice how I said that.
Not "there was an intro, then critics." That's a play-by-play.
Purpose is one level up. What the moves were for.

A good shortcut: look at what the paragraph accomplished.
The view ended up defended. So that's probably what the author was going for.

Authors almost never tell you "my goal here is..."
You read it the way you'd read a person.

**Gilliam, Question 3.** "The author mentions a collage artist primarily to..."
What did the collage artist accomplish in that paragraph?
It made his contemporaries' art concrete. Direct. Political. Popular.
So the job: show the kind of art Gilliam was reacting against. That's D.
C, "representational art was more popular," is true. It's just not the reason the example is there.

## PART 5. Function and Organization (6 min)

These are LR Method questions with more text.

Function: they point at a piece. A word, a sentence, a paragraph.
What did that piece do for the author?

Use your LR method vocabulary. Here's the kind of thing a piece of text does:

Supports a principle with an example.
Defends a claim.
Offers a counterexample.
Draws implications from a claim.
Endorses a claim.
Introduces a body of knowledge.
Outlines an alternative explanation.
Qualifies a claim.

[Pause.]

Example. Paragraph one: "The recycling crisis is caused by a lack of public education."
Paragraph two: "The recycling crisis is actually caused by outdated machinery."

No "however." No signal word.
But you know what paragraph two is doing.
It's offering an alternative explanation.

Now say the passage claims plastic is bad, gives three examples, then ends:
"But plastics make modern healthcare possible."

What's that last part doing?
It's qualifying the claim. Maybe a counterexample.
It is definitely not a fourth example.

For the Mission on a function question:
Go to the reference. Read a little above and a little below.
Then say what it did before you look at the answers.
If you can't say it, widen the window.

A habit that helps while you read: quip the function sometimes.
"Okay, here's an example of why plastic is bad."
That ties the new idea back to the earlier one. You get the function for free.

But knowing "these are examples" doesn't replace knowing what the examples are.
The function goes on top of the translation. It doesn't replace it.

**Organization questions** are a list of functions, in order.

Here's the process. Go in pieces.

Read and translate only the first part of each answer.
Ask: did this happen?

Yes? Move to the next answer. Don't read the rest yet.
No? Now read the rest of that answer. Cross it off, or let it through to the next round.

Then do all the second pieces. Then all the third pieces.
As you go, check the order too. Did it happen in that sequence?

Only choose an answer after you've vetted every word of it.

You're comparing like with like. All the first pieces, then all the second pieces.
And you slow down exactly where something looks wrong.

## PART 6. Specific Reference (4 min)

What did the author mean by this word or phrase?
Or what were they referring to?

On the current test, the stem names the reference. Go find it in the passage.

The rule: read the sentence before, the sentence with the reference, and the sentence after.
That's the minimum.
If it points somewhere further away, go there too.

I watched a student read only the sentence with the reference.
Then miss the question.
The answer was in the next sentence.

They're not asking for the dictionary meaning.
They're asking what it means here. In context.

Take "their reasons."
Who are "they"? Which reasons?
The right answer won't say "someone's evidence."
It'll say what the reasons actually were.

And the whole passage is fair game.
The meaning doesn't have to come from the same paragraph.
That's why a strong translation matters. The context is already in your head.

Then, in the answers, don't just hunt for your Mission's wording.
Ask of each one: "Is this what they were referring to?"

And read the end of the stem.
"...primarily to refer to the risks resulting from ___."
That's almost a fill-in-the-blank. Answer that exact shape.

## PART 7. Audience and Source (3 min)

Audience: who was this written for?
Source: where would this passage come from? A novel, a research journal, a magazine, a court opinion?

They're linked. Know one and you usually know the other.

You don't need to work these out on every passage.
You need a translation rich enough that if the question shows up, it's easy.

The answers live in small things.

Pronouns. If the author keeps saying "you" and "your," they're telling you the audience.
It stands out because RC authors almost never talk to you directly.

How casual the language is.
"Get ready to bulk up like you've been buff before."
That's not a peer-reviewed journal.

Formal, technical, expensive words? Probably written for experts.

"The defendant in this case"? That sounds like a judge.

You'll have to take a small interpretive step. That's fine. Use common sense.

These are the first things people filter out when they read for the gist.
So when the question shows up, they have nothing to go on.

## PART 8. Inference and Author Agreement (5 min)

When people say "RC is going fine, it's just the inferences," this is what they mean.

Inference: given the passage, what's very likely true?
Author Agreement: given what the author believes, what else would they believe?

Same action. Different evidence.

For inference, the whole passage is yours.
For author agreement, only the author's view counts.

Say there's an opponent in the passage. The opponent says plastic is good. The author says plastic is bad.
On an author agreement question, the opponent's view is out.

The standard is "most strongly supported."
And here's where people grind their gears:
The right answer is usually not copy-pasted from the passage.

"This answer uses a word from the passage, so it's right." No.

You translate the passage. You know what the author wants.
Then you find what that implies.
And you need the whole answer to judge whether the whole idea is supported.

"But there are too many possible inferences to predict."
Right. Remember, the Mission isn't a prediction.
Make one inference yourself anyway.
It puts your brain in the right mode before the answers arrive.

And translate the stem. Hard.

"Suppose a corporation is convicted of a crime with a detection ratio of one in ten. The author would most likely endorse which penalty?"

That's not "any inference." That's applying the author's view to a new situation.

**Gilliam, Question 6.** What would Gilliam agree with?
Mission: direct representation falls short. Mood-driven, non-representational art gets the experience across better.
A, "no aesthetic restrictions of any sort," fits his feel. But "any sort" goes past the passage.
D, "color field offers effective ways to express the complexity of human experience," is supported. That's why he chose it. D.

## PART 9. Parallel and Principle Conform (4 min)

**Parallel.** One twist.

RC Parallel works like LR Parallel Flaw, not Parallel Reasoning.
The right answer is short and loose. It captures the essence.
It won't match the passage point for point.

People miss these when they expect a literal match.

Process:
Go to the section they point you to.
Re-translate it.
Build your skeleton from your translation, not from the raw text.

Then keep your bar low in the first round.
Any real resemblance? It stays.
Clearly off? It goes.
Feels strong? Mark it.

Low bar doesn't mean making excuses.
If the passage had one perspective and nobody did anything wrong, an answer with two perspectives and a wrongdoer is out.
Just don't box yourself in too early.

And quip those short answers.
They're short, but the relationships inside them carry a lot. Understand them fully.

**Gilliam, Question 2.** "Which would come closest to exemplifying Gilliam's work?"
Skeleton: non-representational, experimental, creates a mood, no explicit message.
D, the unframed canvas hung from a balcony, matched one feature. But sea, clouds and sails are representational.
E, folded and crumpled canvas with layers of dripped and splashed paint, matched the essence. E.

**Principle Conform** works the same as in LR.
Read the paragraph, translate it, ask: what's the underlying principle?
Same rules for a correct answer.

The RC twist shows up in comparatives.
"A principle in Passage A that the author of Passage B would not apply."
Use your same principle skills, then check both halves of the stem.
In play in A? Okay. Also in play in B? Then it fails the second half. Gone.

## PART 10. Strengthen, Weaken, Resolution (4 min)

Almost everything in RC is provable.
These three are the exception.

You'll see one or two a section.
Often it's the last question on its passage.
Sometimes the last question in the section, right when your memory is weakest.

The standard: move it, don't prove it.

Strengthen makes it more likely.
Weaken makes it less likely.
Resolution makes the situation make more sense.

You won't get a perfect knockout answer.
Expect four answers that do nothing, and one that pushes in the right direction.

Strengthen and Weaken in RC are usually about cause and effect.
So bring your LR causal tools.
Ask what else could explain the effect.
And watch for an answer that rules out the opposite case, often in words the passage never used.

Resolution: in your Mission, go back and find the tension.
Say exactly what doesn't make sense.
People rush this. "Make it make sense." Make what make sense?

It may not be as neat as an LR paradox. Name it anyway.

And treat every answer as true.
If I could add any fact to fix this, what would it be?
That means new words. That's expected.
Apply the idea to the passage. Don't match keywords.
Keyword matching on these three is one of the worst habits you can have.

---

## PART 11. Close (2 min)

Here's the thread through all of it.

Every one of these questions is easy if you translated the passage well.
Tone, purpose and audience come from how the author talks.
Specific reference, inference and function come from context.
Main point comes from knowing what they want you to take away.

Every one gets hard if you skimmed for the gist.

Three things to take with you.

One. Translate the whole stem. That's half the misses at the top.

Two. Make your Mission. Not to predict. To qualify.

Three. Judge whole answers against the real question. Not keywords. Not first words.

Homework: one RC section, untimed.
Before you answer each question, write the question type and the specific detail the stem adds.
Then make your Mission. Then answer.
In review, for every miss, ask: did I misread the stem?

Good work today.
