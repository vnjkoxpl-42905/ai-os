# RC Lesson: Translate, Confirm, Then Answer
### Teleprompter script. Read straight down. Lines in [brackets] are stage directions.

---

## PART 1. Open (2 min)

Today we're doing Reading Comprehension.

And I want to start with one standard.
Everything else today hangs on it.

When you finish a passage, I should be able to ask you anything about it.
Anything that's in there.
And you know it.

Ask Me Anything. AMA.

Not "I got the gist."
Not "it was about some painter."
If it's in the passage, you know it.

Why does that matter?
Because if you know the passage, the questions go fast.
You're not hunting.
You already have the answer in your head before you read the choices.

So today has two halves.
First, how to read so you actually know the passage.
Second, how to use that to answer the questions.

---

## PART 2. The method: Cappuccino (8 min)

The reading method is called Cappuccino.

Here's the core move.
You read RC exactly the way you read LR.

Read a sentence.
Translate it. Put it in your own plain words.
Quip it. React to it. "Okay, so he hates that." "Huh, that's new."

Read. Translate. Quip.
Every sentence.
At most two sentences together, and only if they're really simple.

Why not group five sentences at once?
Because your translation gets worse.
You stop engaging with the text, and you start just looking at it.

[Pause.]

Now. At the end of each paragraph, something specific happens.

**Version one. Full Cappuccino. For practice.**

Step 1. Read, translate, quip paragraph one.

Step 2. Look away.
Recap the full translation of paragraph one from memory. Silently.

Step 3. Look back and confirm.
Take a few seconds. Check your translation against the paragraph.

Step 4. Read, translate, quip paragraph two.

Step 5. Look away. Recap paragraph two from memory.

Step 6. Confirm again. But now you confirm everything so far.
Paragraph one, then paragraph two.

Keep that pattern.
Paragraph three: recap three from memory, then confirm one, two, three.

Optional at the end: look away and recap the whole passage.
Do that when you're new to this, and every so often after.
Just to prove to yourself you still can.

[Pause. Slow down for this next part. It's the part people get wrong.]

**Confirming is not rereading.**

When you look back, you are not reading the paragraph like you've never seen it.
You just recapped it. You already know it.
Keep that knowledge in front of you as you look.

What it should feel like:
You glance at a chunk of the sentence.
"Yep. Had that."
Next chunk.
"Got it. Got it. Got it."

You're scanning. You always want to skim? Here's where you get to.

And if you find something you left out:
"Okay. Don't forget that."
Lock it in, then move on.

Why not just reread?
Two reasons.
It takes too long.
And it throws away the work you just did. Why translate it at all if you're going to read it again?

[Pause.]

**Version two. Cappuccino 35. For the timed section.**

Same reading. Read, translate, quip, every sentence.

The difference: you skip the look-away recap.
End of the paragraph, you go straight to confirming.

Translate one. Confirm one.
Translate two. Confirm one, confirm two.
Translate three. Confirm one, two, three.

End of the passage, one final confirm of the whole thing. About thirty seconds.

That's the version you can actually run in a 35-minute section.

A few times a week, in practice, pop-quiz yourself.
Do a full out-loud recap on a passage.
That's how you keep the AMA standard from slipping.

One note if you have extended time.
With a 53 or 70 minute accommodation, full Cappuccino is fine on test day,
if that's what gives you the best retention.

---

## PART 3. Before we read (1 min)

Here's how we'll do the passage.

You try it first.
Read, translate, quip, confirm. On your own.

Then I'll walk you through how I'd translate it.
Then the questions. Same deal.
You answer first. Then we compare processes.

The point isn't whether you got the letter.
The point is whether your process looks like mine.

[Give them the passage. Let them work. When they're done, read Part 4.]

## PART 4. The passage, with translation (12 min)

PrepTest 140, Section 4, Passage 1. Sam Gilliam.

[Read the **bold** lines as the passage. Read the plain lines as your translation and quip.]

### Paragraph 1

**African American painter Sam Gilliam, born in 1933, is internationally recognized as one of the foremost painters associated with the Washington Color School,**

So. Sam Gilliam. African American painter. Born 1933.
He's one of the top painters in something called the Washington Color School.

**a group of color field style painters practicing in Washington, D.C., during the 1950s and 1960s.**

The Washington Color School: painters in D.C., fifties and sixties.
They did "color field" style.
I picture a big field of color. Like a canvas that's just red.

**This color field style was an important development in abstract art that emerged after the rise of abstract expressionism.**

Color field: big deal in abstract art. Came after abstract expressionism.

**It evolved from complex and minimally representational abstractions in the 1950s to totally non-representational simplified works of bright colors in the 1960s.**

Fifties: barely representational.
Sixties: not representational at all. Simple. Bright. Pure abstraction.

[Look away. Recap paragraph one out loud for them.]

Recap: Gilliam is a major painter in the Washington Color School. That's a D.C. group, fifties and sixties, doing color field painting. Color field is an important abstract movement that came after abstract expressionism, and it moved from barely representational to fully non-representational.

[Look back. Model the confirm: "Gilliam, got it. D.C., fifties, sixties, got it. After abstract expressionism, got it. Evolved to non-representational, got it. Good."]

### Paragraph 2

**Gilliam's participation in the color field movement was motivated in part by his reaction to the art of his African American contemporaries,**

Why did he join color field? Partly as a reaction against other African American artists of his time.
This is the civil rights era. So I'm guessing politics.

**much of which was strictly representational and intended to convey explicit political statements.**

Yep. Their art: strictly representational, explicitly political.
Makes sense for the moment.

**Gilliam found their approach to be aesthetically conservative.**

Gilliam's take: that's aesthetically conservative. You're playing it safe.

**The message was unmistakable, he felt, and there was little room for the expression of subtlety or ambiguity,**

The message hits you in the face. No subtlety. No ambiguity.
Nothing like "what does this big block of red mean?"

**or, more importantly, the exploration of new artistic territory through experimentation and innovation.**

And the bigger problem for him: no experimentation. Nothing new.
Keep "more importantly." That's his ranking. Innovation is the main complaint.

**For example, one of his contemporaries worked with collage, assembling disparate pieces of images from popular magazines into loosely structured compositions that depicted the period's political issues,**

Example: a collage artist. Magazine clippings arranged to show the political issues of the day.

**featuring such themes as urban life, the rural South, and African American music.**

Themes: urban life, the rural South, African American music.
So it's showing the African American experience, directly.

**Such art was quite popular with the general public.**

This kind of art was popular.

**Gilliam was impatient with its straightforward, literal approach to representation.**

Gilliam: impatient with it. Too literal.
Popular, sure. But do something new.

**In its place, he sought an artistic form that was more expressive than a painted figure or a political slogan,**

What he wanted instead: something that expresses more than a figure or a slogan can.

**more evocative of the complexity of human experience in general and of the African American experience in particular.**

Evocative. Not telling you what it means. Making you feel it.
Human experience in general. African American experience in particular.

**In this he represented a view that was then rare among African American artists.**

And that view was rare among African American artists at the time.
Most of them were doing the direct, political work.

[Look away. Recap paragraph two.]

Recap: Gilliam went color field partly as a reaction against his African American contemporaries. Their art was representational and explicitly political. He thought it was aesthetically conservative: an obvious message, no subtlety, and, more importantly, no experimentation. Example: a collage artist showing political themes like urban life, the rural South and music. Popular with the public. Gilliam wanted something more expressive and evocative of human and African American experience. That view was rare.

[Look back. Confirm paragraph one, then paragraph two. "Got it, got it, got it."]

### Paragraph 3

**Gilliam's highly experimental paintings epitomized his refusal to conform to the public's expectation that African American artists produce explicitly political art.**

His experimental work is the clearest example of him refusing to do what the public expected: political art from African American artists.
He's going to be his own person.

**His early experiments included pouring paint on stained canvases and folding canvases over onto themselves.**

Early on: pouring paint on stained canvas. Folding canvas over on itself.

**Then, around 1965, Gilliam became the first painter to introduce the idea of the unsupported canvas.**

Around 1965: he's the first to use the unsupported canvas. No frame holding it up.

**Partially inspired by the sight of neighbors hanging laundry on clotheslines,**

Inspired partly by neighbors' laundry on the line.

**Gilliam began to drape huge pieces of loose canvas along floors and fold them up and down walls, even suspending them from ceilings, giving a third dimension and therefore a sculptural quality.**

So he drapes canvas on floors, up walls, hangs it from ceilings.
Now it has a third dimension. It's part painting, part sculpture.

**These efforts demonstrate a sensitivity to the texture of daily experience,**

That shows he's sensitive to everyday life. Laundry becomes art.

**as well as the ability to generate tension by juxtaposing conceptual opposites, such as surface and depth or chaos and control, to form a cohesive whole.**

And he can create tension by putting opposites together. Surface and depth. Chaos and control.
And it still holds together as one thing.

**In this way, Gilliam helped advance the notion that the deepest, hardest-to-capture emotions and tensions of being African American could not be represented directly, but were expressed more effectively through the creation of moods that would allow these emotions and tensions to be felt by all audiences.**

This is the big one. The author's whole point lands here.
The deepest parts of the African American experience can't be shown directly.
You get them across better by creating a mood. Something every audience can feel.

[Look away. Recap the whole passage.]

Full recap: Gilliam is a major Washington Color School painter, part of the color field movement that went fully non-representational. He chose it partly in reaction against his contemporaries' representational, political art, which he found conservative and unexperimental. He wanted art that evokes human and African American experience, a rare view then. His experiments, folding, pouring, and the unsupported canvas inspired by laundry, made painting sculptural, showed sensitivity to daily life, and joined opposites into a whole. The upshot: the hardest emotions of being African American come through better as a mood everyone can feel than as a direct picture.

[Ask them: "Anything I said that you didn't have?" That's their missing piece.]

## PART 5. The questions (15 min)

Here's how every question goes.

One. Translate the stem. What exactly is it asking?
Two. Answer it in your head first, from your translation.
Three. Then read each choice, the whole choice, and test it against the passage.

Your answer in your head isn't something to match word for word.
It's a target. It tells you what a right answer has to do.

[For each question: read the stem, have them answer, then read your walkthrough.]

### Question 1

**In the passage, the author is primarily concerned with**

My answer first: explaining what made Gilliam special. Why he made his art, and what it was.

**(A) describing the motivation behind and nature of an artist's work**

Motivation and nature. That's exactly my answer. He was motivated by not wanting to be explicitly political. The nature is the mood-driven experimental work. Keep it.

**(B) describing the political themes that permeate an artist's work**

No. His whole point is to be less explicitly political.

**(C) describing the evolution of an artist's style over a period of time**

There's a little bit of time. Early experiments, then 1965. But that's not much of an evolution. It's his style, not the story of it changing.

**(D) demonstrating that a certain artist's views were rare among African American artists**

True, it says that. But that's one sentence. The passage isn't written to prove he was rare.

**(E) demonstrating that a certain artist was able to transcend his technical limitations**

What limitations? He had none. He was pushing boundaries.

**Answer: A.**

The lesson: a main point answer covers the whole passage. A true detail isn't enough.

### Question 2

**Which one of the following would come closest to exemplifying the characteristics of Gilliam's work as described in the passage?**

My answer first: very abstract. No explicit message. Nothing directly representational. Creates a mood.

**(A) a brightly colored painting carefully portraying a man dressed in work clothes and holding a shovel in his hands**

A man with a shovel. That's directly representational. Out.

**(B) a large wrinkled canvas painted with soft blended colors and overlaid with glued-on newspaper photographs depicting war scenes**

Wrinkled canvas, sure. But war photos: representational, and explicitly political. Not him.

**(C) a painted abstract caricature of a group of jazz musicians waiting to perform**

It says abstract, but it's still a picture of musicians. He wants you to feel people's experience, not see people.

**(D) a long unframed canvas painted with images of the sea and clouds and hung from a balcony to simulate the unfurling of sails**

Unframed and hung, that part fits. But sea and clouds, simulating sails, that's still representational. I don't love it. Leave it open.

**(E) a folded and crumpled canvas with many layers of color paint dripped and splashed into one another**

Folded. Crumpled. Dripped paint. All of it blending together. That's exactly paragraph three. And nothing representational.

**Answer: E.**

The lesson: one matching feature doesn't make a match. D got the canvas right and the core idea wrong.

### Question 3

**The author mentions a collage artist in the second paragraph primarily in order to**

My answer first: to contrast with Gilliam. The collage artist is the typical artist of the time. Direct, political. The thing Gilliam pushed against.

**(A) exemplify the style of art of the Washington Color School**

No. The Washington Color School is Gilliam's group. The collage artist is the opposite.

**(B) point out the cause of the animosity between representational artists and abstract artists**

Animosity? There isn't any. Gilliam doesn't like the art. He doesn't hate the artists.

**(C) establish that representational art was more popular with the general public than abstract art was**

The passage does say it was popular. But it says that separately. That isn't why the example is there.

**(D) illustrate the kind of art that Gilliam was reacting against**

Yes. That's my answer.

**(E) show why Gilliam's art was primarily concerned with political issues**

The opposite. His art wasn't about political issues.

**Answer: D.**

The lesson: on a "why is this mentioned" question, ask what job the example does in the argument. C is true. It's just not the job.

### Question 4

**The passage most strongly suggests that Gilliam's attitude toward the strictly representational art of his contemporaries is which one of the following?**

My answer first: he doesn't like it. But he's not at war with anyone. It's just not what he wants to do.

**(A) derisive condescension**

Too strong. He's not mocking anyone.

**(B) open dissatisfaction**

Yes. He's openly unhappy with it and goes another way.

**(C) whimsical dismissal**

"Ha ha, stick figures." No. Nothing playful in the passage.

**(D) careful neutrality**

He isn't neutral. He calls it conservative. He's impatient with it.

**(E) mild approval**

He doesn't approve at all.

**Answer: B.**

The lesson: tone answers have a strength. Match the strength, not just the direction. A and B are both negative. Only one is the right size.

### Question 5

**The passage states all of the following EXCEPT:**

Translate the stem. Four of these are in the passage. One isn't. I'm looking for the one that isn't.

**(A) Draping and folding canvases gives them a sculptural quality.**

Stated. End of paragraph three.

**(B) Gilliam refused to satisfy the public's expectation concerning what subjects African American artists ought to address.**

Stated. He refused to do the explicitly political art people expected.

**(C) Gilliam's views on explicitly political art were rare among African American artists.**

Stated. "A view that was then rare."

**(D) The color field style involved experimentation more than Gilliam believed the art of his African American contemporaries did.**

Yes. Color field is experimental. He thought his contemporaries' work had no experimentation. Stated.

**(E) Everyday images such as laundry hanging out to dry are most likely to give artists great inspiration.**

Not stated. Laundry inspired him, once. The passage never says everyday images are the most likely source for artists in general.

**Answer: E.**

The lesson: watch for a specific fact stretched into a general rule. "He was inspired by laundry" is not "laundry is the best inspiration."

### Question 6

**The passage suggests that Gilliam would be most likely to agree with which one of the following statements?**

My answer first: direct representation falls short. A mood-driven, non-representational approach gets the experience across better.

**(A) Artists need not be concerned with aesthetic restrictions of any sort.**

It fits the feel. He breaks rules. But "of any sort" is a lot. The passage doesn't go that far. Keep it open, but it's strong.

**(B) The images portrayed in paintings, whether representational or not, should be inspired by real-life images.**

He never says where inspiration should come from. His opinions are about how you paint, not where you get the idea.

**(C) Artists ought to produce art that addresses the political issues of the period.**

The opposite of Gilliam.

**(D) The color field style offers artists effective ways to express the complexity of human experience.**

Yes. That's why he chose it. Paragraph two: "evocative of the complexity of human experience." Paragraph three backs it up.

**(E) The public's expectations concerning what kind of art a certain group of artists produces should be a factor in that artist's work.**

The opposite again. He refused to conform to those expectations.

**Answer: D.**

The lesson: A felt right. D is supported. When an attractive answer goes further than the text, pick the one the text actually backs.

## PART 6. Close (2 min)

Look at what happened on those six questions.

Every time, I had an answer before I read the choices.
And every time, that answer came from the translation.
Not from going back and searching.

That's the payoff of AMA.
You do the work while you read, so the questions go fast.

Three things to take with you.

One. Read, translate, quip. Every sentence. Same as LR.

Two. At the end of each paragraph, confirm. Don't reread.
"Got it, got it, got it." If something's missing, lock it in.

Three. On every question, answer it yourself first. Then test each whole choice against the passage.

Homework: three passages with Cappuccino. Full version on the first one, with the look-away recap.
Then the 35 version on the other two.
At the end of one of them, recap the whole passage out loud.
If you can't, that's your signal. Slow down on the translation.

Good work today.
