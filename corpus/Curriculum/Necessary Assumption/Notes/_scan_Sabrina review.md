Practice Test 101, Section 2

1\. Paradox Questions: The "Inverse World"

The Question (PT101, S2, Q14)

\> Stimulus: In an effort to boost sales during the summer months, which are typically the best for soft-drink sales, Foamy Soda lowered its prices. In spite of this, however, the sales of Foamy Soda dropped during the summer months.

\> Question Stem: Each of the following, if true, contributes to reconciling the apparent discrepancy indicated above except:

\> Answer Choices:

\> \* (A) The soft-drink industry as a whole experienced depressed sales during the summer months.

\> \* (B) Foamy Soda's competitors lowered their prices even more drastically during the summer months.

\> \* (C) Because of an increase in the price of sweeteners, the production costs of Foamy Soda rose during the summer months.

\> \* (D) A strike at Foamy Soda's main plant forced production cutbacks that resulted in many stores not receiving their normal shipments during the summer months.

\> \* (E) The weather during the summer months was unseasonably cool, decreasing the demand for soft drinks.

\>

Detailed Analysis & Strategy

1\. The Core Paradox

We have two facts that shouldn't seemingly coexist:

\* Fact A: Foamy Soda lowered prices during the peak season (summer) to boost sales.

\* Fact B: Sales unexpectedly dropped.

2\. The "Inverse World" (The "Except" Nuance)

The challenge here isn't just the paradox; it’s the word "EXCEPT" in the question stem. This flips the entire logic of the question into an "inverse world."

\* Standard Goal: Find the answer that explains the mystery.

\* "Except" Goal: Find the answer that fails to explain the mystery.

\* Four answers will resolve the conflict (and are therefore wrong).

\* One answer will not resolve the conflict (and is therefore right).

\> Strategic Drill: Whenever you see "EXCEPT," mentally replace it with "THE LEAST." Ask yourself: "Which of these answers explains the drop in sales the least?" This prevents your brain from latching onto a good explanation, which is a trap here.

\>

3\. Breakdown of the Options

\* The Valid Explanations (Distractors):

\* (A) Industry Slump: If the whole industry is down, Foamy Soda goes down too. This explains it.

\* (B) Competitor Prices: If rivals cut prices more, customers flock to them. This explains it.

\* (D) Supply Issues: If stores didn't get the soda (strike), people couldn't buy it. This explains it.

\* (E) Weather: If it was cold, people buy less soda. This explains it.

\* The Correct Answer (The Failure):

\* (C) Rising Production Costs: This choice states that sweeteners got expensive, raising production costs.

\* Why it wins: Production costs affect the company's profit margins, not the volume of sales. A customer looking at a cheap can of soda on the shelf doesn't know (or care) that it cost the company more to make it. Because this fact explains nothing about why fewer cans were bought, it fails to bridge the gap.

2\. Must Be True: Quantity & Inference

The Question (PT101, S2, Q15)

\> Stimulus: Dr. Z: Many of the characterizations of my work offered by Dr. Q are imprecise, and such characterizations do not provide an adequate basis for sound criticism of my work.

\> Question Stem: Which one of the following can be properly inferred from Dr. Z's statement?

\> Answer Choices:

\> \* (A) Some of Dr. Q's characterizations of Dr. Z's work provide an adequate basis for sound criticism of Dr. Z's work.

\> \* (B) All of Dr. Q's characterizations of Dr. Z's work that are not imprecise provide an adequate basis for sound criticism of Dr. Z's work.

\> \* (C) All of the characterizations of Dr. Z's work by Dr. Q that do not provide an adequate basis for sound criticism of Dr. Z's work are imprecise.

\> \* (D) If the characterization of someone's work is precise, then it provides a sound basis for criticizing that work.

\> \* (E) At least one of Dr. Q's characterizations of Dr. Z's work fails to provide an adequate basis for sound criticism of that work.

\>

Detailed Analysis & Strategy

1\. Precise Terminology: "Many" vs. "Most"

The stimulus uses the word "Many." In Logical Reasoning, quantity words are mathematical constraints.

\* "Many" logically means "Some" or "More than one." It does not imply a majority.

\* "Most" strictly means a majority (more than 50%).

\* Trap Alert: Do not assume "many" implies "most."

2\. The Directionality Rule

You must maintain the "first half" and "second half" of quantity statements to avoid invalid reversals.

\* Valid: "Many \[Characterizations\] -\> \[Are Imprecise\]"

\* Invalid Reversal: "Many \[Imprecise Things\] -\> \[Are Characterizations\]" (You cannot flip the groups).

3\. The "Bridging Ideas" Technique

To find the inference, look for the concept that repeats. This is your bridge.

\* Claim 1: Many characterizations are imprecise.

\* Claim 2: Such (imprecise) characterizations do not provide an adequate basis for criticism.

4\. The Deduction

Because the "imprecise" characterizations are the same ones mentioned in the first sentence (the "Many"), we can carry the quantity across the bridge.

\* Logic: Since "Many" are "Imprecise," and "Imprecise" means "Inadequate," it must be true that Many (or at least one) are Inadequate.

\* Matching the Answer: Choice (E) says "At least one... fails to provide an adequate basis." This is the mathematical equivalent of "Many," making it the correct inference.

3\. Necessary Assumption: The "Jenga" Mechanics

The Question (PT101, S2, Q18)

(Note: The text below is reconstructed from the transcript discussion, as the source file contained a duplicate stimulus error for this specific question number.)

\> Stimulus: The widespread staff reductions in a certain region's economy are said to be causing people who still have their jobs to cut back on new purchases as they thought they too had become economically distressed. Clearly, however, actual spending by such people is undiminished because there has been no unusual increase in the amount of money held by those people in savings accounts.

\> Question Stem: Which one of the following is an assumption on which the argument relies?

\> Answer Choices:

\> \* (A) If people in the region who continue to be employed have debts, they are not now paying them off at an accelerated rate.

\> \* (B) People in the region who continue to be employed and who have relatives who have lost their jobs commonly assist those relatives financially.

\> \* (C) If people in the region who have lost jobs get new jobs, the new jobs generally pay less well than the ones they lost.

\> \* (D) People in the region who continue to be employed are pessimistic about their prospects for increasing their incomes.

\> \* (E) There exist no statistics about sales of goods in the region as a whole.

\>

Detailed Analysis & Strategy

1\. The Conceptual Framework

A necessary assumption is a load-bearing wall.

\* The Jenga Analogy: This assumption is a specific block in the tower. It doesn't create the entire tower (it’s not sufficient), but if you pull it out, the argument collapses.

2\. The "Level 5" Flaw

The argument claims that because savings accounts haven't gone up, spending must not have gone down.

\* The Blind Spot: The author assumes savings accounts are the only place unspent money goes. This ignores "invisible" savings—money that isn't spent but also isn't put into a bank (e.g., paying off debts, keeping cash).

3\. The Negation Test (The Scientific Proof)

To prove Choice (A) is correct, we must negate it.

\* Original Choice (A): "They are not now paying them off at an accelerated rate."

\* Negated Version: "People ARE paying off debts at an accelerated rate."

\* The Result: If people are aggressively paying off debt, they are spending less on goods (matching the staff reduction fear), but the money is disappearing into debt payments rather than showing up in savings accounts. This explains why savings accounts are flat even though spending dropped.

\* Conclusion Destroyed: This proves the author's conclusion (that spending didn't drop) is wrong. Since the negation destroys the argument, the original statement was necessary.

4\. Strengthening (Principles): The "Should" Standard

The Question (PT101, S2, Q20)

\> Stimulus: When the Pinecrest Animal Shelter, a charitable organization, was in danger of closing because it could not pay for important repairs, its directors appealed to the townspeople to donate money that would be earmarked to pay for those repairs. Since more funds were ultimately donated than were used for the repairs, the directors plan to donate the surplus funds to other animal shelters. But before doing so, the directors should obtain permission from those who made the donations.

\> Question Stem: Which one of the following principles, if valid, most helps to justify the position advocated above and yet places the least restriction on the allocation of funds by directors of charitable organizations?

\> Answer Choices:

\> \* (A) The directors of charitable organizations cannot allocate publicly solicited funds to any purposes for which the directors had not specifically earmarked the funds in advance.

\> \* (B) People who solicit charitable donations from the public for a specific cause should spend the funds only on that cause or, if that becomes impossible, should dispose of the funds according to the express wishes of the donors.

\> \* (C) A charitable organization that has solicited funds for a specific cause must return to the donors any funds not spent on that cause unless the donors permit the organization to spend the funds for other purposes.

\> \* (D) Charitable organizations should not solicit funds for specific causes unless they can guarantee that the funds raised will be spent only on those causes.

\> \* (E) Charitable organizations should be allowed to allocate funds as they see fit, provided the funds are spent for charitable purposes.

\>

Detailed Analysis & Strategy

1\. The "Should" Problem

The conclusion states the shelter "should" obtain permission. In logic, you cannot derive a "should" from facts alone. You need a standard or a "commandment"—just as religious morality relies on a text to define "good," this argument needs a principle to justify its recommendation.

2\. The "Least Restrictive" Constraint

This question adds a layer of complexity: the correct principle must justify asking for permission without tying the directors' hands completely. It needs to balance donor rights with operational flexibility.

3\. Evaluating the Top Contenders

\* Choice (A) - Too Strict: This says funds cannot be used for non-earmarked purposes. This supports the idea of not just giving it away, but it creates a dead end. Even if they asked for permission, this rule implies they still couldn't use the money for a new purpose because it wasn't earmarked "in advance." It freezes the funds.

\* Choice (B) - The Perfect Balance: This principle offers a flowchart:

\* Spend on the specific cause.

\* If impossible (which it is, since repairs are done), follow the "express wishes of the donors."

\* Why it wins: This justifies the need to contact donors (to find out their wishes), which aligns with the "should obtain permission" conclusion. However, it leaves the door open for the directors to ask, "Do you wish for us to donate this to Shelter B?" If the donors say yes, the funds move. It justifies the requirement without blocking the action.
