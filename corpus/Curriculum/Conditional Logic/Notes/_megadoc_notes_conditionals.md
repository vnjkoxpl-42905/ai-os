**Path:** LSAT DUMP/LR/Conditional/Conditional.gdoc  **Modified:** 2025-10-17T08:42:29.714000+00:00  **Words:** 658  **Doc ID:** 10e6Okb__aaEYdU2T2gmAD95tnbTy4KYtrggk3Stl4sQ

Conditional logic forms the bedrock of many arguments and inferences tested on the LSAT requiring test-takers to demonstrate proficiency in recognizing the contrapositive logic and avoiding common illegal moves. While the provided sources do not detail every single LSAT Logical Reasoning question type (such as Parallel Reasoning or Method of Reasoning) they offer a comprehensive breakdown of the core activities required for analysis and specifically target the most critical error tested in Flaw questions. The breakdown of conditional logic primarily revolves around two main analytical activities: Chaining (deriving new rules) and Triggering (deriving new facts). I. General Breakdown Activity: Chaining Conditionals (Deriving New Rules). Chaining involves synthesizing multiple conditional rules to create a new inferred conditional statement. This is crucial for questions that require you to draw broad conclusions from a set of premises (often seen in Inference/Must Be True contexts). Connecting Rules: You must determine if the necessary condition (right side) of one conditional statement matches the sufficient condition (left side) of another. For instance if A rightarrow B and B rightarrow C they chain to infer a new conditional relationship: A rightarrow C. Contraposing Chains: You can contraposit an entire chain starting from the final necessary condition and negating back to the initial sufficient condition. If the chain is A rightarrow D the contrapositive is If not D then not A. The LSAT commonly rewards the ability to connect the beginning of the chain to the ending of the chain. Negating Complex Ideas: If the idea being contraposed (the trigger or the outcome) is a complex idea connected by and or or (e.g. A and B) you must remember to flip the truth values when negating. And turns into or and or turns into and. This ensures you correctly flip the truth value of everything in the complex idea. II. General Breakdown Activity: Triggering Conditionals (Deriving New Facts). Triggering is the process of using a known fact to activate a conditional rule and derive a certain factual inference. This is the process utilized in many Must Be True and Inference questions. A conditional rule (Sufficient rightarrow Necessary) can only be used in two valid scenarios: 1. Affirming the Sufficient Condition: If a fact confirms that the sufficient condition (the left side the trigger) is true then the necessary condition (the right side the outcome) must necessarily follow. 2. Denying the Necessary Condition (Contrapositive Logic): If a fact confirms that the necessary condition (the right side the outcome) is not true then the sufficient condition (the left side the trigger) cannot have been true. If the fact relates to the conditional rule in any other way (e.g. affirming the necessary or denying the sufficient) the rule just disappears and has no power over us. III. Breakdown in Specific Question Types and Contexts. Flaw Questions. Flaw questions are specifically designed to test whether you can recognize errors in conditional reasoning. The Conditional Flaw: The specific mistake frequently made by LSAT authors is confusing the necessary and the sufficient. This happens when an argument presents a rule (A rightarrow B) observes that the right side (the necessary condition B) is happening and then attempts to conclude that this proves the left side (the sufficient condition A) is happening. Breakdown Method: Your breakdown must identify the conditional rule and then recognize that the author has committed the illegal move of reading the conditional backward. This is the primary error the LSAT tries to ensure students avoid. For example learning that Alice is in the USA (necessary condition) does not allow you to conclude she is in NYC (sufficient condition). Inference and Must Be True Questions. These questions rely heavily on the ability to correctly apply the chaining and triggering activities described above. Breakdown Method: When presented with premises (rules) and facts you must diagram the conditionals and perform valid inferences. You must look for ways to chain rules together to find a connection from the premises to the answer choices.

How to Figure Out Which Is Which The What Test vs. Personalized If/Then. The What Test. The What Test is designed to resolve ambiguities in sentences with conditional indicators by pinpointing exactly what the indicator refers to. How to Perform the What Test. This test involves adding a what to whichever side of the indicator makes grammatical sense helping clarify the target of the condition. If what? People who what? Depends on what? When what? Each what? Need what? Any what? Must what? Have to what? All what? Whats necessary? Whats essential? Every what? Whats required? Whats the precondition? In order to what? Only (if) what? This test is typically unnecessary for straightforward indicators like if and all but proves invaluable for more confusing indicators such as depends and required. Example of The What Test. Sentence: Air is required for breathing. Implementation: Question: What is required in this sentence? Answer: Air is the thing that is required. Personalized If/Then Strategy. Personalized If/Then is a technique used to translate conditional statements into personal contexts simplifying understanding and ensuring correct logical diagramming. How to Create a Personalized If/Then. 1. Understand the casual non-conditional meaning of the sentence. 2. Take a guess at which half of the statement is the sufficient condition. 3. Put yourself in the situation of your guess and ask what you know about yourself. 4. If your guess is correct you will find that the necessary condition has to pertain. Example Translation into Personalized If/Then. Original Statement: Orchids depend on frequent conversation. Casual Meaning: Orchids need frequent conversation. Guess: Orchids as the sufficient condition. Personalization: If Im an orchid what do I know about me? I depend on frequent conversation. If Im an orchid then I have frequent conversation. This method not only aids in understanding the conditional but also verifies its correctness. Incorrect guesses can be easily identified and corrected through this personal context. Advantages of Personalized If/Then. Helps intuitively understand conditionals by imagining yourself as part of the scenario. Provides confidence throughout tests especially in correctly interpreting conditionals not phrased as if/then statements. Both the What Test and Personalized If/Then Strategy are crucial tools in deciphering and applying conditional logic particularly in competitive exams like the LSAT.
