# Valid Conclusions & Invalid Conclusions

Now that we have the basics of *premises* and *conclusions*, let's enter into the realm of good and bad. Not all conclusions are created equal.

## VALID CONCLUSIONS = PROVEN

Some conclusions are sturdily supported. We call these beautiful starships **valid conclusions**. A *valid conclusion* must be true, if its *premises* are true. Since we always assume our premises to be true, a valid conclusion is one that *must be true*. That's right; valid conclusions *have to be true*. They're provable. 100%. No question. No wiggle room.

Let's look at a few simple valid conclusions.

### Example 1: Practice Designing a Valid Conclusion

| | |
|---|---|
| **PREMISE 1** | Pears and walnuts both contain potassium. |
| **PREMISE 2** | Potassium is an element. |

**PAUSE & DESIGN YOUR CONCLUSION**

*Based on the premises above, what is the one valid conclusion you can design?*

*Your Answer: _________________*

...Now, let's check your work.

| | |
|---|---|
| **PREMISE 1** | Pears and walnuts both contain potassium. |
| **PREMISE 2** | Potassium is an element. |
| **VALID CONCLUSION** ✅ | Pears and walnuts have an element in common. |

Notice the bolded overlapping term, **potassium**, in the two premises. This point of similarity is an *interlocking point*. The interlocking point lets us create something new by combining both things we know about potassium into one sentence. That's what our valid conclusion did — it combined. This is a great introductory way to come up with valid conclusions: Look for a common term between two premises and figure out what that repetition allows you to conclude. In this case, I basically just replaced the word "potassium" in the first premise with the definition of potassium supplied in the second premise.

### Example 2: Practice Designing a Valid Conclusion

| | |
|---|---|
| **PREMISE 1** | Fido is **15 inches tall**. |
| **PREMISE 2** | Distrust of those **under 18 inches tall** is never justified. |

**PAUSE & DESIGN YOUR CONCLUSION**

*Your Answer: _________________*

| | |
|---|---|
| **PREMISE 1** | Fido is **15 inches tall**. |
| **PREMISE 2** | Distrust of those **under 18 inches tall** is never justified. |
| **VALID CONCLUSION** ✅ | Distrust of Fido is never justified. |

This is a slightly more complex example. We don't see any exact phrases repeated between the premises, but we can see that the state of affairs from the first premise (being 15 inches tall) fits into the category outlined in the second premise. Since Fido is 15 inches tall, he fits into the under-18-inches-tall category and the judgment associated with that category applies to him.

### Example 3: Practice Designing a Valid Conclusion

| | |
|---|---|
| **PREMISE 1** | Leo **won't drink milk**. |
| **PREMISE 2** | Only those who **always drink milk** will be admitted to the flight academy. |

**PAUSE & DESIGN YOUR CONCLUSION**

*Your Answer: _________________*

| | |
|---|---|
| **PREMISE 1** | Leo **won't drink milk**. |
| **PREMISE 2** | Only those who **always drink milk** will be admitted to the flight academy. |
| **VALID CONCLUSION** ✅ | Leo will not be admitted to the flight academy. |

Leo won't drink milk and only those who always drink milk get into the flight academy. By refusing to drink milk, Leo doesn't let himself fall into the category of people who always drink it. So, Leo is not in the *always-drink* category. Only the people in the *always-drink* category go to the flight academy. If Leo isn't in the category, he can't go.

> **INFERENCES VS. VALID CONCLUSIONS**
>
> *Valid conclusions* are always presented as part of an argument. *Inferences*, on the other hand, are not part of an argument. Inferences are something we come up with ourselves from a *Premise Set*. An *Inference* is a valid conclusion you design yourself, not a conclusion inside an argument.

## INVALID CONCLUSIONS = NOT PROVEN ❌

*Invalid conclusions* are the LSAT's bread and butter. There are approximately infinity ways to go wrong when making an argument. Let's start exploring invalid conclusions with the same premises we used above.

### Example 1: Practice Designing Loopholes

| | |
|---|---|
| **PREMISE 1** | Pears and walnuts both contain potassium. |
| **PREMISE 2** | Potassium is an element. |

**INVALID CONCLUSIONS** ❌

- Pears and walnuts are the same food.
- Pears and walnuts taste the same.
- Pears and walnuts have most things in common.
- Pears and walnuts are similar.
- Pears and walnuts are both natural.

**PAUSE & DESIGN YOUR LOOPHOLES**

1. _________________
2. _________________
3. _________________
4. _________________
5. _________________

#### Introducing Loopholes

| INVALID CONCLUSION | LOOPHOLE |
|---|---|
| **Pears and walnuts are the same food.** | What if just because you have an element in common doesn't mean you're the same food? Pears and walnuts could be different in every other way. |
| **Pears and walnuts taste the same.** | What if having potassium in common doesn't necessarily affect taste at all? |
| **Pears and walnuts have most things in common.** | What if we only know they have one thing in common? Let's not generalize that out to a bunch more things. |
| **Pears and walnuts are similar.** | What if potassium is one of many elements in pears and walnuts? Having one element in common isn't enough to justify calling the two things similar. Everything else about them could be different. |
| **Pears and walnuts are both natural.** | What if the potassium in pears and walnuts isn't even naturally derived? And even if the potassium is natural, everything else in them could be artificial. |

We're questioning authority. We're putting every conclusion to the "What if?" test. Valid conclusions aren't vulnerable to reasonable Loopholes. Invalid conclusions are.

### Example 2: Practice Designing Loopholes

| | |
|---|---|
| **PREMISE 1** | Fido is 15 inches tall. |
| **PREMISE 2** | Distrust of those under 18 inches tall is never justified. |

**INVALID CONCLUSIONS** ❌

- Fido is short.
- We can distrust people over 18 inches tall.
- Fido would be hurt by distrust.
- Distrust is a bad thing.
- We should be nice to Fido.

| INVALID CONCLUSION | LOOPHOLE |
|---|---|
| **Fido is short.** | What if I'm talking about my robot named Fido? Always assume there is something being left out of what the author chose to present. |
| **We can distrust people over 18 inches tall.** | What if we don't know anything about people over 18 inches tall? Never assume the opposite judgment holds for the opposite of the group being discussed. |
| **Fido would be hurt by distrust.** | What if we can't predict Fido's feelings? We're not allowed to fill in these gaps for the author. |
| **Distrust is a bad thing.** | What if distrust is good in this fantasy world? Outside knowledge can't lend a helping hand. |
| **We should be nice to Fido.** | What if lack of distrust doesn't mean we have to be nice to Fido? |

### Example 3: Practice Designing Loopholes

| | |
|---|---|
| **PREMISE 1** | Leo won't drink milk. |
| **PREMISE 2** | Only those who always drink milk will be admitted to the flight academy. |

**INVALID CONCLUSIONS** ❌

- Leo will be admitted to the flight academy.
- Leo has no allergies.
- The number of people admitted to the flight academy is small.
- Only those who love milk will be admitted to the flight academy.
- Leo has great taste in beverages.

| INVALID CONCLUSION | LOOPHOLE |
|---|---|
| **Leo will be admitted to the flight academy.** | What if Leo has disqualified himself? We only admit people who always drink milk. Leo doesn't, so he doesn't fit the category. |
| **Leo has no allergies.** | What if having allergies is separate from being admitted to a flight academy? |
| **The number of people admitted is small.** | What if we don't know how many people always drink milk? Drinking milk is only a minimum qualification. It doesn't guarantee admission. |
| **Only those who love milk will be admitted.** | What if loving milk and always drinking milk are not the same thing? Maybe people drink it just for the health benefits and actually hate milk. |
| **Leo has great taste in beverages.** | What if not liking milk doesn't necessarily mean you have great taste? This conclusion, while undoubtedly true in real life, is not provable. |

---

*Valid conclusions* are proven by their premises. *Invalid conclusions* aren't proven by their premises. ***Loopholes*** are how we attack invalid conclusions.

Notice how all of the invalid conclusions took something for granted. We call out this shaky ground by creating objections called **Loopholes**.
