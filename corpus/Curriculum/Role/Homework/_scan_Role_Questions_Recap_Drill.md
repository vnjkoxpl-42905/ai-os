**📘 Recap – Role Questions Accuracy Drill**

**\
Level 3**

**Correct answers on Role**

The correct answer doesn't say anything descriptively wrong. The other four do. (Maybe once in a blue moon, you'd have two "true" answers, where you'd have to ask which one's better.)

The most common correct answer is Premise or some other way of describing Supporting Idea.

Easier Role questions will stick to classic language like Premise and Conclusion, whereas harder Role questions will often switch to less familiar abstract language or synonyms.

**• Main Conclusion –**

the claim the author seeks to establish, a generalization supported by evidence, the only conclusion.

**• Premise –**

support, partial support (just means there's more than one premise), evidence, illustration of, example of, unsupported premise.

**• Intermediate Conclusion –**

subsidiary conclusion, supported premise, a conclusion but not the main conclusion, a statement for which some support is provided but is used to support the main conclusion.

**• Opposing Position –**

the view the argument is designed to discredit, the claim the author attempts to show is false.

**\
Incorrect answers on Role**

**• Wrong Ingredient –**

these describe something in the paragraph, but not the claim we were asked about.

**• Too Strong –**

some answers that are about an idea being required or sufficient for the conclusion.

**• Didn't Happen –**

these reference something that never happened in the paragraph.

**• Wrong Conclusion –**

lots of wrong answers correctly call the claim a Premise but then say it's supporting some idea that doesn't match the argument's Conclusion.

**🔹 To advance from Level 3 to Level 4:**

• You must get 5 out of 5 correct in a row.

• If you get a question wrong:

\- You must write at least 3–5 sentences explaining why you chose the wrong answer — what specifically attracted you to it and why it seemed appealing.

\- Then write another 3–5 sentences explaining what makes the right answer correct and why it may have initially seemed less appealing.

\- This reflection must be done untimed.

**\
Level 4**

On harder Role questions, we're likely to see a couple patterns emerge:

**• Intermediate Conclusion?**

Many of the harder problems test the existence of an Intermediate Conclusion, even if the claim we're being asked about isn’t itself that. Answer choices describing a Premise might differ by saying, “It provides direct support for the Conclusion” vs. “It provides support for a claim that then provides direct support for the Conclusion.”

To assess whether a claim is or isn’t an Intermediate Conclusion, we want to ask ourselves,

*Is there Support for this claim? Why should we believe this claim? Does anything else in the paragraph sound like it's answering that question?*

Often, when there isn't a supporting idea present and thus the claim you're examining is really just a Premise, it’s useful to think about how you would support that claim.

If you're asking yourself, “Was the claim You shouldn't tell jokes at a funeral supported?”—supply your own answer. Just get your brain hearing what actual support would sound like: “You shouldn't tell jokes at a funeral because... funerals are a somber occasion and telling jokes makes it seem like you're not respecting the gravity of the situation.” By articulating what support would sound like, you can sometimes help strengthen your conviction that no support was given.

**🔹 To advance from Level 4 to Level 5:**

• You must get 5 out of 5 correct in a row.

• If you get a question wrong:

\- You must write at least 3–5 sentences explaining why you chose the wrong answer — what about it seemed right or tempting.

\- Then write another 3–5 sentences explaining why the correct answer is actually right, and what you may have overlooked or misunderstood.

\- This must be completed untimed.

**\
Level 5**

Focus mainly on getting more comfortable with the abstract answers.

When you encounter a wrong answer, challenge yourself to come up with a sample argument of your own that would match that answer choice.

We need to pay attention to language indicators in each answer choice signifying which half of the answer is meant to describe the Conclusion and which half is meant to describe the Evidence.

Once we’ve determined that, we can start asking ourselves questions like,

*Can I match up this (abstract answer choice) wording with the Conclusion?*

If not, eliminate.

If so, then we would try to match up the other half, making sure that we’re consistent in terms of matching the same nouns/traits to the same abstract terms in each answer choice.

**🔹 To complete Level 5:**

• You must get 5 out of 5 correct in a row.

• If you miss a question:

\- Write at least 3–5 sentences explaining what drew you to the wrong answer — the specific language, logic, or phrasing that seemed right.

\- Then write another 3–5 sentences on why the correct answer is valid, clear, and ultimately more appropriate.

\- All analysis is to be completed untimed.
