# Part 2: Principle-Conform | Stimulus

## Debrief: Principle-Conform | Stimulus

When a Principle-Conform stimulus is providing an explicit principle (or two), we're usually being tested on our ability to apply a conditional rule.

If the rule(s) is conditional, then we need to be attentive to conditional logic words, conjunctions such as "and / or", and sometimes even little qualifying adjectives, as we diagram how the rule works.

A brutally hard example:

> **PRINCIPLE:** A resignation offered under duress should be accepted only if the person resigning still endorses it one week later or they spoke to HR about it before submitting the resignation.

"only if" → put it on the right:

`offered under duress AND should be accepted → still endorsed 1 week later OR spoke to HR before`

The way these principles get used, you want the normative, "Judgy" term to be on the right of the arrow (here, whether it *should / shouldn't* be accepted is the judgment the rule renders). So an effective rephrase would be something like:

`resignation offered under duress AND didn't speak to HR before AND not endorsed 1 week later → should not be accepted`

This is not the only form a correct version could take. We could leave off the context-setting "under duress" and focus on whether we should accept it based on whether the person spoke to HR and whether they endorsed it 1 week later.

Often, simplifying down to the core ideas is beneficial. But if we end up with two answers that both seem to work, we need to reread the principle because we might be missing context-setting qualifiers like "under duress" that we selectively ignored while diagramming.

Perhaps only one of those two remaining answers actually establishes that this resignation was under duress.

## The Goal

The goal here is to apply the given principle to a set of situations. The correct answer will be a situation that either conforms to the rule or, in some cases, violates it.

- 5 of them want us to apply 1 or 2 conditional rules
- 2 of them want us to conform to a fuzzier, non-conditional rule or pattern
- 1 of them wants us to actually find a situation that **violates** the rule.

**Given a principle:** `has cinnamon in it → delicious`

We can only use that rule to conclude that something *is* delicious. Rules can only prove what's on their **right side**. If an answer concludes "Therefore, the soup was *not* delicious", we know it's wrong.

When given two rules, we're only going to use one or the other to justify our correct answer. Start each answer choice by scanning for the conclusion, so you can see which rule it's trying to use. Remind yourself what that rule is about and focus on whether the answer choice clearly establishes the trigger.

Violating a rule means triggering the left side but going against the right side. "Jonathan had an apple pie that had cinnamon and whip cream in it, but it wasn't that tasty" violates the principle above.
