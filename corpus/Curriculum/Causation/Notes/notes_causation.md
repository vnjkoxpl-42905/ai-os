Correlation vs. Causation. 1. Introduction The Puzzling Case of the Rising Gas Bill. Imagine this mini-mystery: Jimmy replaced his old gas water heater with a new highly efficient one but his gas bills went up. Thats strange. Why would that happen? An efficiency upgrade should lead to lower bills not higher ones. This is a classic puzzling fact an observation that seems to defy common sense and demands an explanation. Solving puzzles like this requires a crucial logic skill understanding the difference between things that just happen together (correlation) and one thing that actually makes another thing happen (causation). This guide will give you the tools to tell them apart. 2. Step One Noticing a Pattern (Correlation). A correlation is simply an observation that two things tend to happen together or are associated with each other. Its a statement about what is happening but it makes no claim about why its happening. Think of it as just noticing a pattern. Here are a couple of examples of pure correlation: A study shows that cities with more public gardens also have higher rates of reported neighborly kindness. An observation that people who own pets report lower stress levels than people who do not own pets. Patterns arent just about two things existing together at the same time. Sometimes the pattern is one of sequence. For example if a companys profits rise right after a new CEO takes over its tempting to say the CEO caused the success. This before and after observation is still just a correlation and its a common trap. But just observing that two things happen together isnt the whole story. To get to the why we need to understand a different concept: causation. 3. Step Two Claiming a Cause (Causation). Causation is an active force. Its the claim that one thing directly makes another thing happen. Unlike correlation which describes a passive association causation is about the why. The cause is the driving force behind the effect. Think of it like this: your cars engine causes the wheels to turn. Theres a dynamic forceful relationship at play. 4. The Great Mistake Why Together Doesnt Mean Because Of. This mistake happens when we act like an overconfident detective who after finding one clue declares the case solved without looking for other evidence. The source of this error is leaping to a single story when multiple explanations are possible. Here is the single most important rule to remember: Correlation does not imply causation. This is one of the most common and powerful logical mistakes people make. When we see that two things (A and B) are correlated we cant automatically assume that A caused B. There are usually other possible explanations. The two most important alternatives are reverse causality and a hidden third factor. 4.1 The Chicken or the Egg Problem (Reverse Causality). Sometimes the causal relationship is flipped B actually causes A. This is known as reverse causality. Imagine a study finds that people who drink green smoothies daily tend to be more physically fit. Its tempting to conclude that the smoothies are causing the fitness. But could it be the other way around? Perhaps people who are already physically fit are the ones more likely to adopt healthy habits like drinking green smoothies. In this case being fit (B) causes the smoothie-drinking (A) not the other way around. 4.2 The Hidden Culprit (The Third Factor). Often a hidden third factor (C) is the true cause of both things were observing pulling the strings on both A and B from behind the scenes. 1. The Correlation We observe that people who drive sports cars have a higher accident rate than people who drive minivans. 2. The Flawed Conclusion We might conclude Therefore sports cars are inherently more dangerous and cause accidents. 3. The Insight What if theres a third factor like a drivers personality? Perhaps a thrill-seeking personality (C) makes a person both more likely to buy a sports car (A) and more likely to drive in a way that leads to accidents (B). The car isnt the cause; the drivers personality is the hidden culprit causing both outcomes. 5. A Language Detectives Guide How to Spot the Difference. The verbs used in a statement are the biggest clue to whether its describing a correlation or claiming a cause. Pay close attention to the specific words being used. Signal Words Correlation vs. Causation. Correlation Language Association: is associated with, is correlated with, coincides with, occurs along with, tends to, are more likely, is found in people who. Causation Language Active Force: causes, makes, produces, results in, leads to, promotes, is responsible for.

A Mastery-Level Dossier on LSAT Causation. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY: The Mastery-Level 80/20. This dossier provides an exhaustive analysis of causal reasoning as it is uniquely constructed and tested on the Law School Admission Test (LSAT). Moving beyond the foundational maxim that correlation does not equal causation, this report deconstructs the full typology of causal claims, the patterns of correlational evidence used to support them, and the precise strategic frameworks required to systematically strengthen, weaken, or diagnose the flaws in these arguments. It is designed as a definitive reference for the advanced test-taker aiming for mastery of the Logical Reasoning section. The central vulnerability of all LSAT causal arguments stems from the test artificial and rigid model of causality. Unlike real-world cause and effect, which is probabilistic and allows for multi-causal complexity, the LSAT treats causal relationships as existing within a deterministic closed system. When an LSAT author concludes A causes B, they are implicitly assuming that A is the only possible cause of B (a necessary condition) and that the presence of A must guarantee the presence of B (a sufficient condition). This all-or-nothing framework is the arguments Achilles heel. Consequently, the standard methods of attacking causal arguments are not merely suggestions of alternative possibilities; they are direct refutations that falsify the arguments unstated, absolutist assumptions. An alternative cause is devastating not just because it plausible, but because it proves the authors assumed cause is not the only cause. A cause without effect example is fatal because it proves the cause is not invariably sufficient. Mastery of LSAT causation hinges on the swift and accurate deployment of a core set of strategic responses. For the vast majority of causal arguments encountered, three high-yield attack vectors are paramount. These vectors directly target the absolutist assumptions of the LSAT causal model: 1. Introduce an Alternative Cause: Propose a different factor that could be independently responsible for the observed effect. 2. Demonstrate Reversed Causality: Argue that the presumed effect is, in fact, the cause of the presumed cause. 3. Posit a Third Factor: Identify an unmentioned external variable that is the common cause of both the stated cause and the stated effect. Conversely, strengthening these inherently flawed arguments requires buttressing them against these same attacks. The two essential strengthening gambits are: 1. Eliminate Alternatives: Systematically rule out one or more of the three attack vectors. An answer choice that demonstrates an alternative cause is not present, that the causality cannot be reversed, or that a potential third factor is irrelevant will make the original conclusion more likely. 2. Reinforce the Data: Provide evidence that the observed correlation is more robust and less likely to be coincidental. This is achieved by showing additional instances of the cause and effect occurring together, or, more powerfully, by providing a control group scenario where the absence of the cause is met with the absence of the effect. This entire strategic approach can be distilled into a minimal winning toolkit for immediate application. The core mental model is to view every LSAT author making a causal claim as a bad scientist who has leaped to a conclusion from insufficient data. Upon identifying a causal argument, the test-taker should immediately deploy a sequence of critical questions: First What else could explain this to probe for alternative and third-factor causes. Second Could it be the other way around to test for reversed causality. And third How good is this data to initiate an audit of the evidence, particularly when it is presented in the form of a scientific study or survey. This disciplined three-question sequence provides the foundational framework for deconstructing over 80% of all causal reasoning questions on the LSAT. DISCOVERED CAUSATION CLAIM TYPOLOGY. The LSAT employs a diverse range of causal claims, varying in strength, scope, and subtlety. Recognizing the precise type of claim being made is critical, as each possesses unique vulnerabilities and requires a tailored strategic response. Type 1: Absolute / Sole-Cause Claims. This is the most direct, most common, and most fundamentally flawed type of causal claim on the LSAT. It asserts that a single factor is the definitive and exclusive cause of a particular effect. This claim operates within the LSAT default all-or-nothing causal universe, implicitly rejecting the possibility of any other contributing factors or alternative explanations. The argument presents the relationship as simple and deterministic.

These correlations often serve as the basis for an authors causal conclusion, which can be a flaw if not properly supported. What are Statistical Correlations? Statistical correlations describe an association or relationship between two factors based on observed data or proportions. The sources highlight several ways these correlations are presented: 1. Most people in a group share a trait: This involves observing that a significant majority within a particular group possesses a common characteristic. Example: most people in this group have a certain trait college graduates tend to have good vocab. 2. One group is more or less likely to have a trait than another group: This type of correlation compares two distinct groups, noting a difference in the likelihood of possessing a specific trait. Example: college grads are more likely than non-college grads to have sophisticated vocab. 3. Disproportionate representation: This indicates that a certain group is over- or under-represented in a particular category relative to their proportion in the general population. This lumpiness in the statistics often prompts the question why. Example: liberals are only 53 percent of the population but 79 percent of university professors. Such a disparity cues the reader to think about what might be skewing it that way. When you encounter a statistical correlation, it is helpful to picture those two factors appearing in parallel, demonstrating some form of association. Statistical Correlations and Causation. A common flaw in LSAT Logical Reasoning is for an author to infer a cause from a mere correlation, including statistical ones. While a statistical correlation shows an association, it does not automatically prove a causal link. The author is considered overconfident about one specific storyline when there could be multiple ways to explain a correlation. For instance, in the example of college graduates tending to have good vocabulary, an author might conclude that going to college causes advanced vocabulary. However, this causal conclusion is flawed because other explanations exist, such as: Reverse Causality: Perhaps having advanced vocabulary is a prerequisite for getting into college, rather than college causing the vocabulary. One might have needed an advanced vocab to get into college. Third Factor (Common Cause): An unidentified third factor might be responsible for both traits. For example, being from a wealthy family might lead to both advanced vocabulary and a higher likelihood of attending college. Therefore, while statistical correlations are a key type of evidence presented in causal arguments, they must be critically examined for unsupported leaps to causation. The LSAT emphasizes recognizing that the presence of a statistical correlation is not sufficient proof for a specific causal relationship. Temporal correlations: These describe events occurring together in time, either before-after or simultaneously.

MASTERY DOSSIER LSAT CAUSATION AND CORRELATION. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY. Causal reasoning is a cornerstone of the LSAT Logical Reasoning section appearing with high frequency in the most statistically difficult question types particularly Strengthen Weaken and Flaw. Mastery of this domain is not an intellectual luxury but a tactical necessity for achieving an elite score. This dossier provides a definitive framework for deconstructing analyzing and mastering every variant of causal argument tested on the exam. It moves beyond the simplistic correlation does not imply causation mantra to equip the learner with a sophisticated test-ready toolkit. The central insight is that the LSAT tests a stylized and deliberately vulnerable form of causality. LSAT authors when concluding a causal relationship from correlational evidence make a critical implicit assumption the stated cause is the only cause of the effect. This artificial constraint not present in real-world scientific inquiry is the arguments primary weakness and the key to its deconstruction. Every correct answer in a causal reasoning question is designed to either exploit or defend this core vulnerability. To operationalize this a five-part framework for analysis replaces vague notions of alternative cause or reverse causation. Any correlation between A and B leading to a conclusion that A causes B can be evaluated against five distinct possibilities: 1. Direct Causation The author is correct A causes B. 2. Reverse Causation The relationship is flipped B causes A. 3. Common Cause (Confounder) A third factor C causes both A and B. 4. Coincidence The correlation is random with no causal link. 5. Controlled Relationship Evidence shows the effect is present with the cause and absent without the cause strengthening the direct causal claim. This framework is a universal diagnostic tool. Weaken questions introduce possibilities 2 3 or 4. Strengthen questions eliminate possibilities 2 3 or 4 or provide evidence for 5. Flaw questions describe the authors failure to consider 2 3 or 4. By mentally cycling through these scenarios upon identifying a causal conclusion a test-taker can systematically pre-phrase the correct answer for any question type. Beyond this core framework the most advanced causal questions disguise their flaws within the data presented in the premises. These are not mere logical leaps but errors of data literacy. Test-takers must learn to identify and challenge arguments based on: Selection and Survivorship Bias Where the evidence pool is inherently skewed because of how it was selected or because only survivors of a process are observed (e.g. studying only successful companies to find the key to success). Baseline and Denominator Mismatches Where an argument ignores the starting point of a comparison or confuses raw numbers with percentages by ignoring the size of the total group (e.g. concluding a city is more dangerous because it has more accidents while ignoring its larger population). Attrition Effects Where participants dropping out of a long-term study skew the results as those who remain are not representative of the original group. Furthermore a critical distinction must be drawn between causal and conditional reasoning. Causal claims are about real-world probability and temporal sequence whereas conditional statements represent absolute timeless logical guarantees. Misapplying the rules of one to the other is a frequent and fatal error for advanced students. Tactically the process is straightforward read the question stem identify the causal conclusion in the stimulus (via explicit keywords like causes or implicit phenomenon/explanation structures) isolate the specific cause and effect and use the five-scenario framework to anticipate the answer. This systematic approach transforms a complex analytical task into a repeatable checklist-driven process providing the speed and accuracy required for a 170+ score.
