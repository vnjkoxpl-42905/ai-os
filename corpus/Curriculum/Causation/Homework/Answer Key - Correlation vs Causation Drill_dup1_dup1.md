# Answer Key: Correlation vs. Causation Drill

## Section A: Direct Causal & Correlational Claims
*(This section focuses on identifying clear, explicit language.)*

### Question 1
- **Two Associated Factors:** (1) New irrigation technology, (2) Crop yields.
- **Classification:** Causation
- **Walkthrough:** The verb "produced" is a direct and forceful causal word, claiming that the technology actively created the increase.
- **Example Third Factor Explanations:** The year the new technology was implemented also happened to have perfect weather conditions (ideal rainfall and sunlight), which was the real cause of the increased yields. The firm also switched to a new, more potent fertilizer in the same year.
- **Example Reverse Causation:** Unlikely. Higher crop yields cannot cause the implementation of a technology that preceded them.
- **Cause without Effect:** A competing firm adopted the exact same technology but saw no increase. Tested on a small plot the year before, it failed.
- **Effect without Cause:** Farms in regions where the new technology had not been installed also saw a 15% increase. National average for that crop type increased by 15% that year due to unrelated factors.
- **Key Takeaway:** Active, productive verbs like "produced" are clear and unambiguous signals of a causal claim.

### Question 2
- **Two Associated Factors:** (1) Amount of public green space, (2) Average property values.
- **Classification:** Correlation
- **Walkthrough:** The phrase "are also the cities with" simply notes co-occurrence of two characteristics — a static relationship without implying causation.
- **Key Takeaway:** Simple statements of co-existence ("X has Y" or "X is found with Y") are correlational.

### Question 3
- **Two Associated Factors:** (1) The four-day workweek, (2) Employee burnout/job satisfaction.
- **Classification:** Causation
- **Walkthrough:** "Resulted in" is a direct causal connector.
- **Third Factor:** Same time as the workweek change, the company also gave a stock grant and bonus, or hired a new respected CEO.
- **Reverse Causation:** Less plausible — hard to argue that a decrease in burnout caused the policy.
- **Cause without Effect:** European branch implemented the same policy with no metric change. Marketing department test showed no change.
- **Effect without Cause:** Other tech companies in the same city kept five-day weeks and saw similar satisfaction gains. Accounting department excluded from the policy reported the same gains.
- **Key Takeaway:** "Resulted in" clearly indicates a causal relationship.

### Question 4
- **Two Associated Factors:** (1) Popularity of processed foods, (2) Prevalence of food allergies.
- **Classification:** Correlation
- **Walkthrough:** "Strong link" = "strong correlation/association" — a parallel trend without causal claim.
- **Key Takeaway:** "Link," "association," "connection" are clear non-causal indicators.

### Question 5
- **Two Associated Factors:** (1) Metal fatigue, (2) The bridge's structural failure.
- **Classification:** Causation
- **Walkthrough:** "Determinant" is an extremely strong causal word — single, decisive cause.
- **Third Factor:** Sudden, extreme weather event would have caused failure regardless of fatigue.
- **Reverse Causation:** Impossible — failure cannot cause pre-existing fatigue.
- **Cause without Effect:** Identical bridge with similar fatigue has not failed. Engineers calculated fatigue wouldn't cause failure for 20 years.
- **Effect without Cause:** Different bridge of different design and materials collapsed similarly, suggesting a common external event. Bridge had fatigued parts replaced a week before failure.
- **Key Takeaway:** Extremely strong causal words like "determinant" or "sole cause" make the firmest, most attackable causal claims.

## Section B: Evidence-Based Claims & Study Interpretations

### Question 6
- **Factors:** (1) Quality of sleep, (2) Development of chronic illnesses.
- **Classification:** Correlation
- **Walkthrough:** "Were less likely" — classic likelihood structure with passive verb. Statistical observation, not a claim that sleep prevents illness.
- **Key Takeaway:** Locate the "driver" verb; passive "were" signals correlation.

### Question 7
- **Factors:** (1) The fitness tracker program, (2) The city's obesity rate.
- **Classification:** Causation
- **Walkthrough:** "Was directly responsible for" — strong causal indicator assigning agency.
- **Third Factor:** New grocery store opened, or public transit expansion increased walking.
- **Reverse Causation:** Public interest in fitness (effect) led to program funding (cause). Obesity rate already trending down for five years.
- **Cause without Effect:** Same program rolled out in demographically identical neighbor city, no change. Heaviest tracker users were already gym members.
- **Effect without Cause:** Third city without the program also saw 5% decrease. Program cancelled at six months but obesity rate still decreased.
- **Key Takeaway:** "Responsible for" should be treated as a strong causal claim.

### Question 8
- **Factors:** (1) Concentration of community gardens, (2) Crime rates.
- **Classification:** Correlation
- **Walkthrough:** "Tend to have" = statistical pattern, not a productive relationship.
- **Key Takeaway:** General-pattern phrases ("tends to," "is often") are correlation markers.

### Question 9
- **Factors:** (1) Lack of communication, (2) Declining profits.
- **Classification:** Causation
- **Walkthrough:** "Led to" is a strong causal indicator.
- **Third Factor:** New competitor entered the market. Main product became obsolete due to a rival's innovation.
- **Reverse Causation:** Declining profits caused budget cuts and low morale, which destroyed communication. Fear of layoffs made departments secretive.
- **Cause without Effect:** Another industry company with poor communication saw no decline. Company's own history shows poor communication during boom years.
- **Effect without Cause:** Different struggling company with celebrated communication saw similar decline. Department with perfect communication declined more steeply.
- **Key Takeaway:** Causal claims often appear inside an expert's argument or conclusion.

### Question 10
- **Factors:** (1) Participation in internships, (2) Holding a higher-paying job.
- **Classification:** Correlation
- **Walkthrough:** Group comparison ("interns" vs. "non-interns") with passive "are... employed" — observation about group characteristics, not a causal claim.
- **Key Takeaway:** In study-based group comparisons, the verb determines classification.

## Section C: Subtle, Temporal, & Implicit Claims

### Question 11
- **Factors:** (1) Time in unstructured outdoor play, (2) Advanced problem-solving skills.
- **Classification:** Correlation
- **Walkthrough:** Dose-response gradient ("the more X, the more Y") with "tend to be" = statistical tendency, not causal.
- **Key Takeaway:** Even strong dose-response is correlational if described with passive/tendency language.

### Question 12
- **Factors:** (1) Ocean temperature change, (2) Whale migration patterns.
- **Classification:** Causation
- **Walkthrough:** "Influenced" is an active, causal verb.
- **Third Factor:** Change in ocean currents could have altered both temperature and food source. Industrial sonar drove whales away.
- **Reverse Causation:** Impossible — whales cannot change ocean temperature.
- **Cause without Effect:** Different ocean basin had same temperature change but no migration shift. Temperature change was in winter; migration shifted in summer.
- **Effect without Cause:** Same species in stable-temperature areas also shifted. Historical records show 50-year migration cycles regardless of temperature.
- **Key Takeaway:** "Influenced" is softer but still definitively causal.

### Question 13
- **Factors:** (1) Rise of literacy/printing press, (2) A wave of scientific innovation.
- **Classification:** Correlation
- **Walkthrough:** Temporal sequence ("shortly after X, Y happened") — suggests but does not state causation.
- **Key Takeaway:** A sequence is not, by itself, a causal statement. It is correlation often used as evidence for a causal conclusion.

### Question 14
- **Factors:** (1) Inadequate cybersecurity training, (2) The data breach.
- **Classification:** Causation
- **Walkthrough:** "Was partially caused by" — explicit contributory/partial cause claim.
- **Third Factor:** State-sponsored hacking group; flawed security software with backdoor.
- **Reverse Causation:** Impossible — breach cannot cause prior training inadequacy.
- **Cause without Effect:** Many companies with same training have never been breached. Worst-compliance department had zero incidents.
- **Effect without Cause:** Most secure department got breached. Competing world-class-trained company hit by same attack on same day.
- **Key Takeaway:** "A factor in" or "partially caused by" is still a causal claim, not correlation.

### Question 15
- **Factors:** (1) The driver's failure to yield, (2) The multi-car pile-up.
- **Classification:** Causation
- **Walkthrough:** "Initiated" = "to cause to begin" — start of a causal chain.
- **Third Factor:** Black ice; malfunctioning traffic lights showing green both directions.
- **Reverse Causation:** Impossible — pile-up cannot cause prior failure to yield.
