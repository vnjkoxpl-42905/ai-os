# Break the Comparison Homework!

## Debrief

In the world of **Comparisons**, there are several recurring assumptions:

- What's true in this timeframe is true in that timeframe
- What is true today will be true in the future
- What was true for this thing / this group / this person will also be true for that thing / that group / that person

When authors lean on comparisons like this, they are **assuming similarities**. We can **weaken** these arguments by **pointing out salient differences** or **strengthen** them by providing more similarities, affirming the assumed similarity, or ruling out some crucial dissimilarity.

## Try these 8 questions, untimed

Focus on the comparisons being made. The author will be assuming that two timeframes, two groups, two situations, or two subjects are fair to compare.

- **On Flaw & Weaken:** find the difference that "breaks the comparison."
- **On Necessary Assumption & Strengthen:** supply the missing similarity or rule out the key difference.

## Part 2: Comparison Drill

Try problems testing a Comparison reasoning structure. There are many different question types here, but every stimulus has some form of comparison/contrast thought process (some take a lot more effort to see).

### Comparisons manifest in many forms:

1. This Option vs. That Option
2. This Group vs. That Group
3. Plan-World vs. Current World
4. Future-World vs. Current World
5. Past-World vs. Current World
6. Sample vs. General Population
7. Part vs. Whole
8. % vs. #
9. Completing an Analogy
10. Assuming a specific Similarity
11. Assuming a specific Difference

As you're doing the problems, just focus on answering them accurately. When you review your answers, pause and ask:

> "How is the author thinking in terms of some comparison or contrast?"
