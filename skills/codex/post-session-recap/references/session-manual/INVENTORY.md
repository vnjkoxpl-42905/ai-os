## E. Voice patterns

**Openings**
- A plain claim that frames the whole topic: "A flaw question is not one task." (2) · "Every conditional is one sentence with a trigger and a requirement." (10) · "Every flaw answer on this test is one of about a dozen moves." (8)
- A definition grounded in a small concrete set: "Suppose a class has been assigned a case to read. These are three different claims about that same class" (12) · "Every trained person has a badge. Start with that rule." (R6 V10)
- Continuity with the session or the week: "Yesterday you were reading conditionals that were handed to you. Today the conditional is missing and you have to supply it." (5)
- Letter openers (now retired) lead with a result and a verdict: "Necessary assumptions today, start to finish ... You got nearly all of them" (R2).

**How traps are taught**
- Name the trap as a label, in bold, then say exactly what it does: "The trap that dominates this set is **No Impact**. A choice sits next to the topic, sounds informed, and changes nothing about why the result happened." (3)
- Show the near-miss beside the right answer: "A nearly perfect answer with one wrong word, sitting next to a correct answer worded so loosely it feels underwhelming." (2)
- Say why the trap works: "A trigger and an outcome often share words ... Overlapping words are not the same idea." (9a)
- A test the student can run in seconds: "write the claim both ways and build the same sentence out of something you already have an opinion about. You need a great LSAT score to get into a great law school. Does a great LSAT score guarantee you get in? Obviously not" (6)
- Later recaps list traps as "Watch for an answer that:" bullets (R7) or "Two tempting changes would overstate the evidence" (R13).

**How examples are chosen**
- Everyday, low-stakes and visual: a gate (9a), a bakery "probably open" vs "definitely open" (9a), oxygen and a match (5), the rooster and the sun (6), standing desks in two offices (6), a shelf of eight books (12), a ten-member club (12), Toronto by train (R3), tulip prices (R7).
- LSAT-flavoured self-reference used sparingly: "If you score a 160, you get the scholarship." (10)
- One example carried through a section rather than many: the gate runs through all of 9a's Type table; the shelf runs through 12.
- In recaps, real session questions are used only when they carry a reusable rule (Parker in R12, democracy/media in R9).

**How praise is given**
- Specific, tied to a behaviour, and short: "You did this on the technology question when you leaned toward the choice that said 'unlikely' ... That instinct is correct. Keep using it." (2)
- Change over time rather than correctness: "You came in reading an argument as the issue being presented. By the second question you were pulling the conclusion out first" (11)
- Calm "nothing slipped" lines: "Nothing slipped in a way that worried me." (R2) · "That is the only thing I would flag, and it is small." (R1)
- In concept recaps praise nearly disappears; R13 keeps one line: "On a hard question, you identified the premise-to-conclusion bridge and selected the answer that supplied it."

**Closings**
- Letter recaps: schedule line, then "Best, / Joshua".
- References close on a practice habit or a reminder: "Write one line on why the loser lost." (2) · "> **REMEMBER:** Pick your mode, tag the conclusion, pre-phrase what the answer must sound like." (9b) · "Do it for a week and it stops being a checklist. It becomes how you see the question." (9b)
- Cheat sheets close on Test yourself, then a link to the next note ("Next: The Breakdown →").
- Concept recaps close on the checkbox list; no sign-off.

**Recurring sentence moves**
