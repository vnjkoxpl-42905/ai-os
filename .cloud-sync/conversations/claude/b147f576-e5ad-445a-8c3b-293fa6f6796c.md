252	1. Read the ordinary meaning.
253	2. Apply it to a case.
254	3. Identify the sufficient and necessary ideas.
255	4. Compare an unsupported reverse.
256	5. Reword the same relationship.
257	6. Show the diagram after its meaning is understood.
258	
259	Introduce a fresh example for independent practice. The learner should not be able to answer only because the lesson already solved that sentence.
260	
261	A repeated “Keep this” slogan without new reasoning is not explanatory repetition. Nor is rendering the same instructions twice in different panels. See the demonstrated corrections in [COPY-CORRECTIONS](../wiki/COPY-CORRECTIONS.md).

263	## Metaphor gives the reader a usable handle
264	
265	The source uses doors, families, activation, burdens, brackets, and putting yourself in a condition's shoes. Each metaphor gives an abstract operation a physical or social handle. A small set of terms recurs, so the reader can keep using it.
266	
267	Transfer the method of choosing a metaphor, not the particular inventory. Prefer a familiar image that helps answer a logical question. Explain its literal meaning immediately. Remove it when it adds more things to remember than it clarifies.
268	
269	For instance, a ticket can illustrate a requirement only if the rule is stated carefully. “A ticket is required to enter” does not say a ticket guarantees entry. An analogy fails if it smuggles in that reverse.
270	
271	Do not build a competing metaphor for every sentence. Do not invent branded names for every skill. The standard technical terms remain the stable vocabulary.

273	## Humor reduces tension without changing the rule
274	
275	The source's coffee obsession makes an unusual absolute rule memorable. Its reluctant-student aside acknowledges the experience of being asked to guess. The imagined green checkmark gives the reader's desire for certainty a recognizable shape.
276	
277	Humor can occur inside an explanation when it makes the thought easier to understand. Joshua's source selection supersedes the earlier blanket restriction that wit must stay outside the explanation. It does not authorize mockery, forced slang, exaggerated praise, or an expanding comic story.
278	
279	Use a light aside only when it helps the reader inhabit a situation or relax around a specific mistake. Keep it optional. A paragraph should still teach clearly if the aside is removed. Do not use the source's distinctive jokes as templates to be copied.
280	
281	Never turn first-person source anecdotes into Joshua's biography. “I ask every student this” requires that Joshua actually does. “Students always learn better this way” requires evidence or a narrower, truthful statement.

283	## Emphasis and visuals identify the decision
284	
285	Bold in the source often marks the rule, an inference, a warning, or an instruction. Italics distinguish examples and sometimes emphasize a word that changes the contrast. A table lets the reader compare controlled variations. A STOP asks the reader to attempt something. A side note answers a local question without interrupting the main line for everyone.
286	
287	For our material:
288	
289	- Bold the relationship or phrase that controls the next decision. Do not bold most of a paragraph.
290	- Give an example its own space. The student should be able to find the sentence currently under discussion.
291	- Put comparisons side by side when the same rule or wording is being contrasted.
292	- Reveal diagram construction one meaningful step at a time. Explain each placement in English.
293	- Preserve a readable text equivalent for every visual. The illustration is an explanation, not a substitute for one.
294	- Make an interactive pause real. Let the learner choose when to reveal the reasoning.
295	- Give a side note a specific job: answer a question, define new notation, or qualify advice. Avoid a second full lesson in a callout.
296	
297	Joshua's notation correction still applies: teaching arrows should look like teaching notation, not little implementation-code boxes. Clear ordinary typography and meaningful labels should communicate the relationship.
298	
299	The source's adjusted HTML design is not a visual brand template. Our bootcamp should preserve the established Intro to LR student flow while using original visuals that serve the current concept. Student pages show the assigned work and the lesson itself; internal stage planning, assigned-start metadata, production notes, and selectable difficulty controls do not belong in the teaching prose.

302	
303	This map records what the supplied excerpt does in order. It is not an assertion that every future lesson should cover every listed topic.
304	
305	### 1. The relationship comes before its parts
306	
307	**The Art of the If/Then**, HTML lines 142 to 154, opens with a plain definition, a single familiar rule, a literal interpretation, several statements of certainty, and a reason to understand the relationship beneath varied language. The transition promises to take the same example apart.
308	
309	The depth comes from making “must” meaningful before presenting a list of words. The reader learns what kind of relationship they are looking for. The source also connects certainty to prior “Power Players” material, which is not included in this attachment. Our standalone introduction must supply any needed context itself.
310	
311	**Transfer:** first explain what a conditional says and why its direction matters. Then give a concrete example. Do not open with a diagramming task or make the definition a label attached after a quiz.
312	
313	### 2. Sufficient is explained through presence and absence
314	
315	**The Sufficient Condition**, lines 156 to 199, contains a definition, the recurring anchor, a metaphor, the possibility that the sufficient condition does not occur, a contrast between its absence and presence, and a clarification of “activation.” It then shows **eight indicator rows with eleven displayed example forms**, all expressing the same anchor relationship, and explains why language recognition cannot replace understanding.
316	
317	This is more than “sufficient goes left.” The text develops what the condition does, what it does not establish, and how wording changes around it.
318	
319	**Transfer:** give sufficient its own explanation, contrasting cases, and nearby practice. A left-side label is useful only after “enough for what?” has a clear answer.
320	
321	### 3. Necessary is developed as a requirement
322	
323	**The Necessary Condition**, lines 201 to 230, defines the other part, reuses the same rule, explains the requirement, and shows **ten indicator rows with eleven displayed forms**. It compares the varied wording with the sufficient-family statements and gives special attention to “only if.” It ends by explaining why symbols help isolate the relationship.
324	
325	The source uses the ordinary force of “necessary,” “required,” and “need” to make the technical term familiar. It does not jump directly to a memorized side rule.
326	
327	**Transfer:** explain what is required and why having a requirement is not the same as satisfying every condition for the result. Teach “if” and “only if” using a controlled comparison. Keep the target of each expression clear.
328	
329	### 4. The contrapositive is discovered before it is manipulated
330	
331	**The Contrapositive**, lines 234 to 285, first recovers the original relationship. It introduces a new coffee/walking thought experiment, removes the required condition, asks for a prediction, and explicitly pauses. The answer is derived from the unmet requirement. The term is then named, the original anchor revisited, and a four-situation chart sets limits on inference.
332	
333	The order is important: meaning, prediction, reason, name, comparison. The flip-and-negate shortcut appears later, after the reader has a reason to believe the result.
334	
335	**Transfer:** preserve this order when contraposition is actually in scope. Do not rush the full procedure into the opening definition merely because it appears early in this source.
336	
337	### 5. Notation is introduced as a solution to varied wording
338	
339	**How to Diagram Conditionals**, lines 289 to 388, recovers the indicator families, explains the arrow, gives a four-step construction procedure, and builds one diagram in stages. It then explains negation, displays four original/contrapositive sign patterns, gives the shortcut, and distinguishes writing a contrapositive from reading one when needed.
340	
341	The first diagram is narrated: notice the indicator, place its target, then complete the other side. Its final appearance is not used as evidence that the learner understands how to make it.
342	
343	**Transfer:** show each operation and its reason. Preserve Joshua's method and meaningful labels rather than copying the source's four-step count or abbreviation rule.

345	### 6. Practical objections receive substantial answers
346	
347	**Quick Conditional Diagramming FAQ**, lines 390 to 433, has five questions:
348	
349	| Reader question | Depth of the answer | Reusable teaching value |
350	|---|---|---|
351	| When should I diagram? | Three paragraphs distinguish early training from later judgment, discuss difficult wording and chains, and address avoidance caused by uncertainty. | Give a criterion, a reason, and an honest account of the learning process. |
352	| How do I know the diagram is correct? | Three paragraphs connect confidence with checked work and studying specific errors. | Teach a verification habit instead of offering confidence as a substitute for one. |
353	| Does diagramming take too long? | Two paragraphs explain the proposed tradeoff and separate learning from speed. | Acknowledge the concern; explain why the slower practice step is useful now. |
354	| Should I always write the contrapositive? | Two paragraphs qualify the advice. A separate note explains why the drills still require it. | Distinguish training evidence from the eventual performance habit. |
355	| How should I abbreviate? | Gives a short example, a complete diagram, and advice about compact labels. | Make the notation choice purposeful and readable. Joshua's label guidance governs where it differs. |

357	This is unusually important to the overall tone. The writer gives practical uncertainty enough room to be answered. It is not squeezed into one generic reassurance.

359	### 7. A useful shortcut is shown, then limited
360	
361	**Figuring Out The Language**, lines 437 to 471, defines an indicator's target and brackets the target in familiar sentences. It first shows the common placement immediately after the indicator. Then it gives five examples in which the target comes before the indicator, followed by three paired wording variations showing that the same indicator can switch position.
362	
363	The sequence deliberately prevents an attractive mechanical generalization. The reader sees why adjacency often helps and why it cannot replace reading the sentence.
364	
365	**Transfer:** teach the indicator's complete target. Change clause order while holding meaning steady. Include one placement trap only after the usual case is understood. Mark the obscured final line as missing evidence.
366	
367	### 8. A translation is checked by trying both interpretations
368	
369	**Your Friend, the Personalized If/Then**, lines 475 to 508, defines the check, explains its purpose, gives four steps, establishes the ordinary meaning of a new sentence, and works through a correct guess. It then deliberately tries the reversed guess and explains why that meaning fails.
370	
371	This adds a verification strategy after notation. It also models the experience of being uncertain without abandoning the task. The teacher does not merely say “use your intuition”; the reader sees how to test an interpretation.
372	
373	**Transfer:** use the imagined situation as a meaning check. Preserve who or what the sentence concerns. Personalization is optional if pretending to be an object makes the wording less natural.
374	
375	### 9. Practice changes the language while keeping the skill
376	
377	**Basic Conditional Drill**, lines 512 to 563, gives supportive instructions and six themed items, with original and contrapositive diagrams displayed in the supplied HTML. The task is the same across varied sentence forms. The source says the adapted sentences come from Shakespeare and points to an answer key not included here.
378	
379	**Transfer:** original practice can have a memorable theme, but it should change only what the learner is ready to handle. Reuse the skill, not the source's exercise wording. Show answers after an attempt and explain why each diagram preserves the complete ideas. Do not treat the displayed annotations as an independently verified answer key.

Their displayed diagrams also lack step-by-step explanations. Our Group 1/2 practice needs broader expression coverage and reasoned feedback; the source's basic set alone would not establish that coverage.

425	
426	1. **Define before demonstrating.** Start by explaining what a conditional says. Let the example make the definition concrete.
427	2. **Explain the purpose through a decision.** Show how direction changes the conclusion the learner may draw. Avoid unsupported prevalence statistics.
428	3. **Keep one relationship stable while changing the view.** Ordinary sentence, case fact, roles, wording variation, and diagram should fit together.
429	4. **Make each pause a useful thinking task.** Ask for a prediction or explanation, then reveal the reason.

431	6. **Teach the target before the placement shortcut becomes a habit.** Complete ideas and their qualifications matter.
432	7. **Explain why the drill exists.** The learner should know what ability they are practicing.
433	8. **Use fresh practice.** Worked examples teach the method; new items show whether the method transfers.

435	10. **Keep later content in its proper lesson.** Contraposition, chains, Groups 3/4, and quantities receive their own development; a quality reference does not override prerequisites.

437	## Accuracy and method boundaries
438	
439	Writing that sounds like the source must still preserve correct logic and Joshua's chosen method.
440	
441	| Source simplification or context | Boundary for our material |
442	|---|---|
443	| “Ignore the conditional” when sufficient is absent or necessary is present. | For this one rule and fact, no new conclusion about the other condition follows. The rule remains available for later facts and chains. Never teach that it disappears. |
444	| Necessary has to happen “at some point.” | A conditional is not necessarily a time sequence or causal claim. Prefer “has to be true if the sufficient condition is true.” |
445	| The original and contrapositive are “all you know.” | The central warning is against an unsupported reverse or inverse. Equivalent formulations and other supplied rules still matter. State the immediate inference limits precisely. |
446	| Required words are described as obligations. | Preserve modality. “Interviewees must bring ID” can assert an obligation, not prove compliance. Diagram the stated requirement unless the facts establish compliance. |
447	| Indicator usually comes before target. | Read its full grammatical target. Targets can precede the indicator, and familiar words can serve other roles in context. |
448	| Shorten conditions to one or two letters, never more than four. | Joshua's clear condensed labels govern. Keep negation, scope, qualifiers, and readability. A short label that loses meaning is not an improvement. |
449	| Try a personalized sufficient guess. | Compare with the whole ordinary meaning and look for a counterexample. A feeling that an arrow is plausible is not enough. |
450	| Original and contrapositive training. | Explain why a drill requires explicit work and when test-day practice may differ. Do not turn a training requirement into a universal performance rule. |
451	| “Always” is in a necessary-family teaching list. | Establish the sentence's subject and relationship. It is not a universal instruction to place the next word on the right. |
452	| Confidence guarantees the diagram is correct. | Confidence can grow from practice, but correctness rests on preserving meaning and valid reasoning. Provide checks. |
453	| Timing and learning claims are absolute. | Treat them as the author's advice unless independently supported. Give a practical explanation without invented evidence. |
454	| References to Logic Games, a book, page 148, or named source tools. | Preserve the teaching function only where our course has an actual equivalent. Do not import obsolete or unavailable context. |
455	| A theme makes a drill memorable. | Keep each sentence logically interpretable and its answer reviewed. Novelty must not obscure the condition or require untaught knowledge. |
456	
457	For the parcel example, three possible cases distinguish the permitted relationship from an invalid reverse:
458	
459	- Blue sticker and inspected: fits the rule.
460	- Inspected without a blue sticker: also fits the rule.
461	- Blue sticker without inspection: breaks the rule.
462	
463	The rule does not itself say any such parcel exists. Examples of possible cases illustrate consistency, not existence claims. This distinction matters when moving from individual facts to general rules.

465	## Calibration examples for different LSAT surfaces
466	
467	All examples below are original drafts for this reference. They demonstrate writing mechanisms; they are not assigned work or approved curriculum.
468	
469	### Explain sufficient and necessary without rushing
470	
471	> Every parcel with a blue sticker has been inspected.
472	>
473	> Suppose yours has a blue sticker. That's enough to know it has been inspected. You don't need another fact about the parcel. The rule has already told you what must be true of every parcel with that sticker.
474	>
475	> We call the blue sticker the **sufficient condition**. Sufficient means enough. Here, enough to establish inspection under this rule.
476	>
477	> Inspection is the **necessary condition**. A blue-sticker parcel has to meet that requirement. Otherwise it would be an exception to a rule that allows no exceptions.
478	>
479	> Now suppose a parcel has been inspected. Does it have to have a blue sticker?
480	>
481	> We still don't know. Inspection was required for the blue-sticker parcels. The shop never said those were the only parcels it inspected.
482	
483	This returns to meaning at every term. The example does not assert why the shop uses stickers or when they are applied. Those extra facts are unnecessary.
484	
485	### Teach a necessary expression through its whole meaning
486	
487	> Editing this newsletter requires an account.
488	>
489	> What does the sentence say the editor needs? An account. Keep that complete idea on the required side of the relationship:
490	>
491	> **Edits this newsletter → Has an account**
492	>
493	> Read it back: if someone edits this newsletter, they have an account. That matches the sentence.
494	>
495	> Try the reverse. If someone has an account, do they necessarily edit the newsletter? The sentence hasn't told us that. An account could belong to someone who only reads it.
496	
497	The final possible counterexample is introduced as something consistent with the sentence, not an asserted fact about this system. Technical placement has a semantic reason.
498	
499	### Give a flashcard a reasoning task
500	
501	**Front**
502	
503	> Every package in Locker 4 has been scanned. A package has been scanned. Must it be in Locker 4? Why?
504	
505	**Back**
506	
507	> No. The rule tells us about packages already in Locker 4. It doesn't say every scanned package goes there.
508	
509	This is more useful than a card that only asks “Necessary condition = which side?” Keep simple recall cards when recall is the actual skill; do not mistake that for complete reasoning practice.
510	
511	### Make feedback identify the missed connection
512	
513	> You used the sentence in the reverse direction. “Only registered visitors enter the archive” requires registration for entry. It hasn't said every registered visitor enters. Read the diagram as **Enters → Registered** and compare it with the sentence.
514	
515	The feedback names the mistake, shows the relevant meaning, and gives a repair. It avoids generic praise, mockery, and labels such as “weak reasoning.”
516	
517	### Explain an ordinary learning objection
518	
519	> “I understood the sentence. Why write it down?”
520	>
521	> With one short rule, you may not need to. When a passage gives you several relationships, a diagram lets you compare them without rereading the wording each time. In this exercise, write the relationship so we can check that you kept its direction and complete ideas.
522	
523	The answer gives a decision criterion and distinguishes the purpose of this exercise from a blanket rule about every test question.
524	
525	### Transfer the voice to another LSAT topic
526	
527	> A conclusion is the claim the author is asking you to accept. The other statements are supposed to support it.
528	>
529	> Nora says, “The library should stay open later because every evening seat was taken this week.” What is she trying to persuade us of?
530	>
531	> That the library should stay open later. The full seats are her reason. She expects that reason to support the proposed change.
532	>
533	> You don't have to agree with her. First, separate what she wants you to accept from what she gives you in support.
534	
535	The same mechanism works outside conditionals: definition, small example, question, explanation, and a useful boundary. No conditional topic has been imported into the conclusion lesson.

537	## Revise the draft in a useful order
538	
539	### First, check the reasoning and scope
540	
541	List what is given, what follows, and why. Try to construct a case that satisfies the given information but makes the proposed conclusion false. If that case is possible, revise the logic before polishing the voice. Check modality, negation, quantifier scope, and whether the learner has actually been taught every required operation.
542	
543	For a rewrite, preserve Joshua's established method and explicit lesson scope. The new reference governs delivery. It does not authorize removing content that students need or adding source topics the current assignment does not cover.
544	
545	### Then, check the learner's experience
546	
547	Read as someone who has never seen the notation. Can they tell what the concept is? Can they answer the first question with what the page has taught? Does each new term refer to an idea they have seen? Does the page tell them why the correct answer follows?
548	
549	Put the explanation beside the point where it is needed. Do not rely on an interaction to imply a distinction the prose never explains. A student who cannot use the animation must still be able to learn from the text.
550	
551	### Then, listen to the voice
552	
553	Read aloud at an ordinary teaching pace. Replace stiff phrasing with the words the teacher would naturally use. Keep the intermediate thoughts. Vary sentence length where the task changes. Remove repeated catchphrases, inflated enthusiasm, branded metaphors, and irrelevant asides.
554	
555	Do not repair generic prose by scattering “Cool,” “Let's,” or “That's it” through it. Repair the missing reasoning, the abrupt transition, or the abstract example.
556	
557	### Finally, verify the student surface
558	
559	Check the actual prompt, answer state, labels, buttons, diagrams, and explanations. A good note can be undermined by a shell that exposes internal planning or reveals the answer too soon. Preview desktop and mobile when the interface changes. Keep the instructor's planning map separate from what the learner sees.

561	## Use this rubric before handing over LSAT writing
562	
563	Score each dimension from 0 to 2: **0** absent or misleading; **1** partly present but needing revision; **2** clear and usable. This is an editorial tool, not a validated learning measure. A logical error or scope violation requires revision regardless of the total.
564	
565	| Dimension | What a strong draft does |
566	|---|---|
567	| **Ordinary meaning** | Defines the idea in language the reader can understand before notation. |
568	| **Concrete anchor** | Uses an original, small, unambiguous situation and states needed assumptions. |
569	| **Visible reasoning** | Shows the intermediate connection instead of asserting a result. |
570	| **Point of view** | Keeps the reader involved; uses teacher narration purposefully. |
571	| **Cadence** | Gives complex thoughts room and short conclusions a clear place to land. |
572	| **Vocabulary** | Keeps useful technical terms, explains them, and avoids internal jargon. |
573	| **Questions and pauses** | Asks a genuine decision and gives an appropriate chance to answer. |
574	| **Boundaries** | Shows what the information leaves open and why a tempting move fails. |
575	| **Progression** | Each operation has a prerequisite, a reason to appear, and adequate practice. |
576	| **Transfer** | Fresh questions require the taught skill, with reasoned feedback. |
577	| **Tone and warmth** | Expects effort, supports the next action, and never mocks uncertainty. |
578	| **Presentation** | Examples, comparisons, notation, and interactions clarify the reasoning. |
579	
580	Do not treat a high total as proof of an identical voice. Use the original source and Joshua's response to calibrate the subjective feel. His concrete corrections override a score.
