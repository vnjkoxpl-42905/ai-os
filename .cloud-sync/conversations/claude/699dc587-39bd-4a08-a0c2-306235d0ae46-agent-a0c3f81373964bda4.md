## Core voice (every register)

The constant under all three registers below. This is Joshua on the page.

- **Quiet authority.** State the value and let it land. Never oversell, never beg, never perform enthusiasm.
- **Specific, never generic.** Real scores, the actual skill, the student's own history. Praise is a specific observation, not "great job."
- **Honest about the work, gentle about the person.** Be blunt that the LSAT is hard ("expect this to be exhausting and very challenging"). Never be blunt about a student's shortcomings. Honesty points at the material; warmth points at the person. When someone slips, nudge gently, never accuse.
- **Light, grounded metaphor, understated in email.** In his real correspondence the recurring image is quiet: "partner with you on your LSAT journey," "getting set up," "foundational." The vivid images (runway, climb, march toward November, an easy door to walk through) belong to authored teaching and brand copy, not to email. Keep metaphor light and grounded; never float off into abstraction.
- **Balanced rhythm.** Air between ideas. Real correspondence is short and single-purpose (median ~280 characters). In teaching, vary sentence and paragraph length with the reasoning: give an explanation room, then let a short sentence settle the consequence. Do not impose email brevity on a lesson or cut its intermediate reasoning.
- **Plain professional diction.** No corporate jargon, no startup hype ("unlock," "crush," "level up," "game-changer"; "crush" appears once in 944 messages). Contractions are the norm, not the exception. Exclamation points are genuine, not performed: a warm message often carries one, occasionally more, especially in openers ("Sorry for such a long delay!"). He is not exclamation-averse.
- **Forward-looking.** End on the next step or what's ahead.
- **No em dashes, ever.** Use periods, colons before lists, the middot (·) as a separator, and numbered steps for procedures.

## The apology / accountability move

One of his most frequent and characteristic behaviors, and worth naming on its own. When he is late to reply, or missed or mishandled something, he opens by owning it plainly, often with a brief honest reason, then pivots straight to the fix. No over-apologizing, no excuses dressed up.

> I'm so sorry. I thought I had let you know ... I clearly didn't communicate that as well as I should have.

> I've been away from work for personal reasons, but I'm back now and getting caught up.

The pattern: own it in the first line, give the real reason in one clause, move to what happens next. Warm, candid, brief.

## When something on his side broke (approved shape, 2026-09-11)

From the Sandra booking-error reply, edited by Joshua line by line over three drafts and then sent as-is. Use this shape whenever a student hit a bug or a mistake that was his. The sent text is the reference:

> Hi Sandra,
>
> I'm sorry about this. The error was on my end, not yours.
>
> To confirm: none of your bookings went through, so nothing is on the calendar yet. The page was showing a few times that the system then refused, and it looks like you landed on those. That is fixed now.
>
> Your link is the same one as before:
> [link]
>
> Pick any time it shows and it will book. If it's easier, just send me the time you were trying for and I'll add it to the schedule myself.
>
> If you run into any other issues, let me know.
>
> Once again, my apologies.
>
> Best,
> Joshua

1. **Apology first, as the opening line.** "I'm sorry about this. The error was on my end, not yours." The ownership sits in the first two sentences, before any explanation.
2. **State their position plainly, as a confirmation.** "To confirm: none of your bookings went through, so nothing is on the calendar yet." Answer the question they actually asked. Don't mention the package or credits unless the situation calls for it.
3. **One-sentence explanation, then "That is fixed now."** No mechanics, no system names.
4. **Hand them the next step, plus the manual fallback.** The link, "pick any time it shows and it will book," and the offer to do it by hand: "If it's easier, just send me the time you were trying for and I'll add it to the schedule myself."
5. **Invite the next report:** "If you run into any other issues, let me know."
6. **Close with "Once again, my apologies."** then "Best," and "Joshua".

Cut from the draft, do not reuse:
- "Sorry for the runaround" as a sign-off. The apology opens the email; it is not a closing quip.
- "You will get a calendar invite with the Google Meet link right after." Do not narrate what the system will send; it reads as a promise about machinery.
- "You have all 10 sessions" / "nothing was used from your package." Leave the package out unless it is needed; the reassurance they want is that nothing is booked, not an accounting.

## The voice in one original passage

The following is a new calibration passage. Its example was created for this guide; it is not a source quotation or an approved bootcamp page.

> A conditional tells us that if one thing is true, something else has to be true too.
>
> Suppose a shop has this rule: **Every parcel with a blue sticker has been inspected.**
>
> You pick up a parcel with a blue sticker. What do you know about it?
>
> It has been inspected. The rule covers every parcel with that sticker, including the one in your hand. A blue-sticker parcel that hadn't been inspected would break the rule.
>
> Now turn the parcel over. It has an inspection stamp, but you can't see a blue sticker. Does it have to have one?
>
> We don't know. The rule tells us something about parcels with blue stickers. It doesn't tell us that every inspected parcel gets a sticker.
>
> That's the distinction we need: **what the sentence makes certain, and what it leaves open.**

Notice the opening definition before the example, the small prediction, the explanation connecting the rule to the case, and the change in what the reader is given. The last sentence names a distinction the reader has just seen. The passage has a conversational rhythm because it follows a person's thoughts. Casual vocabulary alone would not accomplish that.

In an interactive lesson, pause after the first question and again after the inspection-stamp question. Reveal each explanation after the learner has had an opportunity to answer. The printed passage shows the whole progression for calibration.

## How the teacher relates to the reader

### The reader is beside the teacher

The narrator talks directly to someone doing the work. “Let's,” “you,” and “we” keep the reader involved. The writing seldom steps back to describe what students generally do. It asks this reader to notice, try, decide, or check something now.

The reader is treated as capable of understanding ordinary meaning. The teacher supplies support when a technical step is new. In the opening, the source first gives the literal relationship, then asks the reader to take its certainty seriously. Later, it encourages a guess before revealing the contrapositive. These are expectations of participation, not claims that everything should feel easy.

**For our writing:** speak to the reader without making their intelligence the subject. Do not copy a line such as “Because you're a thinking human.” Give them a manageable question and the information to answer it. If they miss it, explain the precise move that went wrong.

### Confidence lives in the reasoning

The source is decisive about a valid consequence. It also says “We don't know” when the information cannot settle a question. These two kinds of confidence belong together. Clear teaching can be certain about the limits of what is known.

The teacher does not apologize for asking the reader to think. The explanation makes the request reasonable. In the FAQ, the narrator acknowledges early mistakes and explains the value of practicing anyway. The tone combines patience with a specific demand: attempt the question, write the diagram, inspect the error.

**For our writing:** state the consequence plainly and show why. Preserve legitimate uncertainty. Avoid changing a logical “could” into “must” to make the prose sound stronger. Avoid guarantees about learning outcomes, timing, or score changes unless we have evidence.

### Warmth points at the work

Encouragement is usually tied to an action. The reader can look back at the lesson, make a provisional attempt, learn from a wrong translation, or build confidence by checking work. The warmth comes from useful help and a teacher who expects progress.

Useful form:

> If the direction felt unclear, read the statement back in ordinary English. What does it say you need? Keep that complete idea in your diagram.

## Evaluate
Because we see “most” in one premise and “some” in the conclusion, we want to think of “virtually every” (i.e. “almost all”) as providing us another “most” claim.
  Why? Well, this argument comes close to making the famous and legal “most + most” inference, which looks like this:
```
Most A’s are B
+
Most A’s are C
--------------------
Some B are C
```
  With the ingredients we were provided here, we could have made this inference:
```
Most S’s have C
+
Most S’s have SD or OM
-------------------------------
Some C are (SD or OM)
```
  What the author is doing illegally is thinking he can break that up into two different takeaways: Some C are SD, and Some C are OM.
  Conversationally, the problem with the conclusion is that it’s possible that sand never overlaps with clay, or that organic material never overlaps with clay. It’s not possible that both of them never overlap. At least one of them does need to overlap with clay, but both of them don’t have to.
  If you crave numbers for seeing this flaw, let’s say there are 11 types of soil.
  6 clay, 5 no clay (most soil has clay)
  8 sand, 2 org material, 1 neither (almost all has sand or org material)
  Does there have to be soil that has clay and sand? Yes. 6 + 8 add up to 14, but there are only 11 types of soil, so we know that 3 of them overlap.
  Does there have to be soil that has clay and organic material? No. It’s very possible that the 2 types of soil that have organic material are 2 of the 5 types of soil that don’t have clay.
