83	What actually separates them, in order of how reliably it predicted my judgment:
84	
85	1. **The Evaluate step argues with the argument.** Strong ones do real work before the choices: they build the objection ("Given that X, how could we argue not-Y?"), do the arithmetic (25% of $400 vs 13% of $1,000), or set the two populations side by side. Loose ones restate the stimulus and move on.
86	2. **The Goal names the answer, not the task.** Strong: "look for an answer saying the fish in the leafless trees are exposed to predators that hunt by sight." Loose: "Find the flaw that mistakes a response to danger for a cause", "Identify an answer choice that supports fairness." Strong Goals often give two acceptable shapes (a linking answer or a defender).
87	3. **Every wrong answer gets a reason tied to this question, and usually a test.** The loose group's signature is the verdict-only note: "This is irrelevant.", "That is unnecessary.", "The argument never discusses profits." Strong notes say what the answer claims, what was needed, and where they diverge, often by running the family's test on it (negate it; would it weaken; is the trigger established; does the conclusion match the rule's right side).
88	4. **The popular trap gets the most attention.** Strong explanations spend their longest wrong-answer note on the choice that drew 15-30% of students and say why it tempts. Loose ones treat it like every other wrong answer.
89	5. **The correct-answer note explains the fit, and often generalizes it.** Strong: "Another correct answer performing the same function could have said..." (PT146-S2-Q13); the karaoke/Jenga reason why A → C still works when you predicted A and B → C (PT139-S4-Q21). Loose: "This is the flaw."
90	6. **Fidelity.** Two loose items are long. Length did not save them: a misquoted answer choice, imported outside facts, or the author disowning the question destroys model value regardless of word count.
91	
92	What did not separate them: word count by itself (PT152-S4-Q19 is 479 words and strong; PT158-S2-Q16 is 716 and loose), the choice-tag vocabulary, and the presence of jokes (several strong ones joke; it only hurts when the joke is at the question's or the answer's expense).

95	
96	Gate (hard requirement): full Argument breakdown and all five choice notes. Every shortlisted candidate passed.

99	
100	| Criterion | 2 | 1 | 0 |
101	|---|---|---|---|
102	| **P** Prediction | Goal names the content or shape of the right answer before the choices (may give two acceptable shapes) | Goal names the task or admits it cannot predict | No prediction |
103	| **X** Precision | Names the exact gap, flaw or relationship in this argument's words | Uses the right label but stays generic | Label only, or wrong |
104	| **E** Eliminations | Every wrong answer gets a question-grounded reason; the popular trap gets the most attention | Most do; one or two verdict-only notes | Verdict-only notes |
105	| **T** Transfer | States the move in a form that works on the next question of this type (the test, the rule, the recurring trap) | Transfer is implicit | None |
106	| **C** Clarity | Plain, no padding, no pasted repetition | Some padding or pasted notes | Hard to follow |
107	| **L** Learner value | Teaches what a student needs to understand and repeat the move: why the trap tempts, what to do when stuck, a worked example or analogy, a second correct answer that would also work | Justifies the key well but does not teach the move | Only justifies the key |

113	## 5. Step structure per family (DB, most common sequences)
114	
115	Labels normalized (parenthetical indicator words dropped: "Conclusion (therefore)" counts as Conclusion).
116	
117	- **Flaw (524):** Conclusion > Evidence > Evaluate > Goal (67%); Evidence > Conclusion > Evaluate (9%); with an Intermediate Conclusion step (5%). Evaluate usually names the famous flaw or builds the objection; Goal often gives both the formal and the "fails to consider" phrasing.
118	- **Must Be True / Most Supported (462):** Statements > Evaluate > Goal (45%); Statements only (16%). Statements is often a diagram. Evaluate scans for conditional, causal, math and pivot wording and names the inference to make.
119	- **Necessary Assumption (352):** Conclusion > Evidence > Evaluate > Goal (66%). Goal typically offers a linking answer and a defender answer, and says "negate it".
120	- **Weaken (335) / Strengthen (277):** Conclusion > Evidence > Evaluate > Goal (59% / 55%). Evaluate uses "Given that ... how could we argue that ..." or Curious Fact / Alternate Explanation / Plausibility.
121	- **Paradox (213):** "Apparent Paradox > Evaluate" (22%, older tests) or "Given that ... > How can it be that / Why is it that ... > Evaluate > Goal" (about 30% combined, often with Evaluate / Goal merged).
122	- **Sufficient Assumption (193):** Conclusion > Evidence > Evaluate > Goal (53%); Evidence > Conclusion > Evaluate (21%). Goal usually diagrams the missing link as the logic path.
123	- **Principle-Strengthen (181):** Conclusion > Evidence > Evaluate > Goal (48%). Goal is "If evidence, then conclusion" in general terms.
124	- **Principle-Conform (99):** mixed. Conclusion > Evidence > Evaluate > Goal (24%) when the principle is in the answers; Principle > Evaluate > Goal (9%) or Principle > Goal (7%) when it is in the stimulus.
125	- **Main Conclusion (144) / Role (126):** Conclusion > Evidence > Evaluate > Goal (42% / 53%), with an Intermediate Conclusion step in about 8%. Evaluate almost always opens with the reminder that the conclusion usually comes early (first sentence or after a pivot).
126	- **Parallel (100) / Parallel Flaw (123):** Conclusion > Evidence > Evaluate > Goal (49% / 67%), Evidence first in about 19-35%. Goal is an algebraic recipe (P1, P2, C) or a plain-language template.
127	- **Agree/Disagree (119):** speaker-named steps. "Speaker's Claims > Speaker's Responses > Goal", often a numbered claim list with "Does [speaker 2] disagree?" per claim.
128	- **Method (91):** Conclusion > Evidence > Evaluate > Goal (37%) for one speaker; "X's Conclusion > X's Evidence > Y's Response > Evaluate > Goal" for two speakers.
129	- **Evaluate (28):** Conclusion > Evidence > Evaluate > Goal (75%).

131	## 6. Recurring phrasing conventions
132	
133	- **Frames:** "Given that ... how could we (still) argue that ..." (Weaken, Strengthen, NA, Flaw); "Curious Fact ... Author's Explanation ... Alternate Explanation / Plausibility" (any causal argument); "Given that ... How can it be that ..." (Paradox); "If Evidence, then Conclusion" (Principle, SA).
134	- **Tests in choice notes:** negate it and ask if it weakens (NA); "fails to consider X" means ask whether X weakens, "presumes X" means ask whether X is necessary (Flaw); "argues X by Y: X must match the conclusion and Y the evidence" (Method, Flaw); a rule can only prove its right-hand side (Principle, SA); "can we support the Agree position and the Disagree position?" (Agree/Disagree); "Why should we believe this?" (Role, Main Conclusion).
135	- **Recurring trap names:** Too Strong (the most common NA and MBT tag), Out of Scope, No Impact, Unclear Impact, No Distinction (Paradox), Deepens Paradox, Wrong Flaw / Not an Objection (Flaw), Bad Conclusion / Evidence / Trigger Match (Principle, Parallel), Premise / Last-Claim Trap / Opposing Position (Main Conclusion), Unsupported Agree/Disagree Position (Agree/Disagree), Fake Opposite / Illegal Negation (NA), Reversal (SA). Tags come from the local markdown; the dump does not include them.
136	- **Rules stated as rules:** "Most X can only be concluded from a Most X premise"; "some / sometimes / can is almost always wrong on Strengthen, Weaken, Paradox, SA"; "the second speaker almost never denies the first speaker's evidence"; "about 85-90% of Main Conclusion questions put the conclusion in the first sentence or after a pivot"; "on EXCEPT, three of the working answers usually work one way and one works another".
137	- **Voice habits that should not be copied:** mocking the question or answer ("hot garbage", "so, so dumb", "unthinkably weird", "dumpster fire"), and the PT157/158 em-dash register.
