96	Not hardcover       →  Not one of these books

107	</details>

109	There is a small qualification. “All A are B” gives a rule about A. By itself, it does not tell us that any A exist. When we already know the group exists, all also guarantees most and some.
110	
111	### What would make “all” false?
112	
113	One exception is enough. “Not all eight books are hardcover” means **at least one of the eight is not hardcover**. The other seven could still be hardcover. This is the negation of the all claim. By contrast, “not hardcover → not one of these eight books” is its **contrapositive**: another way to state the original rule, not a way to deny it.

124	ALL  →  MOST  →  SOME       (when the group exists)
125	```

166	## Two majorities of the same group must meet
167	
168	Suppose a club has ten members. **Most members study law**, and **most members work part time**. Each “most” needs at least six of the ten members. Those two sets cannot fit inside the club without sharing at least two people.
169	
170	![Ten club members. Six study law, six work part time, and at least two do both.](./assets/club-majority-overlap.svg)
171	
172	**What is given?** More than half of the *same ten members* study law. More than half of those same ten work part time. The claims count club members, not all law students or all part-time workers everywhere.
173	
174	**Why must they meet?** Each group needs at least six members. If the groups were separate, we would need twelve different club members. There are only ten. At least two members must be in both groups.
175	
176	**What follows?** **Some law students work part time.** We can reverse that some claim: **some part-time workers study law**. Both sentences describe the same overlapping members. We know at least one exists; we do not know that most law students or most part-time workers have the other trait.

178	```text
179	Given:       Most club members study law.
180	             Most club members work part time.
181	Must follow: Some law students work part time.
182	Why:         Both majorities count the same club members.
183	```

202	
203	There is another way to guarantee an overlap. Suppose **most club members study law**, and **all club members work part time**. We already know there are law students in the club. 260	| What you have | What you may conclude |
261	|---|---|
262	| Some A are B | Some B are A |

264	| All A are B | A → B, and not B → not A |

267	| Most A are B; all A are C | Some B are C |
268	| Some A are B; all A are C | Some B are C |

274	## Nearby words need a closer look
275	
276	The words around a quantity statement can look familiar without making the same promise. Sort them by **what they guarantee**, then check the sentence in front of you:
277	
278	| Word or phrase | What it guarantees |
279	|---|---|
280	| **Some**, **at least one**, **one or more** | One or more members fit. All may fit too. |
281	| **A few**, **several**, **many** | A positive number in ordinary use, but no universal cutoff. They imply at least one. |
282	| **Most**, **more than half**, **a majority of** | Strictly more than half of the named group fit. |
283	| **All**, **every**, **each**, **without exception** | Every member of the named group fits. The group may still need a separate existence premise. |
284	| **Few**, **not many**, **not often** | A small number or low frequency in context. No exact numerical boundary follows. |
285	| **No**, **none**, **zero** | Not a single member fits. |
286	| **Not all** | At least one member does **not** fit. |
287	
288	**Few** is worth separating from **a few**. “A few books are hardcover” affirms that at least some are. “Few books are hardcover” stresses how small the number is. It often suggests some in ordinary speech, but it does not set a dependable numerical minimum. “Not many” can include zero. A hundred menu items could make five feel like few; in a menu of six, five would not feel like few. Do not quietly replace any of these phrases with a fixed number.
289	
290	**Most** is different: it crosses a precise line, **more than half**. Words such as **the majority** plainly express that idea. Words such as **usually**, **typically**, **generally**, **commonly**, **frequently**, **often**, **likely**, and **probably** can suggest prevalence in context, but pay attention to what the sentence actually counts. “Probably” about one event, for example, is a likelihood claim, not a headcount of a group.
291	
292	**Not all** *does* give us a precise minimum: at least one exception. “Not all club members are lawyers” means **at least one club member is not a lawyer**. It does not tell us whether the other members are lawyers.
293	
294	**Quick check:** A menu lists 100 beers. “Many are Belgian,” “few are Belgian,” and “not many are Belgian” are three claims. Can you assign each one an exact minimum and maximum? What does “not all are Belgian” guarantee?
295	
296	<details><summary>Show the answer</summary>
297	
298	The first three do **not** have fixed numerical boundaries. “Many” suggests more than an isolated example, while “few” and “not many” suggest a small number relative to the context. None alone licenses a specific cutoff such as five or ten. “Not all” is exact about one thing: **at least one beer is not Belgian**.

300	</details>
