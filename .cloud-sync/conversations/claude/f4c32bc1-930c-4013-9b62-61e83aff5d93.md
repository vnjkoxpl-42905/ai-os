438	
439	Writing that sounds like the source must still preserve correct logic and Joshua's chosen method.
440	
441	| Source simplification or context | Boundary for our material |
442	|---|---|
443	| “Ignore the conditional” when sufficient is absent or necessary is present. | For this one rule and fact, no new conclusion about the other condition follows. The rule remains available for later facts and chains. Never teach that it disappears. |
444	| Necessary has to happen “at some point.” | A conditional is not necessarily a time sequence or causal claim. Prefer “has to be true if the sufficient condition is true.” |
445	| The original and contrapositive are “all you know.” | The central warning is against an unsupported reverse or inverse. Equivalent formulations and other supplied rules still matter. State the immediate inference limits precisely. |
446	| Required words are described as obligations. | Preserve modality. “Interviewees must bring ID” can assert an obligation, not prove compliance. Diagram the stated requirement unless the facts establish compliance. |
447	| Indicator usually comes before target. | Read its full grammatical target. Targets can precede the indicator, and familiar words can serve other roles in context. |
448	| Shorten conditions to one or two letters, never more than four. | Joshua's clear condensed labels govern. Keep negation, scope, qualifiers, and readability. A short label that loses meaning is not an improvement. |

465	## Calibration examples for different LSAT surfaces
466	
467	All examples below are original drafts for this reference. They demonstrate writing mechanisms; they are not assigned work or approved curriculum.
468	
469	### Explain sufficient and necessary without rushing
470	
471	> Every parcel with a blue sticker has been inspected.
472	>
473	> Suppose yours has a blue sticker. That's enough to know it has been inspected. You don't need another fact about the parcel. The rule has already told you what must be true of every parcel with that sticker.
474	>
475	> We call the blue sticker the **sufficient condition**. Sufficient means enough. Here, enough to establish inspection under this rule.
476	>
477	> Inspection is the **necessary condition**. A blue-sticker parcel has to meet that requirement. Otherwise it would be an exception to a rule that allows no exceptions.
478	>
479	> Now suppose a parcel has been inspected. Does it have to have a blue sticker?
480	>
481	> We still don't know. Inspection was required for the blue-sticker parcels. The shop never said those were the only parcels it inspected.
482	
483	This returns to meaning at every term. The example does not assert why the shop uses stickers or when they are applied. Those extra facts are unnecessary.
484	
485	### Teach a necessary expression through its whole meaning
486	
487	> Editing this newsletter requires an account.
488	>
489	> What does the sentence say the editor needs? An account. Keep that complete idea on the required side of the relationship:
490	>
491	> **Edits this newsletter → Has an account**
492	>
493	> Read it back: if someone edits this newsletter, they have an account. That matches the sentence.
494	>
495	> Try the reverse. If someone has an account, do they necessarily edit the newsletter? The sentence hasn't told us that. An account could belong to someone who only reads it.
496	
497	The final possible counterexample is introduced as something consistent with the sentence, not an asserted fact about this system. Technical placement has a semantic reason.
498	
499	### Give a flashcard a reasoning task
500	
501	**Front**
502	
503	> Every package in Locker 4 has been scanned. A package has been scanned. Must it be in Locker 4? Why?
504	
505	**Back**
506	
507	> No. The rule tells us about packages already in Locker 4. It doesn't say every scanned package goes there.
508	
509	This is more useful than a card that only asks “Necessary condition = which side?” Keep simple recall cards when recall is the actual skill; do not mistake that for complete reasoning practice.
510	
511	### Make feedback identify the missed connection
512	
513	> You used the sentence in the reverse direction. “Only registered visitors enter the archive” requires registration for entry. It hasn't said every registered visitor enters. Read the diagram as **Enters → Registered** and compare it with the sentence.
514	
515	The feedback names the mistake, shows the relevant meaning, and gives a repair. It avoids generic praise, mockery, and labels such as “weak reasoning.”
516	
517	### Explain an ordinary learning objection
518	
519	> “I understood the sentence. Why write it down?”
520	>
521	> With one short rule, you may not need to. When a passage gives you several relationships, a diagram lets you compare them without rereading the wording each time. In this exercise, write the relationship so we can check that you kept its direction and complete ideas.
522	
523	The answer gives a decision criterion and distinguishes the purpose of this exercise from a blanket rule about every test question.
524	
525	### Transfer the voice to another LSAT topic
526	
527	> A conclusion is the claim the author is asking you to accept. The other statements are supposed to support it.
528	>
529	> Nora says, “The library should stay open later because every evening seat was taken this week.” What is she trying to persuade us of?
530	>
531	> That the library should stay open later. The full seats are her reason. She expects that reason to support the proposed change.
532	>
533	> You don't have to agree with her. First, separate what she wants you to accept from what she gives you in support.
534	
535	The same mechanism works outside conditionals: definition, small example, question, explanation, and a useful boundary. No conditional topic has been imported into the conclusion lesson.
