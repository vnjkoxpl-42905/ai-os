163	## Word choice and useful verbal habits
164	
165	### Ordinary language carries the formal idea
166	
167	The source retains sufficient condition, necessary condition, contrapositive, indicator, variable, and stimulus. It gives these words a familiar handle and shows what they mean. It does not hide the vocabulary the student will need.
168	
169	The conversational words are often functional: “have to,” “need,” “what happens,” “what do you know,” “the other side,” “what's left,” “this fits.” They describe a move the reader can make.
170	
171	| Word or phrase function | Source tendency | Writing instruction |
172	|---|---|---|
173	| Certainty | must, has to, necessary, no way around | Preserve the exact logical strength, then demonstrate it. |
174	| Possibility | might, could, may, we don't know | Say what remains open and why the rule leaves it open. |
175	| Drawing a consequence | so, that means, that implies, this tells me | Put the actual reason immediately before the consequence. |
176	| Giving a reason | because, since | Explain the matching relationship, not merely “because that's the rule.” |
177	| Inviting attention | let's look, notice, check, imagine | Name the thing to inspect or the fact to change. |
178	| Naming a boundary | but, that said, sometimes, unless | Keep the qualification beside the advice it limits. |
179	| Recovering context | so far, return to, now that | Remind the reader of the specific idea the new step depends on. |
180	| Testing an interpretation | this fits, does that follow, what would I need | Compare the proposed meaning with the whole original sentence. |
181	
182	Do not replace natural speech with “identify the guaranteed outcome,” “activate the sufficient trigger,” or “execute the conditional reasoning procedure” when the learner needs an explanation. Technical wording earns its place by helping them think.
183	
184	Joshua's browser corrections, 2026-10-03: define “guarantee” as the certainty supplied by the stated rule before using it to explain another idea. Explain “direction” through an actual relationship before using the word as advice. A beginner needs to see what the arrow means, which complete idea belongs on each side, and why sentence order need not match diagram order. “Read left to right” alone does not teach that. Introduce sufficient as enough and necessary as required through the example, then develop their limits.
185	
186	For rule versus fact, make the hypothetical explicit: a rule tells us what would follow **if** a condition were true. It does not report that the condition happened. Contrast “Suppose this order is marked ready” with “This order is marked ready” before drawing a conclusion about an actual case. Keep the anchor sentence idiomatic and complete. Emphasis and motion around “if” should reveal that hypothetical relationship, with equally clear static information when motion is reduced.
187	
188	### Technical names arrive at the useful moment
189	
190	The source gives a simple relationship before dissecting sufficient and necessary. It explains the contrapositive in ordinary terms before presenting the flip-and-negate shortcut. It explains why symbols help before building the first diagram. The label arrives when there is a concrete idea to attach it to.
191	
192	This does not mean withholding a definition for suspense. Start with what the topic is. Then give the learner an experience that makes its vocabulary meaningful. A name should settle a distinction the reader has seen.
193	
194	### Small verbal irregularities create a person
195	
196	The source sometimes says “little squiggle,” “little bracket cage,” “for real,” or “a bunch of different ways.” It also occasionally uses elevated words such as “ballyhoo” or “cumbersome.” This combination makes the prose human without eliminating precision.
197	
198	For our material, choose the natural word for the situation. Do not manufacture slang or rare vocabulary to imitate personality. Do not import profanity, dismissive reactions, or author-specific tics. The recognizable quality should be a teacher's thoughtful judgment, not a checklist of eccentric phrases.

200	## Questions that make the reader think
201	
202	Questions are one of the source's main ways of controlling pace. They have several distinct functions. Choose the function before writing the question.
203	
204	| Kind of question | What the source does | What our version should do |
205	|---|---|---|
206	| **Prediction before a reveal** | Coffee/walking thought experiment asks what the reader knows, gives a blank, and says STOP. | Ask a bounded question with enough information to answer. Wait for an attempt before showing the reasoning. |
207	| **One step inside a worked example** | Asks whether the sufficient condition could occur without the necessary burden. | Let the reader see the missing connection, then answer it in the walkthrough. |
208	| **Meaning check** | Personalized if/then asks what the speaker knows in a proposed situation. | Read the original sentence naturally and test whether the proposed diagram preserves it. |
209	| **Tempting reverse** | Tries the wrong sufficient condition and shows the interpretation failing. | Make the attractive wrong choice visible without ridiculing it. Explain the unsupported inference. |
210	| **Reader objection** | FAQ voices worry about time, confidence, and whether to write. | Use the learner's actual objection, then explain the decision criterion. |
211	| **Notation clarification** | Asks about parentheses and abbreviations. | Answer small questions where the notation first becomes relevant. |
212	| **Reason for practice** | Explains why the drill asks for written contrapositives. | Say what ability the task checks and why practice may be more explicit than test-day work. |
213	| **Boundary test** | Four-situation chart asks what has to happen in each case. | Compare the strongest justified answer with what could still be true. |
214	
215	An exposition question can receive an immediate answer. A student question intended to train reasoning needs an actual pause. Do not ask “What do you think?” and reveal the answer in the same breath. Do not add a rhetorical question to every paragraph merely to sound interactive.
216	
217	A useful check has three parts: a precise prompt, a learner decision, and a reasoned explanation. For example:
218	
219	> **Rule:** Every blue-sticker parcel has been inspected.
220	>
221	> **Fact:** This parcel has been inspected.
222	>
223	> Does the parcel have to have a blue sticker? Explain what the rule says about that direction.
224	
225	The feedback should identify the gap: the rule covers blue-sticker parcels; it hasn't said all inspected parcels have a blue sticker. “Incorrect. Review sufficient and necessary” is too thin.

227	## Transitions explain why the next move follows
228	
229	The source's transitions keep the learner oriented. “So far” recovers what is established. “Let's return” reuses an anchor. “Now that we have a handle on” names the prerequisite for notation. “This leads me to the next question” connects a practical worry to its answer.
230	
231	A transition should answer one of these:
232	
233	1. What have we established that the next idea uses?
234	2. What is still unclear?
235	3. What will we change while holding the rule fixed?
236	4. Why is the next representation useful now?
237	
238	Original transitions:
239	
240	> We know what the sticker tells us. Now let's see what the inspection stamp tells us.
241	>
242	> These sentences use different words, but they describe the same relationship. Let's write that relationship once.
243	>
244	> The arrow is pointing the right way. Check one more thing: did we keep the whole condition?
245	
246	Do not fill the gap with a curriculum announcement such as “Next, we will explore necessary conditions.” Say why the requirement is the next thing worth examining.

248	## Repetition changes the reader's view
249	
250	The breathing/air anchor appears in the opening, indicator families, contrapositive, fact chart, diagram, and target analysis. The reader keeps the same relationship while the representation changes. That saves the effort of understanding a new scenario at every turn.
251	
252	Within the contrapositive explanation, the source repeats the requirement in several forms: air is needed; breathing has to have air; without air the requirement is missing; the original condition cannot hold. Each repetition supplies a step or a new angle.
253	
254	For our writing, reuse an anchor while doing different work:
255	
256	1. Read the ordinary meaning.
257	2. Apply it to a case.
258	3. Identify the sufficient and necessary ideas.
259	4. Compare an unsupported reverse.
260	5. Reword the same relationship.
261	6. Show the diagram after its meaning is understood.

263	Introduce a fresh example for independent practice. The learner should not be able to answer only because the lesson already solved that sentence.

268	
269	The source uses doors, families, activation, burdens, brackets, and putting yourself in a condition's shoes. Each metaphor gives an abstract operation a physical or social handle. A small set of terms recurs, so the reader can keep using it.

445	## Accuracy and method boundaries
446	
447	Writing that sounds like the source must still preserve correct logic and Joshua's chosen method.
448	
449	| Source simplification or context | Boundary for our material |
450	|---|---|
451	| “Ignore the conditional” when sufficient is absent or necessary is present. | For this one rule and fact, no new conclusion about the other condition follows. The rule remains available for later facts and chains. Never teach that it disappears. |
452	| Necessary has to happen “at some point.” | A conditional is not necessarily a time sequence or causal claim. Prefer “has to be true if the sufficient condition is true.” |
453	| The original and contrapositive are “all you know.” | The central warning is against an unsupported reverse or inverse. Equivalent formulations and other supplied rules still matter. State the immediate inference limits precisely. |
454	| Required words are described as obligations. | Preserve modality. “Interviewees must bring ID” can assert an obligation, not prove compliance. Diagram the stated requirement unless the facts establish compliance. |
455	| Indicator usually comes before target. | Read its full grammatical target. Targets can precede the indicator, and familiar words can serve other roles in context. |

473	## Calibration examples for different LSAT surfaces
474	
475	All examples below are original drafts for this reference. They demonstrate writing mechanisms; they are not assigned work or approved curriculum.
476	
477	### Explain sufficient and necessary without rushing
478	
479	> Every parcel with a blue sticker has been inspected.
480	>
481	> Suppose yours has a blue sticker. That's enough to know it has been inspected. You don't need another fact about the parcel. The rule has already told you what must be true of every parcel with that sticker.
482	>
483	> We call the blue sticker the **sufficient condition**. Sufficient means enough. Here, enough to establish inspection under this rule.
484	>
485	> Inspection is the **necessary condition**. A blue-sticker parcel has to meet that requirement. Otherwise it would be an exception to a rule that allows no exceptions.
486	>
487	> Now suppose a parcel has been inspected. Does it have to have a blue sticker?
488	>
489	> We still don't know. Inspection was required for the blue-sticker parcels. The shop never said those were the only parcels it inspected.
490	
491	This returns to meaning at every term. The example does not assert why the shop uses stickers or when they are applied. Those extra facts are unnecessary.
492	
493	### Teach a necessary expression through its whole meaning
494	
495	> Editing this newsletter requires an account.
496	>
497	> What does the sentence say the editor needs? An account. Keep that complete idea on the required side of the relationship:
498	>
499	> **Edits this newsletter → Has an account**
500	>
501	> Read it back: if someone edits this newsletter, they have an account. That matches the sentence.
502	>
503	> Try the reverse. If someone has an account, do they necessarily edit the newsletter? The sentence hasn't told us that. An account could belong to someone who only reads it.
504	
505	The final possible counterexample is introduced as something consistent with the sentence, not an asserted fact about this system. Technical placement has a semantic reason.
506	
507	### Give a flashcard a reasoning task
508	
509	**Front**
510	
511	> Every package in Locker 4 has been scanned. A package has been scanned. Must it be in Locker 4? Why?
512	
513	**Back**
514	
515	> No. The rule tells us about packages already in Locker 4. It doesn't say every scanned package goes there.
516	
517	This is more useful than a card that only asks “Necessary condition = which side?” Keep simple recall cards when recall is the actual skill; do not mistake that for complete reasoning practice.
518	
519	### Make feedback identify the missed connection
520	
521	> You used the sentence in the reverse direction. “Only registered visitors enter the archive” requires registration for entry. It hasn't said every registered visitor enters. Read the diagram as **Enters → Registered** and compare it with the sentence.
522	
523	The feedback names the mistake, shows the relevant meaning, and gives a repair. It avoids generic praise, mockery, and labels such as “weak reasoning.”
524	
525	### Explain an ordinary learning objection
526	
527	> “I understood the sentence. Why write it down?”
528	>
529	> With one short rule, you may not need to. When a passage gives you several relationships, a diagram lets you compare them without rereading the wording each time. In this exercise, write the relationship so we can check that you kept its direction and complete ideas.
530	
531	The answer gives a decision criterion and distinguishes the purpose of this exercise from a blanket rule about every test question.
532	
533	### Transfer the voice to another LSAT topic
534	
535	> A conclusion is the claim the author is asking you to accept. The other statements are supposed to support it.
536	>
537	> Nora says, “The library should stay open later because every evening seat was taken this week.” What is she trying to persuade us of?
538	>
539	> That the library should stay open later. The full seats are her reason. She expects that reason to support the proposed change.
540	>
541	> You don't have to agree with her. First, separate what she wants you to accept from what she gives you in support.
542	
543	The same mechanism works outside conditionals: definition, small example, question, explanation, and a useful boundary. No conditional topic has been imported into the conclusion lesson.
544	
545	## Revise the draft in a useful order
546	
547	### First, check the reasoning and scope
548	
549	List what is given, what follows, and why. Try to construct a case that satisfies the given information but makes the proposed conclusion false. If that case is possible, revise the logic before polishing the voice. Check modality, negation, quantifier scope, and whether the learner has actually been taught every required operation.
550	
551	For a rewrite, preserve Joshua's established method and explicit lesson scope. The new reference governs delivery. It does not authorize removing content that students need or adding source topics the current assignment does not cover.
552	
553	### Then, check the learner's experience
554	
555	Read as someone who has never seen the notation. Can they tell what the concept is? Can they answer the first question with what the page has taught? Does each new term refer to an idea they have seen? Does the page tell them why the correct answer follows?
556	
557	Put the explanation beside the point where it is needed. Do not rely on an interaction to imply a distinction the prose never explains. A student who cannot use the animation must still be able to learn from the text.
558	
559	### Then, listen to the voice
560	
561	Read aloud at an ordinary teaching pace. Replace stiff phrasing with the words the teacher would naturally use. Keep the intermediate thoughts. Vary sentence length where the task changes. Remove repeated catchphrases, inflated enthusiasm, branded metaphors, and irrelevant asides.
562	
563	Do not repair generic prose by scattering “Cool,” “Let's,” or “That's it” through it. Repair the missing reasoning, the abrupt transition, or the abstract example.
564	
565	### Finally, verify the student surface
566	
567	Check the actual prompt, answer state, labels, buttons, diagrams, and explanations. A good note can be undermined by a shell that exposes internal planning or reveals the answer too soon. Preview desktop and mobile when the interface changes. Keep the instructor's planning map separate from what the learner sees.
568	
569	## Use this rubric before handing over LSAT writing
570	
571	Score each dimension from 0 to 2: **0** absent or misleading; **1** partly present but needing revision; **2** clear and usable. This is an editorial tool, not a validated learning measure. A logical error or scope violation requires revision regardless of the total.
