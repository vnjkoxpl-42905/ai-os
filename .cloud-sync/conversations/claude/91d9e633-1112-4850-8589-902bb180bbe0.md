## Proposed teaching copy

A necessary condition is a requirement for another condition to be true. To understand it, ask: what has to be true whenever the other condition is true?

If you are enrolled in law school, you are a student.

Being a student is necessary for being enrolled in law school. Every person enrolled in law school must be a student. Someone who is not a student cannot be enrolled in law school.

But being a student does not, by itself, establish law-school enrollment. A history student who is not enrolled in law school meets the requirement without being enrolled. Being a student is required for enrollment, but is not enough to establish it.

Connect to the earlier lesson: being enrolled is the sufficient condition, because it guarantees being a student. Being a student is the necessary condition, because enrollment requires it. They describe the same relationship from two sides.

Boundary: do not say every necessary condition is never sufficient. Some relationships work both ways. Do not teach then as a standalone necessary-condition indicator; infer roles from the whole conditional. Keep existing Group2 inventory separate.

163	## Word choice and useful verbal habits
164	
165	### Ordinary language carries the formal idea
166	
167	The source retains sufficient condition, necessary condition, contrapositive, indicator, variable, and stimulus. It gives these words a familiar handle and shows what they mean. It does not hide the vocabulary the student will need.
168	
169	The conversational words are often functional: “have to,” “need,” “what happens,” “what do you know,” “the other side,” “what's left,” “this fits.” They describe a move the reader can make.
170	
171	| Word or phrase function | Source tendency | Writing instruction |
172	|---|---|---|
173	| Certainty | must, has to, necessary, no way around | Preserve the exact logical strength, then demonstrate it. |
174	| Possibility | might, could, may, we don't know | Say what remains open and why the rule leaves it open. |
175	| Drawing a consequence | so, that means, that implies, this tells me | Put the actual reason immediately before the consequence. |
176	| Giving a reason | because, since | Explain the matching relationship, not merely “because that's the rule.” |
177	| Inviting attention | let's look, notice, check, imagine | Name the thing to inspect or the fact to change. |
178	| Naming a boundary | but, that said, sometimes, unless | Keep the qualification beside the advice it limits. |
179	| Recovering context | so far, return to, now that | Remind the reader of the specific idea the new step depends on. |
180	| Testing an interpretation | this fits, does that follow, what would I need | Compare the proposed meaning with the whole original sentence. |
181	
182	Do not replace natural speech with “identify the guaranteed outcome,” “activate the sufficient trigger,” or “execute the conditional reasoning procedure” when the learner needs an explanation. Technical wording earns its place by helping them think.

200	## Questions that make the reader think
201	
202	Questions are one of the source's main ways of controlling pace. They have several distinct functions. Choose the function before writing the question.
203	
204	| Kind of question | What the source does | What our version should do |
205	|---|---|---|
206	| **Prediction before a reveal** | Coffee/walking thought experiment asks what the reader knows, gives a blank, and says STOP. | Ask a bounded question with enough information to answer. Wait for an attempt before showing the reasoning. |
207	| **One step inside a worked example** | Asks whether the sufficient condition could occur without the necessary burden. | Let the reader see the missing connection, then answer it in the walkthrough. |
208	| **Meaning check** | Personalized if/then asks what the speaker knows in a proposed situation. | Read the original sentence naturally and test whether the proposed diagram preserves it. |
209	| **Tempting reverse** | Tries the wrong sufficient condition and shows the interpretation failing. | Make the attractive wrong choice visible without ridiculing it. Explain the unsupported inference. |
210	| **Reader objection** | FAQ voices worry about time, confidence, and whether to write. | Use the learner's actual objection, then explain the decision criterion. |
211	| **Notation clarification** | Asks about parentheses and abbreviations. | Answer small questions where the notation first becomes relevant. |
212	| **Reason for practice** | Explains why the drill asks for written contrapositives. | Say what ability the task checks and why practice may be more explicit than test-day work. |
213	| **Boundary test** | Four-situation chart asks what has to happen in each case. | Compare the strongest justified answer with what could still be true. |

473	## Calibration examples for different LSAT surfaces
474	
475	All examples below are original drafts for this reference. They demonstrate writing mechanisms; they are not assigned work or approved curriculum.
476	
477	### Explain sufficient and necessary without rushing
478	
479	> Every parcel with a blue sticker has been inspected.
480	>
481	> Suppose yours has a blue sticker. That's enough to know it has been inspected. You don't need another fact about the parcel. The rule has already told you what must be true of every parcel with that sticker.
482	>
483	> We call the blue sticker the **sufficient condition**. Sufficient means enough. Here, enough to establish inspection under this rule.
484	>
485	> Inspection is the **necessary condition**. A blue-sticker parcel has to meet that requirement. Otherwise it would be an exception to a rule that allows no exceptions.
486	>
487	> Now suppose a parcel has been inspected. Does it have to have a blue sticker?
488	>
489	> We still don't know. Inspection was required for the blue-sticker parcels. The shop never said those were the only parcels it inspected.
490	
491	This returns to meaning at every term. The example does not assert why the shop uses stickers or when they are applied. Those extra facts are unnecessary.
492	
493	### Teach a necessary expression through its whole meaning
494	
495	> Editing this newsletter requires an account.
496	>
497	> What does the sentence say the editor needs? An account. Keep that complete idea on the required side of the relationship:
498	>
499	> **Edits this newsletter → Has an account**
500	>
501	> Read it back: if someone edits this newsletter, they have an account. That matches the sentence.
502	>
503	> Try the reverse. If someone has an account, do they necessarily edit the newsletter? The sentence hasn't told us that. An account could belong to someone who only reads it.
504	
505	The final possible counterexample is introduced as something consistent with the sentence, not an asserted fact about this system. Technical placement has a semantic reason.
506	
507	### Give a flashcard a reasoning task
508	
509	**Front**
510	
511	> Every package in Locker 4 has been scanned. A package has been scanned. Must it be in Locker 4? Why?
512	
513	**Back**
514	
515	> No. The rule tells us about packages already in Locker 4. It doesn't say every scanned package goes there.
516	
517	This is more useful than a card that only asks “Necessary condition = which side?” Keep simple recall cards when recall is the actual skill; do not mistake that for complete reasoning practice.
518	
519	### Make feedback identify the missed connection
520	
521	> You used the sentence in the reverse direction. “Only registered visitors enter the archive” requires registration for entry. It hasn't said every registered visitor enters. Read the diagram as **Enters → Registered** and compare it with the sentence.
522	
523	The feedback names the mistake, shows the relevant meaning, and gives a repair. It avoids generic praise, mockery, and labels such as “weak reasoning.”
524	
525	### Explain an ordinary learning objection
526	
527	> “I understood the sentence. Why write it down?”
528	>
529	> With one short rule, you may not need to. When a passage gives you several relationships, a diagram lets you compare them without rereading the wording each time. In this exercise, write the relationship so we can check that you kept its direction and complete ideas.
530	
531	The answer gives a decision criterion and distinguishes the purpose of this exercise from a blanket rule about every test question.

533	### Transfer the voice to another LSAT topic
534	
535	> A conclusion is the claim the author is asking you to accept. The other statements are supposed to support it.
536	>
537	> Nora says, “The library should stay open later because every evening seat was taken this week.” What is she trying to persuade us of?
538	>
539	> That the library should stay open later. The full seats are her reason. She expects that reason to support the proposed change.
540	>
541	> You don't have to agree with her. First, separate what she wants you to accept from what she gives you in support.
