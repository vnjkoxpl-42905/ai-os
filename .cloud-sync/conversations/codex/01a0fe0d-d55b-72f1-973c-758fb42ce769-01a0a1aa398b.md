2	
3	Draft for Joshua's review · 2026-10-02
4	
5	These six lessons form the opening chapter. Each teaches one idea, works through an example, and gives the student a small task before the attached practice. 6	
7	## 1. Start with a guarantee
8	
9	Take this statement:
10	
11	> All dogs are mammals.
12	
13	Now suppose I tell you that Luna is a dog. You know something else about Luna: she's a mammal.
14	
15	You didn't need another fact about her. The rule already covers every dog, and Luna falls inside that group.
16	
17	We can say the same rule another way:
18	
19	> If something is a dog, then it is a mammal.
20	
21	That's a conditional. It tells you that **when one condition is met, another must be met too.**
22	
23	Notice the guarantee. The statement doesn't say dogs are usually mammals. It leaves no room for a dog that isn't a mammal.
24	
25	We can write the relationship like this:
26	
27	**Dog → Mammal**
28	
29	Read the arrow as “if this is true, then that must be true.”
30	
31	The rule doesn't tell us that a particular dog exists. It tells us what must be true of anything that is a dog. When we add the fact about Luna, we can apply the rule to her.
32	
33	### See the relationship
34	
35	Imagine a box containing every mammal. Every dog has to fit inside that box. Mammals can sit outside the dog box, but dogs can't sit outside the mammal box.
36	
37	That's why knowing “dog” gives us “mammal.”
38	
39	### Your turn
40	
41	> All volunteers wear badges. Ari is a volunteer.
42	
43	What does the rule guarantee about Ari?
44	
45	<details>
46	<summary>See the reasoning</summary>
47	
48	Ari wears a badge. The rule covers every volunteer, and Ari is a volunteer. **Volunteer → Wears a badge.**

54	## 2. Why the direction matters
55	
56	Let's stay with the same rule:
57	
58	**Dog → Mammal**
59	
60	If I tell you Luna is a dog, you know she's a mammal.
61	
62	But if I tell you Luna is a mammal, do you know she's a dog? No. She could be a cat. The rule has room for mammals that aren't dogs.
63	
64	The arrow gives us a direction. Switching that direction changes the claim.
65	
66	That's a major part of conditional reasoning on the LSAT. You need to know what a rule gives you, and where someone has gone beyond it.
67	
68	In a Must Be True question, you may apply a rule to a fact. In a Flaw question, someone may reverse the rule and claim more than the premises establish. In a Sufficient Assumption question, you may need a missing rule that connects the evidence to the conclusion.
69	
70	The question changes. The relationship still matters.
71

73	
74	| What we know | What we can conclude | Why |
75	|---|---|---|
76	| Luna is a dog. | Luna is a mammal. | The rule covers every dog. |
77	| Luna is a mammal. | We don't know whether Luna is a dog. | Other mammals are allowed. |
