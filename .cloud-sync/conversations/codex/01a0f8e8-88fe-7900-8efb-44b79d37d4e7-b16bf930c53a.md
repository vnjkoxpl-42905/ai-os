1	# Facts, Rules, and What Follows
2	
3	Every trained person has a badge. Start with that rule. Here are two different ways to use it.
4	
5	**Fact means:** Something established as true about a person, an object, or a group. “Mira is trained.”
6	
7	**Rule means:** What must be true if a condition is met. “Every trained person has a badge.”
8	
9	### 1 | Apply a rule to a fact
10	
11	**Fact:** Mira is trained.
12	
13	**Rule:** Every trained person has a badge.
14	
15	**Conclusion:** Mira has a badge.
16	
17	**Why:** Mira meets the rule's starting condition: she is trained. The rule tells us she has a badge.
18	
19	**Result:** A conclusion about Mira.
20	
21	### 2 | Link two rules
22	
23	**Rule 1:** Every trained person has a badge.
24	
25	**Rule 2:** Every badge holder has building access.
26	
27	**Conclusion:** Every trained person has building access.
28	
29	**Why:** The first rule gets us from trained to badge. The second gets us from badge to access. The shared condition, has a badge, connects them.
30	
31	**Result:** A new rule. It does not establish that anyone is trained.
32	
33	Treat the statements given in an inference question as true. Use them to prove the conclusion. Do not add an unstated fact or connection.
34	
35	## 01 | Facts state what is true
36	
37	A fact supplied by the question is your starting point. A new fact needs proof from what you were given.
38	
39	“Mira is trained” establishes training for Mira. It does not establish anyone else's training, a badge for Mira, or a relationship between training and badges.
40	
41	A fact can be negative: **“Niko does not have a badge.”** It can describe a group: **“Some staff members are trained.”** That last statement establishes at least one trained staff member, without establishing that every staff member is trained.
42	
43	<!-- FACT-SCOPE -->
44	
45	**Try it:** Some reports are missing pages. Does at least one report with missing pages exist? Must every report be missing pages?
46	
47	**Worked answer:** At least one report is missing pages. The statement establishes that such a report exists, but does not establish that every report is missing pages. Other reports could be complete.
48	
49	## 02 | Rules connect conditions
50	
51	A conditional rule covers **every person or object that meets its starting condition**. It does not establish that anyone or anything meets that condition.
52	
53	In **“Everyone who is trained has a badge,”** training is the starting condition. Having a badge is the result that must follow for that same person.
54	
55	<!-- RULE-PARTS -->
56	
57	Write the conditions as short phrases: **Is trained → Has a badge**. The arrow connects the condition we start with to the result that must follow. If a person is trained, that same person must have a badge.
58	
59	Being trained is **sufficient** to establish that someone has a badge: it is enough to trigger this rule. Having a badge is **necessary** for being trained under this rule: a trained person cannot lack a badge.
60	
61	That does not mean training is the only way to get a badge. It also does not establish that anyone is trained. The rule could hold even if no one met its starting condition.

64

1	# Some, Most, and All
2	
3	Quantity statements tell us whether a claim applies to some members of a group, most of them, or all of them. Suppose a class has been assigned a case to read. “Some students read it,” “most students read it,” and “all students read it” are three different claims about that same class. We do not need an exact headcount to understand the difference.
4	
5	For each sentence, ask: **What must be true? What could still be true?** The answer changes with the word we use.
6	
7	## Some guarantees at least one
8	
9	Imagine a shelf with eight books. I tell you that **some of the books are hardcover**. How many hardcover books must there be?
10	
11	At least **one**. There could be one, four, or all eight. “Some” rules out zero. It does not rule out “most” or “all.”
12	
13	![Eight books showing that one hardcover book and eight hardcover books both satisfy some.](./assets/some-books.svg)
14	
15	This is easy to miss because in ordinary conversation “some” often suggests “some, but not all.” On the LSAT, that extra phrase is not part of the statement. If all eight books are hardcover, it is still true that some are hardcover.
16	
17	**Try it:** “Some applicants received an interview.” Could every applicant have received one? Does the sentence tell us that anyone was denied an interview?

30

33	> **Some hardcover items are books.**
34	>
35	> **At least one hardcover item is a book.**

38

42
