General Breakdown Activity: Chaining Conditionals (Deriving New Rules). Chaining involves synthesizing multiple conditional rules to create a new inferred conditional statement. This is crucial for questions that require you to draw broad conclusions from a set of premises (often seen in Inference/Must Be True contexts). Connecting Rules: You must determine if the necessary condition (right side) of one conditional statement matches the sufficient condition (left side) of another. For instance if A rightarrow B and B rightarrow C they chain to infer a new conditional relationship: A rightarrow C. Contraposing Chains: You can contraposit an entire chain starting from the final necessary condition and negating back to the initial sufficient condition. If the chain is A rightarrow D the contrapositive is If not D then not A. The LSAT commonly rewards the ability to connect the beginning of the chain to the ending of the chain. Negating Complex Ideas: If the idea being contraposed (the trigger or the outcome) is a complex idea connected by and or or (e.g. A and B) you must remember to flip the truth values when negating. And turns into or and or turns into and. This ensures you correctly flip the truth value of everything in the complex idea. II. General Breakdown Activity: Triggering Conditionals (Deriving New Facts). Triggering is the process of using a known fact to activate a conditional rule and derive a certain factual inference. This is the process utilized in many Must Be True and Inference questions. A conditional rule (Sufficient rightarrow Necessary) can only be used in two valid scenarios: 1. Affirming the Sufficient Condition: If a fact confirms that the sufficient condition (the left side the trigger) is true then the necessary condition (the right side the outcome) must necessarily follow. 2. Denying the Necessary Condition (Contrapositive Logic): If a fact confirms that the necessary condition (the right side the outcome) is not true then the sufficient condition (the left side the trigger) cannot have been true. If the fact relates to the conditional rule in any other way (e.g. affirming the necessary or denying the sufficient) the rule just disappears and has no power over us. III.
