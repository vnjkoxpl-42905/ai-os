## Section 2: The Common DNA (these become MUST RULES)

Across every sample, regardless of topic or register, the following patterns hold. These are non-negotiable for the skill.

**1. The reader is in the room.**
Every sample uses "you," "we," "us," "let's." No third-person distancing. The writer is sitting next to the reader, walking them through it. Even Sagan, operating at a cosmic register, addresses "us" and "we." Feynman opens by literally calling out "you, the reader."

**2. Abstraction is always cashed out in the concrete.**
No sample lets an abstract idea sit unanchored. Every concept is grounded in a specific scenario, sensory image, or character. Conditional logic gets breathing/air. Loopholes get koalas. Mass-in-physics gets a spinning top vs. a still one. Cosmic scale gets a mote of dust. Information overload gets a 1750 guy seeing a smartphone. The pattern: **abstract claim → concrete instance → walk it.**

**3. Anticipate the reader's pushback or confusion.**
The writer is in the reader's head. Feynman: "You might ask why we cannot teach physics by just giving the basic laws on page one..." Tim Urban: "But here's the interesting thing-" The Loophole sample: "you'd be tempted to think...WRONG." Every sample interrupts itself to address an objection or wonder the reader is about to have.

**4. Long-then-short rhythm for landing points.**
Sentences breathe in long, breathe out short. A flowing exploratory sentence sets up an idea, then a punchy fragment lands it. Examples of the landing move: "That's it." / "WRONG." / "Now the argument is golden." / "He might actually die." / "Cool." / "No, it is not possible to do it that way." This rhythm is the single most consistent device across all 19 samples.

**5. Confidence without hedging.**
No "perhaps," "it could be argued," or "one might say" softening. When the writer makes a claim, they make it. Disagreement and limitation are stated plainly: Feynman says we are "completely wrong" with the approximate law and doesn't soften it. The Loophole says "WRONG." Sagan says "there is no hint that help will come." The voice trusts itself.

**6. Pedagogical signposting in plain English.**
Transitions are conversational, not academic. "Let's start," "Let's look at," "Now, let's," "Here's the thing," "First...Second...," "OK so." Never "Furthermore," "Moreover," or "It is therefore the case that." The signposts feel like a teacher talking, not a textbook.

**7. Metaphor and analogy as primary explanatory tools.**
Every sample uses metaphor to do real explanatory work, not decoration. Conditional logic = a door opener. Comma = a breath in a story. The Loophole = an evil twin / Omar burning Marlo's money. Designer's exploration = walking a city + reading a map. Watches = an opening tour. The metaphor isn't a flourish, it carries the load.

**8. Earned authority via clarity, not jargon.**
None of these writers prove their expertise through vocabulary. They prove it by making hard things feel obvious. Feynman explains relativity's implications without showing off. Bret Victor introduces "ladder of abstraction" and immediately grounds it in walking-the-city. Even the LSAT reference material uses "blowhard" and "crappy argument." The writer is smart enough to be casual.

**9. Paragraphs are short and breathing.**
Across all samples, paragraphs run roughly 3–6 sentences. Even when the topic is dense, the visual cadence stays generous. No walls of text.

**10. The voice has personality.**
Every writer here has tics that make the prose feel human. Tim Urban's "magical wizard rectangle." Feynman's "(So, not satisfied to learn physics in four years, you want to learn it in four minutes?)". The Loophole's "my personal favorite." Sagan's repetition: "every hunter and forager, every hero and coward, every creator and destroyer..." Personality is not optional, flat prose is failure.

## Section 3: Style Dials (modes the skill can switch between)

The samples span a real range. These are the dials Joshua can pick from per assignment.

**Tone dial: whimsical ←→ sober**
- Whimsical end: koalas/banana bread/Camille (A3, A4, A7), Tim Urban time-traveler (C5)
- Mid: cluster sentence walkthrough (A1), Mechanical Watch (C4), 7Sage Synopsis (B2)
- Sober end: Feynman opening (C1), Sagan (C2)

Whimsical works for hooks, intros, and sticky example-creation. Sober works for ground-truth definitions and high-stakes framing.

**Density dial: narrative-explanatory ←→ procedural-reference**
- Narrative end: Block A samples, Feynman, Sagan, Tim Urban, flowing prose, walks the reader through reasoning
- Procedural end: B1 (Skills to Build), B6 (Stimulus Tendencies bullets), modular, scannable, list-heavy

The same skill should know: chapter intros and conceptual explanations are narrative. Reference cards, drill instructions, and quick-reference sheets are procedural. Don't mix.

**Casualness dial: relaxed ←→ formal**
- Relaxed: Tim Urban ("magical wizard rectangle," "die level of progress," all-caps for emphasis), The Loophole sample ("Cool.", "evil twin"), Flaw Synopsis ("confused blowhard's crappy argument")
- Mid: Block A LSAT pedagogy, Bret Victor
- Formal-but-warm: Feynman, Sagan

LSAT U content lives mostly in the mid-to-relaxed band. Joshua's existing voice (per memory: terse, opinionated) leans relaxed. Sober/formal is reserved for stakes, opening framings about what the LSAT is and what's at risk for the student.

**Pacing dial: lyrical ←→ punchy**
- Lyrical: Sagan (long anaphoric sentences, "every X, every Y, every Z"), some Feynman
- Mid: most of Block A
- Punchy: Tim Urban, The Loophole, Block B references

Default to punchy. Reach for lyrical only at peak emotional/motivational moments (e.g., why this work matters, why the LSAT is winnable).

## Section 4: Style Rules: Voice & Tone

### Do
- Address the reader as "you." Use "we" and "let's" to invite them into the work.
- State claims plainly. If a thing is wrong, say it's wrong.
- Have opinions. Show preferences ("my personal favorite," "the most versatile asset").
- Let personality leak, odd analogies, asides, casual reactions.
- Trust the reader to follow you. Don't over-explain.

### Don't
- Use third-person academic voice ("the student," "one") to avoid intimacy.
- Use hedging fillers (perhaps, arguably, it could be the case that, in some sense).
- Use stiff academic transitions (furthermore, moreover, in addition, thus it follows).
- Use generic SaaS / corporate tone.
- Use em dashes, ever. Replace with commas, parentheses, periods, or restructured clauses. (Joshua's standing rule.)

## Section 5: Style Rules: Sentence & Paragraph Mechanics

### Sentence rhythm
- Vary sentence length deliberately. The single most recognizable move is **long exploratory sentence → short landing sentence/fragment.**
- One- to four-word sentences are encouraged for emphasis ("That's it." / "Boring." / "Cool." / "WRONG.").
- Don't chain three long sentences in a row without a short one to land.
- Rhetorical questions are allowed and effective, but only when followed by an immediate answer.

### Paragraph shape
- Default 3–5 sentences per paragraph. Hard cap around 6.
- Open the paragraph with the move it's making (a claim, a question, an example, a transition).
- End the paragraph with the takeaway, often as a short sentence.
- Use a one-sentence paragraph occasionally for impact.

### Punctuation
- Commas for breath and pacing, generous use.
- Parentheses for asides and self-aware moments.
- Colons for setup → payoff structures.
- Periods to land. Use them aggressively.
- No em dashes. (See above.)
- Italics for emphasis on a single key word, and for example sentences quoted within prose.
- ALL CAPS sparingly for shock-value emphasis ("WRONG.", "NO."). Once per page max.

## Section 6: Style Rules: Pedagogical Structure

The default "explain a concept" sequence across samples:

1. **State the concept simply.** One sentence. Often paired with "That's it." or similar landing.
2. **Concrete example, called out visually.** Italicized, indented, or set apart. The example should be memorable and small enough to hold in working memory.
3. **Walk the example.** Plain-prose tour through what the example shows, often using "Let's..." or "So..."
4. **Anticipate the reader's confusion or objection.** Address it directly.
5. **Restate the takeaway as a bold sentence or callout.** This is the line the student should remember.
6. **Optional: chart, diagram, or table.** Used in samples A6 (contrapositive) and B6 (stimulus tendencies). Only when the structural relationships are worth visualizing.
7. **REMEMBER!-style callout** for the single non-negotiable takeaway, when one exists.

For multi-concept chapters, this 7-beat structure is repeated per concept, with brief connective transitions between.

### Procedural/reference content (Block B mode)
- Lead with a one-line definition.
- Use numbered steps when sequence matters.
- Use bullet lists when items are parallel.
- Use bold for the term being defined, not the whole sentence.
- Keep bullets to 1–3 sentences. Longer goes to prose.

## Section 7: Style Rules: Typography & Emphasis

Patterns I'm seeing visually:
- **Bold** lands the takeaway sentence inside a paragraph. Not whole paragraphs, just the line worth remembering.
- *Italics* mark example sentences (e.g., conditional statements, sample arguments) and key vocabulary terms when first introduced.
- ALL CAPS for section headers and rare shock emphasis.
- Indented italic blocks for example sentences set apart from prose.
- "REMEMBER!" black-tag callouts for the single most important takeaway of a section.
- Q. / A. format for FAQ insertions.
- Short bolded mini-headers (sentence-case) inside long sections to anchor the reader.

## Section 8: Signature Devices

Specific moves to deploy:

**The "Let's X" opener**
"Let's start," "Let's look at," "Let's talk through," "Let's bridge those gaps." Invites the reader. Use at the open of any walkthrough.

**The single-fragment landing**
After explaining a thing, land it in two to four words. "That's it." / "Cool." / "Boring." / "WRONG." / "Now the argument is golden." Use sparingly, once or twice per section. Loses force if overused.

**The anticipated-question pivot**
"You might ask why...", "But here's the thing...", "Wait, what does that mean?" Use to break up exposition and re-engage the reader at points where confusion is likely.

**The recurring memorable example**
Pick a concrete cast (a Camille, a Senator Henley, a koala lobby) and reuse them across the chapter. The reader should feel like they're watching characters, not abstract variables.

**The unexpected-register pop**
A casual phrase ("crappy argument," "magical wizard rectangle," "evil twin," "blips on the radar") inside otherwise serious explanation. Reminds the reader the writer is human. Use once or twice per section.

**The paired contrast**
"X is awesome at Y. However, X is rarely arranged well enough to Z." / "Necessary assumptions are about safety. The Loophole is about danger." Make a strong claim, then immediately establish its limit or counterpart.

**The numbered or labeled inventory inside narrative prose**
"First...Second...," or "(SA), (NA), (the Loophole)", used to handle multiple cases inside flowing prose without breaking out into a full list.

**The pop-culture or everyday-life anchor**
The Wire (A8), time machines (C5), spinning tops (C1), maps and walking (C3), watches (C4). When introducing a hard idea, reach for something the reader already knows from outside school.

**The motivational-stakes paragraph**
At the chapter open or close, a paragraph that names why this skill or idea matters for the reader's actual life. Sagan does this whole-cloth (responsibility to the pale blue dot). Feynman does it ("If you are going to be a physicist, you will have a lot to study"). For LSAT U, this is the "why this question type is the linchpin" move at chapter open and the "now you have this in your toolkit" move at chapter close.

## Section 9: Anti-Patterns (never do these)

- ❌ Em dashes anywhere
- ❌ Generic SaaS or marketing voice
- ❌ Passive-voice hedging ("It can be argued that...")
- ❌ Listing without grounding (a bulleted list of abstract terms without concrete examples)
- ❌ Three+ long sentences in a row without a short landing
- ❌ Filler transitions (Furthermore, Moreover, In addition)
- ❌ Throat-clearing openers ("In this section, we will discuss...")
- ❌ Apologizing for difficulty ("This is a hard concept, but...")
- ❌ Over-explaining after a punchy line lands. Once you say "That's it.", stop. Don't add a clarification.
- ❌ Writing about the LSAT as if it's neutral subject matter. The voice has stakes, the test is something to beat.

## Section 10: Quick reference: what makes this voice unmistakable

If a piece of LSAT U content is truly in this voice, all of the following should be true:

- A reader can hear a person, not a textbook.
- Abstract ideas have concrete bodies attached to them within two sentences of being introduced.
- The reader's questions are anticipated and addressed in line.
- At least one short fragment-sentence lands a takeaway in every section.
- No em dashes. No academic hedging. No filler.
- The takeaway sentence of every section is bolded and could be read in isolation.
- A memorable example or character anchors each major concept.
- The voice has opinions and shows them.
