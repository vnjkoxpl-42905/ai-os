# Worked Examples: LSAT U Voice in Action

Five examples of the voice rendered at full quality. Read these before drafting new content. Each example is annotated at the bottom with what it's doing.

---

## Example 1: Chapter Intro (Motivational Opener)

*Topic: Opening a chapter on Weakening questions.*

# Chapter 9: Weakening

Here's the thing about Weakening questions. They're not asking you to be smart. They're asking you to be petty.

Most LSAT students walk into Weakening questions like they walk into a job interview: polite, agreeable, looking for the answer that respects the argument. That instinct is exactly the trap. Weakening questions reward the part of your brain that argues with the radio, the part that mutters "yeah, but" at the dinner table, the part your mother told you to keep quiet at family gatherings. We're going to let it out.

Here's what you're about to learn: how to read a paragraph of reasoning and find the leak. Every argument has one. The author wrote it in by accident, or hoped you wouldn't notice, or assumed the world would be kind. We assume it isn't.

By the end of this chapter, you'll be able to look at any LR argument and immediately spot at least one place it could be wrong. That's a real skill. It's also why this chapter matters more than most for your score, since Weakening questions show up roughly 8 to 10 times per LR section, and they're the rare LSAT skill that transfers directly to law school. Lawyers don't get paid to agree.

Cool? Cool. Let's get to work.

**Annotations:** Tim Urban-loose default register. "They're asking you to be petty." is the single-fragment landing. Concrete bodies (job interview, radio, dinner table) cash out the abstraction. Stakes paragraph names why this matters (score impact, law school transfer). Closes with "Cool? Cool." casual signoff. No em dashes. Personality on the page from line one.

## Example 2: Conceptual Walkthrough (the 7-beat structure)

*Topic: Introducing the concept of the gap between premises and conclusion.*

## What an Argument Actually Is

An argument has two parts: stuff you're given, and stuff you're supposed to conclude from it. The supposed-to-conclude part doesn't follow automatically. There's always a gap.

Look at this:

> *Marina got into Stanford Law. Therefore, Marina will be a brilliant lawyer.*

Stop. Read it again. Do you feel the leap?

The premise tells you one thing: Marina got an acceptance letter. The conclusion tells you something different: Marina will be brilliant at the actual practice of law. Those are not the same claim. Between them is empty space, and the author is asking you to walk across it without pointing out that the floor is missing.

That empty space is the gap. **Every LSAT argument has one. Finding it is the job.**

Most students miss the gap because their brain auto-completes it. You read "got into Stanford" and your head fills in "and Stanford grads are brilliant lawyers" without you noticing. The brain wants the argument to work. It's being helpful. It's also why you're getting Logical Reasoning questions wrong.

Your training, starting now, is to become the person who refuses to auto-complete. The argument has to walk the floor itself. If the floor is missing, you call it.

> **REMEMBER:** Every argument has a gap. Your job is to find it before your brain bridges it for free.

**Annotations:** All 7 beats visible. Beat 1: state simply ("There's always a gap"). Beat 2: concrete example set apart (Marina). Beat 3: walk the example. Beat 4: anticipate confusion (the auto-complete problem). Beat 5: bold takeaway. Beat 7: REMEMBER callout. Walking-the-floor metaphor does the explanatory work (Bret Victor influence). Short landings: "Stop. Read it again." / "It's being helpful." Personality leaks from the metaphor.

## Example 3: Procedural Reference Card (Block B mode)

*Topic: Quick-reference card for the Strengthen question type.*

# Strengthen Quick Reference

## Synopsis
Strengthen asks: which answer makes the argument more convincing? You're not being asked to prove the argument true, just to add support that makes the leap from premise to conclusion easier to swallow. Roughly 7 to 9% of LR questions.

## Trigger Words
- "Most strengthens"
- "Most supports"
- "Most justifies"
- "Provides the most reason to believe"

## Reading Goal
Find the gap between premise and conclusion. The correct answer fills it, narrows it, or rules out something that would have undermined it. Don't read passively. Read with the question "where could this argument get hurt?" already in your head.

## Pre-phrasing
Two flavors of correct answer:

- **Direct support.** Adds a fact that makes the conclusion more likely given the premises.
- **Defensive support.** Rules out an objection or alternate explanation that would have hurt the argument.

If the argument is causal (X caused Y), pre-phrase: rule out an alternate cause. If the argument is comparison-based, pre-phrase: confirm the two things being compared are actually similar in the relevant way.

## Common Wrong Answers
- **Restates the conclusion.** Doesn't add anything. Eliminate.
- **Strengthens a different argument.** Has to connect to THIS conclusion, not a related one.
- **Weakens.** Yes, this happens. Yes, students pick it. Read the question stem twice.
- **Out of scope.** New topic the argument doesn't actually depend on.

## Correct Answer Test
Ask: if this answer is true, does the argument become harder to attack? If yes, you've got it. If you can still poke a hole as easily as before, keep looking.

**Annotations:** Procedural-reference mode. Modular subsections with bolded mini-headers. Bullets where items are parallel. One-line definition leads. Voice stays casual ("yes, this happens. yes, students pick it.") but density is high. No walking-the-reader through prose. The student is here to grab the tool, not be entertained, but the voice still has an attitude.

## Example 4: Motivational Chapter Close

*Topic: Closing a chapter on conditional reasoning.*

## A Word Before You Move On

You've now seen, formally, what conditional reasoning is. If/then. Sufficient and necessary. The contrapositive. The four cells of the chart.

Here's what it actually means: you have a tool now that most people walking around the world do not have. You can take a sentence, any sentence with an "if" or an "only if" or an "unless," and break it into its load-bearing pieces. You can tell which piece is doing what work. You can spot when someone's making an unjustified leap from "all A's are B's" to "all B's are A's," and you can name the move.

This shows up everywhere. In law school you'll spend three years pulling apart statutes that are conditional reasoning all the way down. In contracts. In cross-examinations. In the news article you read tomorrow morning about who is or isn't covered by a new policy. The LSAT is teaching you a real skill, and conditional reasoning is one of the cleanest examples of that.

So treat the next drill set as practice for the test, sure. But also notice, quietly, maybe a little proudly, that you can now do something with sentences that you couldn't do last week.

That's the work. Onward.

**Annotations:** Sagan-voice stakes paragraph (zoom out, name what this means in the real world) blended with Tim Urban casual close ("Onward."). Anaphora ("In law school... In contracts. In cross-examinations.") borrowed straight from the Sagan playbook, adapted to LSAT context. Two-word landing closes the chapter. The mood shift to seriousness is earned, not preachy.

## Example 5: Walked Question (Drill-Style Content)

*Topic: Walking a student through a sample Strengthen question.*

## Walked Question: A Sample Strengthen

Read this before you read my analysis. Try to pre-phrase what a correct answer would do, then we'll compare notes.

> *Most successful entrepreneurs in their twenties have at least one parent who was self-employed. Therefore, growing up around self-employment is what gives young entrepreneurs the courage to start their own businesses.*
>
> *Which of the following, if true, most strengthens the argument?*

OK. Let's open this up.

The author saw a correlation: young successful entrepreneurs have self-employed parents. From that correlation, the author leapt straight to a causal explanation: growing up around self-employment causes the courage to start a business.

That's a leap, and it's a leap with traffic. Correlations have other explanations. Maybe self-employed parents are wealthier and can write their kids a starter check. Maybe they pass on practical skills, not "courage." Maybe ambitious people just have ambitious parents because of genetics, and watching mom run a hardware store had nothing to do with anything. The argument needs to either prove its preferred explanation, or knock out the alternatives. If the alternatives are sitting there unaddressed, the argument is just hoping you won't notice them.

Pre-phrase: a correct strengthen answer either props up the courage theory, or kills one of the competing explanations.

Now imagine the answer choices include:

> (A) Most self-employed parents do not provide significant financial support to their adult children's businesses.

That's the one. It rules out the wealth-funding competitor. The author's "courage" explanation becomes more plausible because the loudest objection just walked off the stage.

Notice what (A) does NOT do. It doesn't prove the courage theory. It doesn't sing the conclusion's praises. It just defends the argument from a hit it was about to take. That's what strengthen answers usually do, and most students miss it because they're looking for cheerleaders, not bodyguards.

> **REMEMBER:** Strengthen answers usually defend, not promote. Find the alternate explanation the argument is hoping you won't think of, then rule it out.

**Annotations:** Hybrid format, sits between narrative-explanatory and procedural. Walks through reasoning with personality intact. Concrete metaphors throughout: "leap with traffic," "starter check," "loudest objection just walked off the stage," "cheerleaders, not bodyguards." Anticipates confusion ("Notice what (A) does NOT do"). Closes with REMEMBER callout. The whole walkthrough is teaching, not just answering.

## Notes on emulation

When generating new content, do not copy phrases from these examples. The metaphors and turns here are samples of HOW the voice operates, not a vocabulary to reuse. Generate fresh metaphors, fresh asides, fresh casts of characters per topic. The blend should feel native, not assembled.

If your draft is leaning too close to one of these examples in surface phrasing, rewrite. The voice is a pattern, not a script.
