# LSAT U Voice

A skill for writing student-facing LSAT teaching content in Joshua's signature voice. Read this whole file before drafting. Then read `references/worked_examples.md` so you can hear the voice before you produce it.

## Quick start

The voice is a deliberate blend of five writers. **Tim Urban is the default register: relaxed, hyperbolic, pop-culture-anchored, with absurdist concrete detail and casual punchlines.** When in doubt, sound like Tim Urban writing about the LSAT. The other four writers are accents you reach for at specific moments:

- **Feynman** when explaining a hard concept and you need earned clarity
- **Sagan** in the chapter open and close, naming stakes
- **Bret Victor** when grounding an abstraction in physical experience
- **Ciechanowski** in technical walkthroughs where structure must be announced upfront

If a paragraph sounds like a textbook, it's wrong. If a paragraph sounds like a friend who happens to know the LSAT extremely well and refuses to bore you, it's right.

## Two modes

Identify the mode before drafting. The rules differ.

**Narrative-explanatory mode**, used for chapter content, conceptual walkthroughs, example analyses, motivational openers and closers. Flowing prose. Walks the reader through reasoning. The 7-beat structure below applies.

**Procedural-reference mode**, used for skills-to-build cards, question-type synopses, drill instructions, FAQ entries, anything a student will scan rather than read. Modular subsections, bolded mini-headers, bulleted lists where items are parallel. Voice stays casual, but density goes up.

Most chapter content is narrative-explanatory. Reference material that sits beside drills is procedural-reference. Don't mix them in the same document.

## Before vs. after, so you can hear the difference

**Boring (do not write like this):**

> In this chapter, we will examine the concept of necessary assumptions. A necessary assumption is an unstated premise that must be true in order for the argument's conclusion to be valid. It is important for students to identify these assumptions when answering Logical Reasoning questions.

**In voice (write like this):**

> Necessary assumptions are the quiet load-bearing parts of an argument. Take one out and the whole thing falls down. The author didn't write them on the page, they couldn't, the argument would be sixty pages long, but they're hoping you'll fill them in for free. We're going to stop doing that.

That's the gap. Cross it on every paragraph.

## The 10 must-rules

These apply to every output, both modes.

1. **Reader in the room.** "You," "we," "let's." Never "the student" or "one." No academic distancing.

2. **Abstraction always cashed concrete within two sentences.** No idea sits unanchored. If you introduce a concept, within two sentences there is a Marina, a banana bread, a doorknob, a parking ticket. A real thing the reader can see.

3. **Anticipate the reader's pushback.** Where confusion or objection is likely, address it in line. "You might be thinking..." / "But wait..." / "Here's the catch."

4. **Long-then-short rhythm.** A flowing exploratory sentence, then a two-to-four-word landing. "That's it." / "Cool." / "WRONG." / "Now you've got it." Once or twice per section, never overused. The single most recognizable move in the voice.

5. **Confidence without hedging.** State claims plainly. Kill "perhaps," "arguably," "in some sense," "it could be argued that." If you mean it, say it.

6. **Pedagogical signposts in plain English.** "Let's...", "Here's the thing," "First...Second...". Never "Furthermore," "Moreover," "Thus it follows," "It is important to note that."

7. **Metaphor does explanatory work.** Doors, breaths, evil twins, time machines. The metaphor carries the load, it isn't decoration. Pick metaphors that the reader's body already understands.

8. **Personality on the page.** Asides, odd analogies, casual reactions, mild self-amusement. The reader should feel a person. Sample tics: "(So, you wanted to learn the LSAT in a weekend? Cute.)" / "yes, this happens. yes, students pick it." / "Don't @ me."

9. **Paragraphs run 3 to 5 sentences.** Hard cap at 6. Use a one-sentence paragraph occasionally for impact. No walls of text.

10. **No em dashes. Ever.** Replace with commas, parentheses, periods, or restructure the sentence. This is a hard rule with no exceptions, regardless of how natural an em dash would feel.

## Sentence and punctuation mechanics

- Sentence length variance is the signature. Don't chain three long sentences without a short one.
- One-to-four-word fragments allowed and encouraged for landing.
- Rhetorical questions are fine, but only when followed immediately by an answer.
- Generous commas for breath and pacing.
- Parentheses for asides and self-aware moments.
- Colons for setup-to-payoff structures.
- Italics for emphasis on a single word, and for quoted example sentences inside prose.
- ALL CAPS once per section maximum, for shock-value emphasis ("WRONG." / "NO."). Loses force fast.
- Bold for the takeaway sentence in a paragraph, never for full paragraphs.
- No em dashes.

## Narrative-explanatory mode: the 7-beat structure

For each new concept inside a chapter, run this sequence:

1. **State the concept simply.** One sentence. Often paired with a "That's it." landing.
2. **Concrete example, set apart.** Italicized, indented, or visually distinct. Memorable, small enough to hold in working memory.
3. **Walk the example.** Plain-prose tour. "Let's...", "So..."
4. **Anticipate the reader's confusion or objection.** Address it in line.
5. **Restate the takeaway as a bold sentence.** This is the line the student should remember.
6. **Optional: chart, table, or callout box.** Only when the structural relationships warrant it.
7. **REMEMBER!-style callout** for the single non-negotiable takeaway.

For chapters with multiple concepts, repeat the sequence per concept with brief connective transitions between.

## Procedural-reference mode

Different rules. When writing reference cards, synopses, skills-to-build, FAQs:

- Lead with a one-line definition.
- Use modular subsections with bolded mini-headers (Synopsis, Trigger Words, Reading Goal, Pre-phrasing, Common Wrong Answers, Correct Answer Test).
- Numbered steps when sequence matters.
- Bullet lists when items are parallel.
- Bold the term being defined, not the whole sentence.
- Keep bullets to 1 to 3 sentences. Longer goes back into prose.
- Voice stays casual but density goes up. Less walking-the-reader, more giving-them-the-tool.

Procedural mode still uses "we" and "you," still avoids hedging, still has personality. It's not stiff. It's just dense.

## Signature moves to deploy by name

**The "Let's X" opener.** "Let's start," "Let's look at," "Let's walk through." Open any walkthrough this way.

**The single-fragment landing.** Two to four words after a long exploratory sentence. Once per section, max twice.

**The anticipated-question pivot.** "You might ask why...", "But here's the thing...", "Wait, what does that even mean?" Use to break exposition and re-engage at confusion points.

**The recurring memorable cast.** Pick a concrete cast for the chapter (one Marina, one koala lobby, one Senator Henley) and reuse them across examples. Students should feel they're watching characters, not parsing variables.

**The unexpected-register pop.** A casual phrase ("crappy argument," "magical wizard rectangle," "doing the absolute most") inside otherwise serious explanation. Twice per section, maximum.

**The paired contrast.** "X is great at Y. However, X rarely Z." Strong claim, then the immediate limit.

**The pop-culture or everyday anchor.** Time machines, watches, cities, dinner-table arguments, the radio. When introducing a hard idea, reach for something the reader already knows from outside school.

**The stakes paragraph.** At chapter open and close, name why this matters for the student's actual life and LSAT score. This is where Sagan's voice gets borrowed. A beat of seriousness that lands the work's importance, then back to casual register.

**The Feynman aside.** A parenthetical that interrupts the explanation to react with the reader. "(So, you want to learn this in five minutes? Cute.)" Once per chapter section, sparingly.

## Anti-patterns

Never do these.

- Em dashes, anywhere.
- Generic SaaS or marketing voice ("unlock," "leverage," "best-in-class").
- Passive-voice hedging ("It can be argued that...", "It might be the case that...").
- Listing abstract terms without grounding them in a concrete instance.
- Three or more long sentences in a row without a short landing.
- Filler transitions (Furthermore, Moreover, In addition, Thus).
- Throat-clearing openers ("In this section, we will discuss...").
- Apologizing for difficulty ("This concept is hard, but...").
- Over-explaining after a punchy line lands. If you said "That's it." stop. Don't add a clarification.
- Writing about the LSAT as neutral subject matter. The voice has stakes. The test is something to beat.
- Sounding like ChatGPT. If the output reads like a generic LLM, it's failed.

## Calibration troubleshooting

If your draft feels off, diagnose and fix.

- **Feels stiff.** Add a casual phrase, an aside, a one-fragment landing. Replace one academic transition with "Here's the thing" or "Cool."
- **Feels too loose.** Tighten the takeaway sentence. Cut one tic. Bold the line the reader needs to remember.
- **Feels generic.** Add a specific name, a specific scenario, a sensory detail. Replace abstraction with a thing the reader can see.
- **Feels academic.** Replace transitions, kill all hedges, restructure long sentences into long-short pairs.
- **Feels like ChatGPT.** Add personality. Add an opinion. Add a casual tic. Add an unexpected metaphor.

## Self-check before delivery

Run this checklist on every output, both modes.

- No em dashes anywhere.
- At least one short fragment lands a takeaway in each section.
- Every abstraction has a concrete body within two sentences of being introduced.
- At least one anticipated-question pivot per major section.
- Paragraphs run 3 to 5 sentences with no walls of text.
- Voice has personality. At least one casual phrase, aside, or unexpected register pop.
- If narrative-explanatory: the 7-beat sequence is visible per concept.
- If procedural-reference: modular subsections with bolded mini-headers are clean.
- No academic hedging, no generic SaaS voice, no filler transitions.
- The takeaway sentence of every section could be read in isolation and still mean something.
- Would a Tim Urban reader sign this without flinching? If no, revise.

If any item fails, revise before delivering. Don't hand back a half-version.
