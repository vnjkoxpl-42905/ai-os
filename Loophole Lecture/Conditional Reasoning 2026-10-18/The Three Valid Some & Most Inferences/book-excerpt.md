<div><div><div><h4>Book Excerpt</h4><span>Page 139</span></div><div><div><div><p>Some of the hardest Logical Reasoning stimuli will provide you with premises containing <strong>SOME</strong> and <strong>MOST</strong> and ask you to combine them. You can chain <strong>SOME/MOST</strong> with normal Must Arrows, but you have to handle those chains very carefully. You cannot simply read <strong>SOME</strong> and <strong>MOST</strong> chains straight through like with normal conditionals. But don’t fret! There are only three <strong>SOME</strong> and <strong>MOST</strong> chains that lead to any real Inferences.</p>
<p><strong>Premise</strong>: <strong>A ←ˢ→ B → C</strong></p>
<p><strong>Valid Inference</strong>:<strong>A ←ˢ→ C</strong></p>
<p><strong>Pattern</strong>: You can extend the Some Arrow by pointing away from it in a chain.</p>
<p><strong>Premise:A —ͫ→ B → C</strong></p>
<p><strong>Valid Inference</strong>: <strong>A —ͫ→ C</strong></p>
<p><strong>Pattern</strong>: You can extend the Most Arrow by pointing away from it in a chain.</p>
<p><strong>Premise</strong>: <strong>A —ͫ→ B</strong>, <strong>A —ͫ→ C</strong></p>
<p><strong>Valid Inference</strong>:  <strong>B ←ˢ→ C</strong></p>
<p><strong>Pattern</strong>: Two Most Arrows stacked on the same sufficient condition (A) means there must be a Some Inference overlap between the necessary groups (B &amp; C).</p></div></div></div></div></div>
