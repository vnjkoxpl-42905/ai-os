<div><div><div><h4>Book Excerpt</h4><span>Page 136</span></div><div><div><div><p><strong>SOME</strong> and <strong>MOST</strong> are rogue members of the sufficient family. They’re less strictly inclusive than their brethren, so they were kicked out of the sufficient household and sent to the end of the chapter. But that doesn’t mean you can ignore them, especially if you want a near-perfect LR section. Some of the hardest questions in LR will ask you to diagram <strong>SOME</strong> and <strong>MOST</strong> statements, and then test you on your ability to combine those statements into valid Inferences.</p>
<p><strong>SOME</strong> means any quantity of one or more. If I have a total of five cookies, and I tell you that some of my cookies are salted shortbread, that could mean one, two, three, four, or all five of my cookies are salted shortbread. With SOME, anywhere from 1% to 100% of my cookies could be salted shortbread.</p>
<p><strong>MOST</strong> means any quantity in the majority. If I still have five cookies, but now I say most of my cookies contain dark chocolate, that could mean three, four, or all five of my cookies contain dark chocolate. With MOST, anywhere from 51% to 100% of my cookies could contain dark chocolate.</p>
<p>Even though <strong>SOME</strong> and <strong>MOST</strong> refer to quantity, we can still diagram them like we do with regular conditionals. <strong>SOME</strong> and <strong>MOST</strong> are super simple to diagram! They always go right before their target, and their target goes straight in the sufficient condition.</p>
<p><strong>The Some Family</strong></p>
<p><em>few, many, at least one, several</em></p>
<p><strong>The Most Family</strong></p>
<p><em>usually, probably, mostly, more often than not</em></p></div></div><div></div></div></div></div>
