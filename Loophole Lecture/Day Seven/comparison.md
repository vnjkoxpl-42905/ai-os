## Three lessons specify the comparative process

[Why Comparatives Aren't Harder](<Why Comparatives Aren't Harder/source.json>), 0:55–1:46, warns against presuming disagreement or a fixed set of relationships. Two excerpts might instead be a theory and application, or similar ideas in different settings. Claims that students perform equally or better on comparatives, or that anxiety causes the apparent difficulty, are instructor observations rather than independently supplied performance evidence.

[How to Translate Comparatives](<How to Translate Comparative Passages/source.json>) and its [notes](<How to Translate Comparative Passages/ellens-notes.txt>) give three concrete steps:

1. Finish A's complete passage process, including its main point, before beginning B (0:13–0:47). Keep the views separate rather than blending them.
2. While reading B, highlight topics already mentioned in A conceptually rather than by literal word matching (1:13–2:19).
3. Include at least one quip per B paragraph connecting it to A (2:30–2:58).

This extends Day Three's separate-main-point instruction and Day Six's A/B confirmation demonstration. Cross-passage reactions expose relationships; they do not replace translating the detailed content of either passage. Highlight absence is not proof a concept was absent: the method depends on the accuracy and completeness of the reader's own highlighting.

[Why Not Answer A's Questions Immediately](<Why You Shouldn't Do Passage A's Questions Immediately/source.json>), 1:02–2:23, argues that an intermediate question pass adds decisions, rushed reads and repeat work; 2:23–3:10 argues that it interrupts comparative connections. The stated 90-plus-percent result and one exceptional student are anecdotes. The lesson itself permits using a different sequence when actual personal evidence supports it, rather than establishing a universal prohibition.
