Sufficient and necessary logical fallacies
* Sufficient conditions guarantee an outcome, whereas necessary conditions represent strict requirements for occurrences.
* Joshua reviewed an LSAT flaw question where an author misidentified a sufficient condition as necessary.
* Flaw questions frequently test conditional logic by introducing restrictive assumptions unsupported by premises.


Correlation and causation fallacies
* Correlations indicate that two variables occur together without proving a direct causal relationship.
* Authors commit causal fallacies by establishing a correlation in premises and claiming causation in conclusions.
* Evaluations of causal claims must account for potential third factors and competing alternative explanations.


Part-to-whole and whole-to-part fallacies
* Traits true of a collective group do not automatically apply to every individual component.
* Characteristics of individual parts do not necessarily reflect the properties of the entire collective whole.


Unproven-versus-proven-false fallacies
* A lack of supporting evidence for a claim does not constitute definitive proof of falsity.
* Treating an unproven proposition as false incorrectly equates a failure of proof with guilt.


LSAT flaw question strategies
* Flaw questions comprise over 50% of LSAT logic sections and require recognizing abstract answer patterns.
* Translating abstract answer choices into plain language improves accuracy when resolving difficult questions.
