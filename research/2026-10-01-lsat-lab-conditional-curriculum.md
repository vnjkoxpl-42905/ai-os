### 1. Rebuttal Rephrase

**Week:** Week 1  
**Page activity:** 13  
**Type:** AI Skills Training  
**Estimated time:** 10 mins  
**Coverage:** Related activity with conditional material

[Start](https://app.lsatlab.com/skills-training/8?scheduleactivityid=13250137&studyactivityid=911)

#### Description

Articulate the Rebuttal Conclusion



This is one of our nerdiest phrases. It sounds complicated, but it's a very easy idea. We're essentially just flipping the logical light switch on an idea.



EXAMPLE:

Some people say that Lebron is the best basketball player ever. But they're wrong. After all, [evidence].



The conclusion is the sentence that says "They're wrong". But by itself it has no meaning. So we need to better articulate what the author means by rebutting "they're wrong".



BAD: They're wrong.

BETTER: People are wrong to say that Lebron is the best basketball player ever.

BEST: Lebron isn't the best basketball player ever.



We took the original attributed idea, "Lebron is the best", and just flipped its logical light switch to get "Lebron isn't the best".



This drill is about practicing that skill.


#### Instructions

Practice This Skill for 7 Minutes



You'll be given an argument that contains a rebuttal conclusion. Your job is to express what that conclusion means in specific wording. If the conclusion is using any sort of pronoun / referential wording (e.g. "**This **reasoning is misguided / **Such **actions will not produce **the intended effect**"), you'll need to import the specific wording into those referential words/phrases. And of course you'll need to flip the logical light switch on the idea.



Flipping the switch on the idea is essentially the same idea as negating an answer choice (as we do in Necessary Assumption), or negating conclusions when we're trying to argue the Anti-Conclusion perspective (as we do on Flaw, Weaken, and other question types).



Negating a claim means the same as "the *minimum *way to contradict this claim". (it has nothing to do with **illegal negations** in conditional logic)



- When the truth value of a claim hinges on its *Quantifier*, you negate that quantifier.


- When the truth value hinges on the Telling Part **not **being true, you negate the "not" by removing it.


- If it's a conditional claim, the truth value hinges on its certainty. It's saying something is Always true or true in All cases. So you negate the certainty / the universal / the guarantee. By claiming "it's not certain", we are claiming that "there is at least one counterexample" or "it's possible for the trigger to be true but the outcome to be false".


- For all other sentences, the truth value hinges on the verb, because the verb is what starts the Telling Part of the sentence.

### 4. Famous Flaws Level 1

**Week:** Week 2  
**Page activity:** 40  
**Type:** Level Drill  
**Estimated time:** 27 mins  
**Coverage:** Related activity with conditional material

[Start](https://app.lsatlab.com/practice?type=LR&questionsubtypes=Famous%20Flaws&difficulty=1&tests=A%2CF1997%2C1%2C2%2C3%2C4%2C5%2C6%2C7%2C8%2C9%2C10%2C11%2C12%2C13%2C14%2C15%2C16%2C17%2C18%2C21%2C23%2C101%2C102%2C103%2C104%2C105%2C106%2C107%2C108%2C109%2C110%2C111%2C112%2C113%2C114%2C115%2C116%2C117%2C118%2C119%2C120%2C121%2C122%2C123%2C124%2C125%2C126%2C127%2C128%2C129%2C130%2C131%2C132%2C133%2C134%2C135%2C136%2C137%2C138%2C139%2C140%2C141%2C142%2C143%2C144%2C145%2C146%2C147%2C148%2C149%2C150%2C151%2C152%2C153%2C154%2C155%2C156%2C157%2C158%2C159&showall=true&studyactivityid=838&scheduleactivityid=13250164&milestoneid=52&returnurl=https%3A%2F%2Fapp.lsatlab.com%2Fstudy-plan)

#### Description


#### **The Ten Famous Flaws**



Here is a description and an example of the top 10 **Famous Flaws** that show up routinely as abstract answer choices, followed by a few more that almost made the cut.



**Necessary vs. Sufficient **- Presenting a conditional rule and then using it in an illegal backwards or opposite fashion to get to the conclusion (i.e. an illegal reversal or illegal nation). This is **frequently** the actual flaw on Flaw and Parallel Flaw questions.

- *People who have seen "The 13th" have a great understanding of the US prison system. Since Megan still hasn't seen that documentary, she must have a poor understanding of the US prison system. *
- *People who have seen "The 13th" have a great understanding of the US prison system. Jane understands the US prison system really well, so clearly she has seen "The 13th".*


**Causal** - Overconfidently concluding one possible causal interpretation of a curious fact, usually a Correlation, when other possible interpretations exist.

- *People who are enlisted in the military are more likely than those who haven't been to have excellent physiques. Evidently, military recruitment officers select their future soldiers in large part based on the applicant's fitness and strength.*


**Part vs. Whole** - Assuming that a trait that is true of each Part of a collective is therefore true of the Whole, or vice versa.

- *The military is part of the government, and the military is conducted with lots of honor and discipline. Thus, the government overall is conducted with lots of honor and discipline. *
- *Since rat poison is very harmful if ingested, each ingredient in rat poison would be very harmful if ingested.*


**Unproven vs. Proven False** - Acting like X must be false, either because there's no evidence that X is true, or because someone made a faulty argument for X being true.

- *Billy says that his uncle was related to Elvis, but he hasn't been able to connect them on Ancestry.com. Thus, Billy's uncle was ****not ****related to Elvis. *
- *Some claim that the Atlanta Braves should change their name because they are the only major sports team that still has a Native American mascot. However, the Chicago Blackhawks still have a Native American mascot. Thus, the Braves should not change their name.*


**Ad Hominem** - Dismissing a point of view because the source has a biased interest or has conflicting past behavior.

- *My friend Sameer says that social workers would be better at responding to domestic abuse calls than would armed police officers. But we can ignore this notion, since Sameer is a social worker and so his industry would benefit were cities to make this switch. *
- *My governor is telling me that we should all wear masks now, but we should not heed her words. After all; she was vocally against wearing masks a month ago.*


**Sampling** - Extrapolating/generalizing too readily from a small sample, while failing to consider that the sample might be biased, too small, or unrepresentative.

- *Charlie, a staunch Republican, is from San Francisco and works for a theater company. Since Shelia is also from San Francisco and also works for a theater company, she is probably a staunch Republican too. *
- *Today's youth prefer comic books to literature. After all, at a survey conducted at last year's Comic-Con, over 85% of the young people surveyed reported liking comic books much more than novels.*


**Inappropriate Appeal** - Your evidence is a plea for sympathy, you cite an "authority" without establishing their credibility, or you cite popular Opinion in order to judge a Factual matter.

- *I know I was speeding, Officer, but you shouldn't give me a ticket because it would ruin my Anniversary. *
- *Dr. Fauci, the government's leading expert in infectious diseases, says that coronavirus can't be transmitted by sharing the same hat. But he's wrong, for my friend Brandy says that you can transmit coronavirus by sharing hats.*


**Circular Reasoning** - The premise of an argument restates or assumes the conclusion. (these answers are almost always wrong)

- *The LSAT is clearly the hardest grad school test, because all the other tests people take to go to grad school pale in comparison to the difficulty of the LSAT.*


**Internal Contradiction** - Something the author says early on undermines or contradicts something else they say. (these answers are almost always wrong)

- *LSAT is an incredibly challenging test for anyone who takes it. However, if you commit to Eddie Spaghetti's Steady 'N Ready LSAT system, you will soon be feeling like LSAT is no harder than a kindergarten spelling quiz.*


**Equivocation** - Using the same "term" or concept to mean two very different things. (these answers are almost always wrong)

- *Some question whether Egypt condones Romania's recent military buildup on their eastern border. But clearly Egypt sanctions this activity, since in the weeks following the Romanian military's actions, Egypt issued sanctions against Romania.*







#### **A Few More Kinda-Famous Flaws**



**Intent vs. Result** - Assuming that if an action resulted in some consequence, then that consequence was intended by whoever performed the action.

- *My neighbor filled up a kiddie pool for his daughter. When he emptied it, the water spilled on to our driveway. Thus, the neighbor apparently wanted to get our driveway wet.*


**False Choice** - Acting as though you have to choose between two options, as though they're a binary / exhaustive set, even though you never established those are the only options.

- *We could use Grandma's birthday money to buy the new Mario Kart. But we could also use it to buy a rope swing for the yard. Since we can't find Mario Kart available anywhere online, we'll have to buy the rope swing.*


**Relative vs. Absolute** - Getting mixed up with Percents vs. Amounts, or confusing more/less language with yes/no language.

- *Some say that older people are never great at video games, but they're wrong. After all, Grandma is much better than Uncle Toby is at video games. *
- *This year, the work force at Foot Locker is 60% female, up from 50% female a year ago. Clearly, Foot Locker has hired more females. *

#### Instructions

**Get 4 out of 5 correct**

to pass this level

### 6. Conditional Logic

**Week:** Week 2  
**Page activity:** 43  
**Type:** Video  
**Estimated time:** 30 mins  
**Coverage:** Dedicated conditional activity



#### Description

This lesson introduces conditional logic and how it plays a role in the Logical Reasoning section.



**Conditional logic** is one of the least sexy, least intuitive, but **most important** areas of improvement, when it comes to Logical Reasoning and Games.



To get meaningfully better with conditional logic, there are about **15 - 20** conditional logic **indicator words** we want to get used to, so that when we see them, it automatically tells us whether to put that idea on the Left of the arrow, put the negated version on the Left of the arrow (if-not), or put the idea on the Right of the arrow.


#### Instructions

**Have a sheet or two of paper** handy.



Just like with Logic Games, learning Conditional Logic is partly about building up muscle memory. We diagram conditional logic on paper until we're eventually so automatic with it that we don't need to write it down any more.



But we're simply not a fully qualified LSAT test-taker if we haven't drilled ourselves into being adept at recognizing and interpreting conditional statements.



So as you watch the video, take the opportunity to **write your own version** of the conditionals being shown on screen. Your brain learns both through the intellectual content of the lesson as well as through the physical performance of you drawing crap on your paper.



And when you hit that **cheat sheet **at the very end,** copy it over** **in your handwriting**. That 3-4 min exercise will probably be more important for your brain's sensitivity to these 15-20 money words than watching the video will.

How does Conditional Logic usually show up, by type?



- **Principle-Conform w/ Principle in Stimulus  **(makes a lot of sense to diagram these conditionals, because all five answers are testing us on whether we have an argument that establishes the left side of a rule and concludes the right side of a rule)


- **Parallel** (makes a lot of sense to diagram these arguments in the abstract, using generic variables like A, B, C. The more specific we are with seeing the abstract recipe, the more picky and decisive we can be with answers.)


- **Sufficient Assumption** (the conclusion gives us an A --> Z logic path we want to prove. the evidence, combined with the correct answer choice, assemble together to create that A --> Z path)


- **Must Be True / Most Supported** (if there are multiple conditionals, they will often chain together. otherwise, a fact provided will trigger the contrapositive of a conditional)


- **Flaw / Parallel Flaw** (if we're doing Flaw and see conditional logic, it usually means that the author is about to use that conditional rule in an illegal Backwards or Opposite fashion, which is the #1 Famous Flaw, known as **Necessary vs. Sufficient**)

### 9. The Core Connection

**Week:** Week 2  
**Page activity:** 46  
**Type:** Patterns  
**Estimated time:** 37 mins  
**Coverage:** Related activity with conditional rule or indicator material

[Continue](https://app.lsatlab.com/practice?type=LR&questionsetid=54&questionsetattemptid=441749&scheduleactivityid=13250174)

#### Description

Most LR questions come from the **Assumption & Objection Family.**

*Flaw, Weaken, Necessary Assumption, Strengthen, *

*Sufficient Assumption, Principle-Strengthen, Evaluate*



These questions measure our ability to analyze the case the Prosecution is making for possible holes, and our ability to think of possible objections that the Defense could make. So, respectively, we end up looking for missing ideas or connections in the body of evidence presented, or we try to think about what types of issues haven't even been considered so far.



Most answers can be lumped into one of these two buckets.

- those testing the **Internal Logic** of the argument ("in the box" ideas)
- those testing **New Considerations** ("outside the box" ideas)


This set of problems is all about the Internal Logic, the missing facts or missing links in the argument. Usually the argument missing a connection between certain language in the evidence and certain language in the conclusion, but sometimes it's missing a connection between the language in two premises.


#### Instructions

Try these six questions, **untimed**.



- Find the Conclusion.
- Find the main Supporting Idea (the Premise).


- On **Assumption** questions, try to find an answer reinforcing
*If Premise, then Conclusion*



- If you're doing **Strengthen**, look for answers saying
*The Premise is heavily connected to the Conclusion*



- If it's **Flaw** or **Weaken**, look for answers saying
*The Premise can be true, while the Conclusion is false*

### 10. Conditional Rephrase

**Week:** Week 2  
**Page activity:** 47  
**Type:** AI Skills Training  
**Estimated time:** 10 mins  
**Coverage:** Dedicated conditional activity

[Start](https://app.lsatlab.com/skills-training/2?scheduleactivityid=13250170&studyactivityid=908)

#### Description

Conditional Indicator Words



While we can understand conditional relationships conversationally, the test loves to entice us into accidentally treating them as binaries (which leads us to fall for bad answers that are actually illegal reversals or negations of what we want).



So it greatly improves our accuracy and efficiency to memorize the common conditional indicator words. That way, in 90% of cases, we know exactly how to diagram the relationship.



There are four types of keywords, each of which instructs us to do a different thing.



(*put it on the LEFT*)

if, when, all, each, any, every, the only, *universal nouns*



(*put it on the RIGHT*)

only, only if, must, requires, will, always, ensures, implies, guarantees



(*negate the LEFT*)

unless, without



(*negate the RIGHT*)

no, none, never


#### Instructions

Practice Spotting and Categorizing Indicator Words

for 7 minutes

***Telling Part Not Mentioned***

The human ego is certainly frail and volatile, but when we see monkeys that can dance ballet, we know that we are not categorically different from other higher primates. Thus, monkeys that can dance ballet make humans feel inferior.



*missing link:  *When we don't feel categorically different from other higher primates, we feel inferior.



***Naming and Telling Parts Are Both Mentioned***

The human ego is certainly frail and volatile, but when we see monkeys that can dance ballet, we know that we are not categorically different from other higher primates. And since we feel inferior when we don't feel special, monkeys that can dance ballet make humans feel inferior.



*missing link: *When we don't feel categorically different from other higher primates, we don't feel special.



***Naming and Telling Parts Are Both Unmentioned***

Watching higher primates do a sophisticated task makes us feel not-special. Thus, monkeys that can dance ballet make humans feel inferior.



*missing links*: Monkeys are higher primates / doing ballet is a sophisticated task / feeling not-special makes us feel inferior

#### Description

**Principle** isn't one question type. It's really a stylistic twist that gets used with a variety of familiar tasks. We cluster some of these together for simplicity and treat them like question types, just so it's easier to search for them and see analytics on them.



**Principle-Strengthen** (w/ Principles in Answers)

This is by far the most common type of Principle question. We're given an argument in the stimulus and we're asked to pick the principle that most justifies the conclusion. Because these are conditional logic heavy, they feel more like **Sufficient Assumption **than Strengthen, but the question stem says "which principle *most *justifies", not "fully justifies". Our default expectation is that we will be pre-phrasing an "IF premise, THEN conclusion" conditional and then reading five answers that are full of conditional logic trigger words. Conditional logic skills allow you to make really quick eliminations on some answers and to avoid falling for the "right ideas, wrong order" trap answers. A small share of these is more conversational like regular Strengthen; if we see the argument seems to be *Weighing Tradeoffs *(considering upsides and downsides), then the correct answer is more likely to be squishy and/or comparative.

- Which of the following principles, if valid, most justifies the argument?


**Principle-Strengthen** (w/ Principles in Stimulus)

These don't appear on older tests but have become more popular over the past twenty tests (though still pretty rare). They're awesome! We're given an explicit Principle, and we're given an Application of that principle (an argument that attempts to apply that principle. It will be concluding the right side of the principle, but it will always fail to completely establish the left side of the principle. So all we need to do is pick an answer that finally makes clear that the left side of the principle has been triggered.

- Which of the following, if true, most justifies the application of the principle?


**Principle-Conform** (w/ Principles in Answers)

Not that common, but recent tests have had a few. It's essentially the same as the Strengthen version. We are still pre-phrasing "IF premise, THEN conclusion" and then expecting answers that *Link *language from the Evidence to the Conclusion's language. What's different is that because the question stem says *conform *to the author's reasoning, we can't pick an answer that's overly strong.

- Which of the following principles most conforms to the reasoning in the argument?
- Which of the following most accurately expresses the principle underlying the reasoning above?


**Principle-Conform** (w/ Principle in Stimulus)

These are fun, since the stimulus is short; it just spells out a principle, a rule or two that we need to follow. We then go check out the five answers and try to apply that rule (i.e. we make sure that the Evidence in an answer choice triggers the left side of the principle and we make sure the Conclusion is the right side of the principle). The principles/rules we're given are almost always in conditional logic, and frequently test some of the more complicated moves in conditional logic: dealing with "unless", bi-conditionals, ands/ors. If we understand that the correct answer's conclusion has to match a *Right Side *of the principle, we'll sometimes be able to instantly eliminate some answers.

- Which of the following situations conforms best to the principle cited above?
- The principle above most helps to justify which one of the following?


**Principle-Conform** (w/ Implicit Principles)

These are rare. Normally, Parallel questions feature arguments that have conditional logic terms or quantified (some/most/all) terms, and we typically diagram those. We jot down a recipe for how many premises there are and what each premise is doing in relation to the other premises and the conclusion. But other arguments on Parallel questions don't have those features, so we don't diagram, and instead we just pre-phrase a gist-y slogan like "If it was true before, it'll be true again" or "If you did if for them, you should do it for me". - Which of the following is best illustrated by the example described above?

#### Description

There are three varieties of **Principle-Conform**

- The stimulus is a principle (or pair of principles)
- The answers are principles
- There are no explicit principles, just implied/illustrated ones


**Principles in Stimulus**

When the stimulus is providing an explicit principle (or two), we're usually being tested on our ability to apply a conditional rule.



To find an answer that Conforms to this principle, we want an answer where the situation described matches the Trigger of the principle, and the judgment concluded matches the Outcome of the principle. (Even when the principles aren't expressed as conditionals, there will still usually be two things we need to make sure match up).



**Principles in Answers**

It's essentially the same mindset as a Principle-Strengthen question. We read the argument and then anticipate the principle underlying the argument by pre-phrasing "IF evidence, THEN conclusion". We expect the correct answer to *Link *language from the Evidence to the Conclusion's language. All that's different is that because the question stem says *conform *to the author's reasoning, we can't pick an answer that's overly strong. (That usually doesn't matter, but occasionally it does)



**Implicit Principles**

These feel similar to Parallel reasoning questions, because we're given an argument in the stimulus, and then each answer choice is its own argument on its own unique topic. We're looking for an underlying similarity to connect the correct answer to the original argument.



On Parallel questions, those similarities come more in the form of technical / structural similarities (i.e. "Since the original argument had a *Most *premise and a *Some *premise, the correct answer's argument will also have those features").



But with Principle-Conform it's more about the underlying gist or rule-of-thumb. We don't try to diagram the arguments. We just want to articulate a general moral to the story or operating principle that's guiding the author's thinking, and then we try to find an answer that matches that.

### 29. Necessary Assumption - First Taste

**Week:** Week 3

**Type:** Process  
**Estimated time:** 45 mins  
**Coverage:** Related activity with conditional material

[Continue](https://app.lsatlab.com/practice?type=LR&questionsetid=57&questionsetattemptid=159042&scheduleactivityid=13250219)

#### Description

**Necessary Assumption** is the second most common question type in Logical Reasoning, and it's pretty difficult for a number of reasons. Assumptions perform one of two functions:



- they supply a missing idea the author was clearly thinking
- they rule out an objection that would hurt the author's argument


When they are supplying a **missing idea**, Necessary Assumption questions feel a lot like Sufficient Assumption or Principle-Strengthen. Sometimes they even feel like Must Be True, which makes sense because we're being asked what Must Be Assumed. These examples are heavy on conditional logic, and the correct answer seems to be regurgitating concepts that were mentioned in the stimulus.  ("in-the-box" answers)



But when they are **ruling out an objection**, Necessary Assumption questions feel more like Weaken or Flaw. These examples are more about causation and comparisons. The correct answer will often feel like it's bringing in new, outside considerations that haven't been talked about yet. ("out-of-the-box" answers).



How do we know which sort of Necessary Assumption question we're doing?



We don't, but generally speaking if we see conditional logic or see certain buzzwords / phrases being repeated twice within the paragraph, then we're being tested on a missing idea.



If we otherwise think the author is making a recommendation, making a prediction, or offering a hypothesis to explain some curious background data, then we're probably being tested on potential objections.


#### Instructions

Try these 10 questions, **untimed**



- Ask yourself if there's a New Concept in the conclusion that was never defined in the evidence. If so, then what idea from the evidence is the author connecting with this New Concept in the conclusion? (that's the most common way we spot missing links)


- Ask yourself if the author is assuming a Similarity between two things, or if the author is assuming a Difference between two things. (don't be afraid to spell out the 'obvious' -- in many cases that's all correct answers are doing)


- If neither of those methods worked, then you should try to argue the Anti-Conclusion. If the author is concluding "Thus, the sky is blue", then you're arguing "the sky is NOT blue". How can we accept the author's evidence but still argue our anti-conclusion. Or, alternatively, given the anti-conclusion we're arguing for, how can we respond to the author's evidence?


Can you think of an alternate explanation for it?

Can you argue it's not truly relevant to what's being debated in the conclusion?

Can you outweigh its effect by introducing new considerations?



If you're able to articulate an Objection, then the author is assuming that your objection is **not** the case.



**ANSWERS**



be wary of stuff that's **Too Strong**

be wary of **Comparisons** never made or implied

be wary of **Unmentioned** concepts



if an answer is ruling out an idea with the word "**not**", check that one out! negate it by removing the "not" and see if it turns into an Objection. if so, pick it.



if an answer is **conditional**, diagram it. see whether that conditional (or more likely its contrapositive) matches a reasoning move the author made. be wary of illegal reversals or negations of the author's thinking.

### 31. Negations Practice

**Week:** Week 3  
**Page activity:** 69  
**Type:** Flashcards  
**Estimated time:** 7 mins  
**Coverage:** Related activity with conditional material

[Start](https://app.lsatlab.com/flashcards/?sectiontype=&type=28&difficulty=&scheduleactivityid=13250221)

#### Description

**Necessary Assumption** is the 2nd most common question type in LR (behind Flaw).



One way to think about our task on Necessary Assumption is:

*which answer, if negated, most weakens*



Whichever answer's negation most weakens will always be the correct answer. So why is that most students don't use that approach to Necessary Assumption?



- It's not always the most efficient. If we hear a term shift from X to Y, we can spot a good answer saying that the author assumes "if X, then Y" without needing to negate the answer to verify that the negation would weaken.
- Negating claims can be tricky and involve such heavy thinking that it slows us down.


The most important time to use the Negation Test is when answer choices are ruling out an idea with the word "not". These answers are the simplest to negate (just get rid of the "not"), and they are usually Defender answers that don't make a ton of sense as written, but if we ask ourselves whether the negation would weaken we get a much better sense of whether the answer has potential.


#### Instructions

Set a timer for at least **6 minutes**



If the truth value of the claim hinges on a quantifier,

then **negate the quantifier**.

(Some vs. None / Most vs. 49% / All vs. Not-All )



If the answer is ruling out an idea with the word "not", then **remove the not**.

(note: never remove the "not" from the *Naming Part *of a sentence. If the sentence says "People who have not gone to college are unable to explain Critical Race Theory", the sentence is about people who haven't gone to college. Our negation should also be about people who haven't gone to college. The answer said one thing about those people, "unable to explain CRT", and we're contradicting what was said about those people:  "People who have not gone to college are **able **to explain CRT".)



Otherwise, **negate the main verb** of the sentence.



If it's a conditional idea, negating isn't the best idea.

Instead, just consider it as a conditional statement, and ask  yourself whether the author's reasoning moved from establishing the trigger idea (somewhere in the evidence) and then assuming or concluding the outcome idea.  Beware illegal reversals and negations, which are super common with conditional answer choices. Negating "if X, then Y" means you're saying, "It's possible that X is true but Y is false". The negation will seem like a very weak idea, since we're just saying "there's at least one case in which  X is true but Y is false". But since that counterexample disrupts the author's certainty in moving from X to Y, it still weakens the argument.

### 33. Negation Station

**Week:** Week 3  
**Page activity:** 71  
**Type:** AI Skills Training  
**Estimated time:** 10 mins  
**Coverage:** Related activity with conditional material

[Start](https://app.lsatlab.com/skills-training/6?scheduleactivityid=13250226&studyactivityid=910)

#### Description

When do we **negate **claims?



**Necessary Assumption **answers

One way to answer any NA question is to think of the task as "Which answer, if negated, most weakens the argument?" Since they're asking us about *necessity*, we can assess whether an argument needs a claim to be true by asking ourselves whether it would matter to the argument if the claim were false.



Slightly more than half of the correct answers on NA are **ruling-out **an objection. They usually use the word "not". We can easily negate these by removing the word "not" and seeing if they suddenly turn into an objection.



**Anti-Conclusions**

When we're doing Flaw, Weaken, Necessary Assumption, and Strengthen, we are usually trying to debate the author / trying to figure out what gaps the argument has or what objections we could make. Two useful framings we can use for this thought process are:

- *If I accept the Evidence, how can I still argue the Anti-Conclusion?*
- *If I'm arguing the Anti-Conclusion, how can I respond to the Evidence?*


We're trying to think like the Defense attorney.

If they're concluding "Eddie is guilty", we're arguing "Eddie is **not **guilty".

If they're arguing that "some chefs are artists", we'd argue "**No **chefs are artists".

If they're arguing that "all politicians are liars", we'd argue "**at least one** **isn't **a liar".



The conclusion vs. anti-conclusion clarifies the debate we're having in this 'courtroom'. We're trying to determine whether it's true to say "Miranda is a gossip" or true to say "Miranda isn't a gossip".



**Agree / Disagree **answers

To select an answer on Disagree questions, we need to feel like we can derive it as a stated or likely opinion from one of the two people. And then we need to feel like we can derive *the negation *of that answer from the other person.


#### Instructions

Practice Your Negations



Negating a claim means the same as "the *minimum *way to contradict this claim". (it has nothing to do with **illegal negations** in conditional logic)



When the truth value of a claim hinges on its *Quantifier*, you negate that quantifier.

All vs. Not all (at least one isn't)

Some (at least one is) vs None

Most (at least 51%) vs. Not-Most (at most 49%)

Many (at least a handful) vs. Not-Many (none, or almost none)



When the truth value hinges on the Telling Part **not **being true, you negate the "not" by removing it.

Sally will **not** cry vs. Sally will cry

The photo does **not** mislead vs. The photo does mislead

This plan will **not** result in a water crisis vs. it will result in a water crisis



If it's a conditional claim, the truth value hinges on its certainty. It's saying something is Always true or true in All cases. So you negate the certainty / the universal / the guarantee.

a 180 **ensures** admission vs. might have 180 but not be admitted

NBA players **must** be tall vs. might be an NBA player but not tall

If different tone, then different authors vs. might be diff tone but same author



For all other sentences, the truth value hinges on the verb, because the verb is what starts the Telling Part of the sentence.

Exercising increases focus vs. exercising doesn't increase focus

Quotas are insulting vs. quotas are not insulting

#### Description

There are about a baker's dozen **Famous Flaws** that show up a lot on these problems, especially within the answer choices:



- **Necessary vs. Sufficient **- taking a conditional relationship and but then illegally thinking through it in backwards or opposite fashion.


- **Causal** - overconfidently concluding one possible causal interpretation of a curious fact, usually a Correlation, when other possible interpretations exist


- **Part vs. Whole **- Assuming that a trait that is true of each Part of a collective is therefore true of the Whole, or vice versa.


- **Unproven vs. Proven False **- Pointing out that someone failed to prove X is true, and then concluding that this means that X is false.


- **Ad Hominem** - Dismissing a point of view because the source has a biased interest or has conflicting past behavior.


- **Sampling** - Extrapolating/generalizing too readily from a small sample, while failing to consider that the sample might be biased, too small, or unrepresentative.


- **Irrelevant Rebuttal **- Rebutting someone's claim or argument by mischaracterizing it in some Straw Man way or by bringing up something wholly irrelevant to the original claim.


- **False Choice** - Acting as though you have to choose between two options, as though they're a binary / exhaustive set, even though you never established those are the only options.


- **Relative vs. Absolute** - getting mixed up with Percents vs..Amounts, or confusing more/less language with yes/no language.


Almost always wrong answers:

- **Circular Reasoning **- The premise of an argument restates or assumes the conclusion.


- **Internal Contradiction** - Something the author says undermines or contradicts something else they say


- **Equivocation** - Using the same "term" or concept to mean two very different things.


- **Inappropriate Appeal** - your evidence is a plea for sympathy, you cite an "authority" without establishing credibility, or you favor one source's opinion over another source's, without justifying your pick.

#### Description

When we see conditional logic show up in a stimulus, that doesn’t mean we automatically start diagramming it on our paper. But we start to consider that move! Typically, students need to physically diagram conditionals earlier in their prep, so that we can ingrain the right conditional thinking. But as you get more automatic about dealing with conditional logic, you’ll probably save diagramming for when the stimulus feels particularly tricky or cumbersome to keep in your head.



There are a few common reasons we might diagram.



**CONDITIONAL CHAINS**

Many *Must Be True, Parallel* and *Sufficient Assumption *questions will involve multiple conditionals that chain together. Writing out those chains can be useful, especially since trap answers will often provide a tempting but illegal Reversal or Negation of the chain. Looking at our diagram can usually be a quick way to remind ourselves that this answer is NOT what we want.



**APPLYING A CONDITIONAL TO A FACT**

Many *Must Be True, Parallel, *and *Assumption *questions involve applying a rule (or chain of rules) to a fact that matches the Trigger, or a fact that triggers the contrapositive (it’s the opposite of the Outcome).



Often, people won’t realize that a fact triggers the contrapositive unless they actually write out the contrapositive.



**PRINCIPLES IN STIMULUS**

When the stimulus provides a principle, it’s almost always conditional logic (and often they provide two at a time). We often want to contrapose the principle they’ve provided us with so that the normative/judgy language is on the right side of the arrow.



If we had a principle like:

“A painting is beautiful only if it makes people cry”



Then we could write the rule either of these ways:

*Beautiful → Make ppl cry*

*Not make ppl cry → Not beautiful*



A correct answer *could *use either formulation. It could say, “Bob’s painting was beautiful. Thus, it must have made ppl cry.” or “Bob’s painting did not make ppl cry. Thus it is not beautiful”.



But correct answers have always (so far) stuck to that second form. The normative/judgy language is whether or not to deem a painting “beautiful”, so you want that concept on the right of the arrow, because when they apply the principle they always end up concluding the judgy language.



An important time saver here is that this principle only has the power to prove “NOT beautiful”. If there are any answers concluding something “IS beautiful”, they’re automatically wrong.

#### Description


#### **The Ten Famous Flaws**



Here is a description and an example of the top 10 **Famous Flaws** that show up routinely as abstract answer choices, followed by a few more that almost made the cut.



**Necessary vs. Sufficient **- Presenting a conditional rule and then using it in an illegal backwards or opposite fashion to get to the conclusion (i.e. an illegal reversal or illegal nation). This is **frequently** the actual flaw on Flaw and Parallel Flaw questions.

- *People who have seen "The 13th" have a great understanding of the US prison system. Since Megan still hasn't seen that documentary, she must have a poor understanding of the US prison system. *
- *People who have seen "The 13th" have a great understanding of the US prison system. Jane understands the US prison system really well, so clearly she has seen "The 13th".*


**Causal** - Overconfidently concluding one possible causal interpretation of a curious fact, usually a Correlation, when other possible interpretations exist.

- *People who are enlisted in the military are more likely than those who haven't been to have excellent physiques. Evidently, military recruitment officers select their future soldiers in large part based on the applicant's fitness and strength.*


**Part vs. Whole** - Assuming that a trait that is true of each Part of a collective is therefore true of the Whole, or vice versa.

- *The military is part of the government, and the military is conducted with lots of honor and discipline. Thus, the government overall is conducted with lots of honor and discipline. *
- *Since rat poison is very harmful if ingested, each ingredient in rat poison would be very harmful if ingested.*


**Unproven vs. Proven False** - Acting like X must be false, either because there's no evidence that X is true, or because someone made a faulty argument for X being true.

- *Billy says that his uncle was related to Elvis, but he hasn't been able to connect them on Ancestry.com. Thus, Billy's uncle was ****not ****related to Elvis. *
- *Some claim that the Atlanta Braves should change their name because they are the only major sports team that still has a Native American mascot. However, the Chicago Blackhawks still have a Native American mascot. Thus, the Braves should not change their name.*


**Ad Hominem** - Dismissing a point of view because the source has a biased interest or has conflicting past behavior.

- *My friend Sameer says that social workers would be better at responding to domestic abuse calls than would armed police officers. But we can ignore this notion, since Sameer is a social worker and so his industry would benefit were cities to make this switch. *
- *My governor is telling me that we should all wear masks now, but we should not heed her words. After all; she was vocally against wearing masks a month ago.*


**Sampling** - Extrapolating/generalizing too readily from a small sample, while failing to consider that the sample might be biased, too small, or unrepresentative.

- *Charlie, a staunch Republican, is from San Francisco and works for a theater company. Since Shelia is also from San Francisco and also works for a theater company, she is probably a staunch Republican too. *
- *Today's youth prefer comic books to literature. After all, at a survey conducted at last year's Comic-Con, over 85% of the young people surveyed reported liking comic books much more than novels.*


**Inappropriate Appeal** - Your evidence is a plea for sympathy, you cite an "authority" without establishing their credibility, or you cite popular Opinion in order to judge a Factual matter.

- *I know I was speeding, Officer, but you shouldn't give me a ticket because it would ruin my Anniversary. *
- *Dr. Fauci, the government's leading expert in infectious diseases, says that coronavirus can't be transmitted by sharing the same hat. But he's wrong, for my friend Brandy says that you can transmit coronavirus by sharing hats.*


**Circular Reasoning** - The premise of an argument restates or assumes the conclusion. (these answers are almost always wrong)

- *The LSAT is clearly the hardest grad school test, because all the other tests people take to go to grad school pale in comparison to the difficulty of the LSAT.*


**Internal Contradiction** - Something the author says early on undermines or contradicts something else they say. (these answers are almost always wrong)

- *LSAT is an incredibly challenging test for anyone who takes it. However, if you commit to Eddie Spaghetti's Steady 'N Ready LSAT system, you will soon be feeling like LSAT is no harder than a kindergarten spelling quiz.*


**Equivocation** - Using the same "term" or concept to mean two very different things. (these answers are almost always wrong)

- *Some question whether Egypt condones Romania's recent military buildup on their eastern border. But clearly Egypt sanctions this activity, since in the weeks following the Romanian military's actions, Egypt issued sanctions against Romania.*







#### **A Few More Kinda-Famous Flaws**



**Intent vs. Result** - Assuming that if an action resulted in some consequence, then that consequence was intended by whoever performed the action.

- *My neighbor filled up a kiddie pool for his daughter. When he emptied it, the water spilled on to our driveway. Thus, the neighbor apparently wanted to get our driveway wet.*


**False Choice** - Acting as though you have to choose between two options, as though they're a binary / exhaustive set, even though you never established those are the only options.

- *We could use Grandma's birthday money to buy the new Mario Kart. But we could also use it to buy a rope swing for the yard. Since we can't find Mario Kart available anywhere online, we'll have to buy the rope swing.*


**Relative vs. Absolute** - Getting mixed up with Percents vs. Amounts, or confusing more/less language with yes/no language.

- *Some say that older people are never great at video games, but they're wrong. After all, Grandma is much better than Uncle Toby is at video games. *
- *This year, the work force at Foot Locker is 60% female, up from 50% female a year ago. Clearly, Foot Locker has hired more females. *

#### **Common Concerns**

The biggest chunk of Math-y Stuff is probably occupied by *Quantifiers* like "some / most / all / few". There are "Venn Diagram" overlap inferences we can make by combining certain claims.



**Quantifier Inferences**

These are the most common quantifier inferences tested. They all either involve **two **Most claims, or they involve an **All / No **statement. So if you're doing an Inference question and the stimulus has a handful of quantified claims, see if there are two Most claims about the same group. If not, then you know whatever inference they would want us to make would involve whatever **All / No **claim they've provided. Stay anchored to that one (it's a conditional claim, since it provides certainty). And ask yourself, just as you do with any conditional rule, "Can I apply this rule to any of the other facts? Do any other facts deal with the trigger of this rule (or the contrapositive trigger)?"



GIVEN:      Most A's are B                  Most A's are B

+ Most A's are C              + Most B's are ~A

INFER:    Some B's are C             There are more B's than A's





GIVEN:      Most A's are B                  Most A's are B

+ All B's are C                   +  All A's are C

INFER:    Most A's are C                  Some B's are C





GIVEN:            All A's are B                  All A's are B

+ Some A's are C            +    All A's are C

INFER:      Some B's are C              Some B's are C



It'a also important to protect yourself and be aware of ILLEGAL quantifier inferences. The most common one would be trying to chain together MOST claims.



GIVEN:      Most A's are B

Most B's are C

CAN'T INFER:      Most A's are C





**Percent vs. Number**

The percentage of females in a given group can increase by adding females to the group or by subtracting men (or both).



LSAT authors always forget about this 2nd way. They think that any percent increase in X is caused by an increase in the **raw number** of X. But it's possible that it's caused by a decrease in the number of **not-X. **



If we have a 10 person company, 6 women and 4 men,

then the company is 60% female, 40% male.



If 2 men join we'll have a 12 person company, with 6 women and 6 men.

Then the company will be 50% female, 50% male.



Notice that the company just went from being 60% female to being 50% female. An LSAT author would erroneously assume that this means that females left the company, but no females left. We have the same 6 women as we started with.



The percent of the company that is female is calculated by taking the fraction of females out of the total.



Was          Now

FEMALES        6              6

TOTAL          10            12



% female        60%          50%



LSAT authors always think that a percent is changing because the numerator is changing, and we need to understand that it can also change from the denominator changing.



Similarly, if we hear that the rate of car thefts is down (10 years ago, 15% of people had their car stolen but now only 12% of people had their car stolen), that doesn't mean that the **number **stolen cars is down.



This city might have had a population of 1 million ten years ago but now has a population of 2 million.



15% of 1 million people  <  12% of 2 million people



So the number of stolen cars is higher now, even though the rate / proportion / percentage of people who have had their car stolen is lower.



**Gambler's Fallacy **

Sometimes LSAT authors will think, "If the last three flips of the coin have been tails, then the *next *flip is more likely to be heads", but that is not true. Each flip is an independent even with a 50% probability of heads or tails.



**Too Literal with Averages / Probability**

Sometimes LSAT authors will think, "The average income in this neighborhood is $100k per household. Betty lives in this neighborhood, so her household must bring in $100k." That is interpreting average too literally. Betty's house might make way above or way below the average.



They might interpret a ratio too literally and say, "The ratio of boys to girls in this class is 5 to 7. Since three girls just left the class, the ratio must now be 5 to 4." It's possible the class started off with 20 boys and 28 girls, which is a 5 to 7 ratio. If three girls leave, then now it's 20 boys and 25 girls, which is a 4 to 5 ratio. Ratios are relative ideas, not real numbers. We're not allowed to take a real number like "3 girls left" and apply it directly to the ratio.



**Weighted Average**

When two things are mixed together, we get something in between. When we mix black coffee with white milk, we get some form of brown or tan, depending on how much of each we use.



A drop or two of white milk in black coffee will make it a dark brown.

A drop or two of black coffee in white milk will make it more of an eggshell white.

If we mix equal parts of black coffee and white milk, we get a light tan color.



Similarly, we can do this with statistics. If the average age of Company A is 40 yrs old and the average age of Company B is 30 yrs old, then when the companies merge into one, the average age of the employees will be somewhere between 30 and 40.



The way LSAT tends to test this is by saying, "The average age at Company A is 30 yrs old. After they merged with Company B, the average age of the resulting company was 35 years old."



That would allow us to infer that Company B's average age was something **higher than 35**. Because when we combine A and B, the result will be a number somewhere in between their two separate numbers.



So if we know that 35 is somewhere in between Company A's number (30) and Company B's number (?), then we know that Company B's number must be higher than 35.

Other times we'll have a **functional **goal for the answer, but not specific wording:

- If an author is relying on a comparison, we might think, "Let's look for an answer that rules out a significant difference between the two things".
- If an author is concluding that X is the explanation for some Curious Fact, we might think, "Let's look for an answer that rules out an alternate explanation or that provides some fact that must be true for X to be a plausible explanation".


Necessary Assumptions are the opposite of Objections. If we would object to an author, "hey, maybe those two people are twins!", then we can say the argument was assuming "those two people are *not *twins". If we could tell an author was assuming "Jack likes cookies", then we could object to that argument by saying "Jack doesn't like cookies".



So in some cases, we read a Necessary Assumption paragraph and can already hear something the author is assuming. In those cases, that's our pre-phrase. Other times, we don't hear an assumption, so we switch into debating mode and try to think of a possible objection. If we come up with Objection X, then we can pre-phrase, "Okay, the author is assuming that Objection X is *not *true."

to pass this Level for **Necessary Assumption**

Ideally, give it some space and then revisit the last batch of Necessary Assumption problems you tried, by going to your [**LR History**](https://app.lsatlab.com/my/history.php?type=LR) page and filtering for Necessary Assumption.



**negating answers**

One of the most reliable ways to get Necessary Assumption answers correct is to get good at the Negation Test: the correct answer, if negated, badly weakens the argument. An incorrect answer, if negated, doesn't weaken the argument. Negating a claim means *contradicting it in the most minimal fashion*. The negation of "All my friends are honest" isn't "None of my friends are honest", it's "at least one of my friends is not honest". There are a few standards to go by when negating:



- Don't negate every single answer as you read them. Try to get rid of some for easier reasons like Out of Scope or Too Strong wording. Use the negation test more to confirm the answer you're picking.


- **If the truth value of an answer hinges on a quantity word**, f.e. "Some clowns are scary", then negate the quantity term. Know your quantity yin-yangs (Some vs. None / All vs. Not all / Most vs. Not-Most, i.e. 51% vs. 49% / Many vs. Not-Many / Sometimes vs. Never)


- **If the truth value hinges on the word *not***, then just remove the *not, *f.e. negating "Taking the vacation will not deplete Luke's savings" gives us "taking the vacation *will *deplete his savings".


- **If the truth value hinges on the verb**, negate the verb, f.e. negating "Washing dishes dries out your hands" gives us "Washing dishes *does not dry out *your hands".


- **If the answer is conditional**, don't negate it. Negating a conditional does not give you a conditional. If gives you one counterexample, in which the trigger happens but the outcome does not. Instead of negating conditional answers, just mentally or physically diagram the If / Then statement and ask yourself if the author's argument made that move. Most of these will be wrong because they'll be Backwards or Opposite logic.

#### Description

About 15% of the arguments we read in LR involve **Plans** or **Recommendations**.



With a **Recommendation**, the author usually uses normative language like “should / ought / must” in her conclusion. If someone is trying to argue that we *should* do X, they are basically promising us a net gain by doing X: the upsides outweigh the downsides.



Thus, arguments making Recommendations are usually about *Comparative *reasoning. We have to compare the upsides/downsides of following the recommendation vs. not-following the recommendation.



However, in the remaining cases, the "should" language is coming from a *Conditional *place. The author is concluding that something should/shouldn't be the case because some rule compels us to do so (this is how most Principle questions work). If there’s a rule that tells us what we “should” or "shouldn't" do, then it’s really a conditional logic question, and we want to be concerned with whether the evidence establishes the trigger of the principle.



The majority of conclusions in **Principle-Strengthen **questions use normative language, and the correct answer on those questions is usually a conditional rule that has matching normative language in its outcome. On a first pass, we don't devote much time to any answer supplying a rule that doesn't have normative language matching the conclusion's.



With **Plans**, the author isn’t necessarily claiming we *should *do X or *must *do X, but merely claiming that doing X would achieve Y. The only way to dispute this author is to argue that doing X would not result in Y. Because of this, to simplify our thinking, we can switch from thinking, “**How can I accept **this Evidence, **while arguing that **the Conclusion is wrong?”, into thinking, ““**How could we follow **this Plan, **while arguing that **the Conclusion is wrong?”



These are the main issues correct answers deal with:

- is the Plan **feasible**?
- will people **cooperate** with it?
- could the Plan **backfire **and undermine the Goal?
- does the Plan address the **root problem **we're trying to solve?


If an author ever does conclude that we **must **do a certain Plan, then she is automatically assuming that (A) this Plan is feasible and effective, and that (B) there are no *other plans *we could do instead (the correct answer would usually deal with the possibility of other plans, if the author is overconfidently saying we **must **do a certain plan).

to beat this Milestone level



As you're reviewing questions about Plans, ask yourself questions like:

- What was the PLAN? What was the GOAL?
- Did the correct answer deal with Feasibility? Cooperation? Backfire? Root Cause?


If the question feels like it was more about a Recommendation, ask yourself questions like:

- Did the correct answer provide a rule whose Trigger matches the Evidence and whose Outcome matches the Conclusion?
- Or did the correct answer talk about some *other *consideration the author hadn't yet addressed, some difference between the world in which we follow the Recommendation vs. the world in which we don't?
- Or did the correct answer do a *Weighing Tradeoffs *thing, where it explained whether the upside outweighs the downside (or vice versa)?

#### Description

There are three varieties of **Principle-Conform**

- The stimulus is a principle (or pair of principles)
- The answers are principles
- There are no explicit principles, just implied/illustrated ones


**Principles in Stimulus**

When the stimulus is providing an explicit principle (or two), we're usually being tested on our ability to apply a conditional rule.



To find an answer that Conforms to this principle, we want an answer where the situation described matches the Trigger of the principle, and the judgment concluded matches the Outcome of the principle. (Even when the principles aren't expressed as conditionals, there will still usually be two things we need to make sure match up).



**Principles in Answers**

It's essentially the same mindset as a Principle-Strengthen question. We read the argument and then anticipate the principle underlying the argument by pre-phrasing "IF evidence, THEN conclusion". We expect the correct answer to *Link *language from the Evidence to the Conclusion's language. All that's different is that because the question stem says *conform *to the author's reasoning, we can't pick an answer that's overly strong. (That usually doesn't matter, but occasionally it does)



**Implicit Principles**

These feel similar to Parallel reasoning questions, because we're given an argument in the stimulus, and then each answer choice is its own argument on its own unique topic. We're looking for an underlying similarity to connect the correct answer to the original argument.



On Parallel questions, those similarities come more in the form of technical / structural similarities (i.e. "Since the original argument had a *Most *premise and a *Some *premise, the correct answer's argument will also have those features").



But with Principle-Conform it's more about the underlying gist or rule-of-thumb. We don't try to diagram the arguments. We just want to articulate a general moral to the story or operating principle that's guiding the author's thinking, and then we try to find an answer that matches that.

#### Description

Questions involving multiple quantified words are usually testing our ability to see when two traits do / don't have to overlap, and to what degree.



COMMON QUANTIFIERS

**                    100%*** - all, any, each, every, *(or universal nouns)

**    more than 50%*** - most, usually, typically, generally, majority*

**      less than 50%*** - few, rarely, infrequently*

**at least a handful*** - many, commonly, often*

**          at least one*** - some, sometimes, can, not all, not always, need not*



If we see a conditional-strength quantifier like "No A's are B", we think

A → ~B

B → ~A



If there are any additional facts about A or B, then we can derive a Trait Overlap inference.

*    If we know  * Some X's are **B**

* and we know*                    All **B**'s are ~A

*we can derive *  Some  X's            are  ~A



*    If we know  * Most X's are **B**

* and we know*                  All **B**'s are ~A

*we can derive *  Most  X's            are  ~A



*      but if we know  * Most **B**'s are X

*        and we know*      All **B**'s are ~A

*we can only derive *  Some X's are ~A



In the first two examples, we can hook the conditional onto the end of the first statement, and that allows us to "chain" the trigger of the first claim to the outcome of the conditional.



In the third example, the overlapping term is the trigger in both claims, so we can't "chain" the ideas together. But we still know for sure that there are some X's who have the property of being B, and thus they also must have the property of being ~A.



Sometimes, we can infer something from **two Most claims**.



GIVEN:      Most A's are B          Most A's are B

+ Most A's are C      + Most B's are ~A



INFER:    Some B's are C      There are more B's than A's



Then there's a famous ***incorrect*** inference from two Most claims:



GIVEN:    Most A's are B

+ Most B's are C



INFER:      nothing  (but LSAT authors think, "Most A's are C")

#### Description

There are about a baker's dozen **Famous Flaws** that show up a lot on these problems, especially within the answer choices:



- **Necessary vs. Sufficient **- taking a conditional relationship and but then illegally thinking through it in backwards or opposite fashion.


- **Causal** - overconfidently concluding one possible causal interpretation of a curious fact, usually a Correlation, when other possible interpretations exist


- **Part vs. Whole **- Assuming that a trait that is true of each Part of a collective is therefore true of the Whole, or vice versa.


- **Unproven vs. Proven False **- Pointing out that someone failed to prove X is true, and then concluding that this means that X is false.


- **Ad Hominem** - Dismissing a point of view because the source has a biased interest or has conflicting past behavior.


- **Sampling** - Extrapolating/generalizing too readily from a small sample, while failing to consider that the sample might be biased, too small, or unrepresentative.

- **False Choice** - Acting as though you have to choose between two options, as though they're a binary / exhaustive set, even though you never established those are the only options.


- **Relative vs. Absolute** - getting mixed up with Percents vs..Amounts, or confusing more/less language with yes/no language.


- **Inappropriate Appeal** - your evidence is a plea for sympathy, you cite an "authority" without establishing credibility, or you favor one source's opinion over another source's, without justifying your pick.


Almost always wrong answers:

- **Circular Reasoning **- The premise of an argument restates or assumes the conclusion.


- **Internal Contradiction** - Something the author says undermines or contradicts something else they say


- **Equivocation** - Using the same "term" or concept to mean two very different things.

With a **Recommendation**, the author usually uses normative language like “should / ought / must” in her conclusion. If someone is trying to argue that we *should* do X, they are basically promising us a net gain by doing X: the upsides outweigh the downsides.

#### Description

There are three varieties of **Principle-Conform**

- The stimulus is a principle (or pair of principles)
- The answers are principles
- There are no explicit principles, just implied/illustrated ones


**Principles in Stimulus**

When the stimulus is providing an explicit principle (or two), we're usually being tested on our ability to apply a conditional rule.



To find an answer that Conforms to this principle, we want an answer where the situation described matches the Trigger of the principle, and the judgment concluded matches the Outcome of the principle. (Even when the principles aren't expressed as conditionals, there will still usually be two things we need to make sure match up).



**Principles in Answers**

It's essentially the same mindset as a Principle-Strengthen question. We read the argument and then anticipate the principle underlying the argument by pre-phrasing "IF evidence, THEN conclusion". We expect the correct answer to *Link *language from the Evidence to the Conclusion's language. All that's different is that because the question stem says *conform *to the author's reasoning, we can't pick an answer that's overly strong. (That usually doesn't matter, but occasionally it does)



**Implicit Principles**

These feel similar to Parallel reasoning questions, because we're given an argument in the stimulus, and then each answer choice is its own argument on its own unique topic. We're looking for an underlying similarity to connect the correct answer to the original argument.



On Parallel questions, those similarities come more in the form of technical / structural similarities (i.e. "Since the original argument had a *Most *premise and a *Some *premise, the correct answer's argument will also have those features").

#### **Common Concerns**

The biggest chunk of Math-y Stuff is probably occupied by *Quantifiers* like "some / most / all / few". There are "Venn Diagram" overlap inferences we can make by combining certain claims.



**Quantifier Inferences**

These are the most common quantifier inferences tested. They all either involve **two **Most claims, or they involve an **All / No **statement. So if you're doing an Inference question and the stimulus has a handful of quantified claims, see if there are two Most claims about the same group. If not, then you know whatever inference they would want us to make would involve whatever **All / No **claim they've provided. Stay anchored to that one (it's a conditional claim, since it provides certainty). And ask yourself, just as you do with any conditional rule, "Can I apply this rule to any of the other facts? Do any other facts deal with the trigger of this rule (or the contrapositive trigger)?"



GIVEN:      Most A's are B                  Most A's are B

+ Most A's are C              + Most B's are ~A

INFER:    Some B's are C             There are more B's than A's





GIVEN:      Most A's are B                  Most A's are B

+ All B's are C                   +  All A's are C

INFER:    Most A's are C                  Some B's are C





GIVEN:            All A's are B                  All A's are B

+ Some A's are C            +    All A's are C

INFER:      Some B's are C              Some B's are C



It'a also important to protect yourself and be aware of ILLEGAL quantifier inferences. The most common one would be trying to chain together MOST claims.



GIVEN:      Most A's are B

Most B's are C

CAN'T INFER:      Most A's are C





**Percent vs. Number**

The percentage of females in a given group can increase by adding females to the group or by subtracting men (or both).



LSAT authors always forget about this 2nd way. They think that any percent increase in X is caused by an increase in the **raw number** of X. But it's possible that it's caused by a decrease in the number of **not-X. **



If we have a 10 person company, 6 women and 4 men,

then the company is 60% female, 40% male.



If 2 men join we'll have a 12 person company, with 6 women and 6 men.

Then the company will be 50% female, 50% male.



Notice that the company just went from being 60% female to being 50% female. An LSAT author would erroneously assume that this means that females left the company, but no females left. We have the same 6 women as we started with.



The percent of the company that is female is calculated by taking the fraction of females out of the total.



Was          Now

FEMALES        6              6

TOTAL          10            12



% female        60%          50%



LSAT authors always think that a percent is changing because the numerator is changing, and we need to understand that it can also change from the denominator changing.



Similarly, if we hear that the rate of car thefts is down (10 years ago, 15% of people had their car stolen but now only 12% of people had their car stolen), that doesn't mean that the **number **stolen cars is down.



This city might have had a population of 1 million ten years ago but now has a population of 2 million.



15% of 1 million people  <  12% of 2 million people



So the number of stolen cars is higher now, even though the rate / proportion / percentage of people who have had their car stolen is lower.



**Gambler's Fallacy **

Sometimes LSAT authors will think, "If the last three flips of the coin have been tails, then the *next *flip is more likely to be heads", but that is not true. Each flip is an independent even with a 50% probability of heads or tails.



**Too Literal with Averages / Probability**

Sometimes LSAT authors will think, "The average income in this neighborhood is $100k per household. Betty lives in this neighborhood, so her household must bring in $100k." That is interpreting average too literally. Betty's house might make way above or way below the average.



They might interpret a ratio too literally and say, "The ratio of boys to girls in this class is 5 to 7. Since three girls just left the class, the ratio must now be 5 to 4." It's possible the class started off with 20 boys and 28 girls, which is a 5 to 7 ratio. If three girls leave, then now it's 20 boys and 25 girls, which is a 4 to 5 ratio. Ratios are relative ideas, not real numbers. We're not allowed to take a real number like "3 girls left" and apply it directly to the ratio.



**Weighted Average**

When two things are mixed together, we get something in between. When we mix black coffee with white milk, we get some form of brown or tan, depending on how much of each we use.



A drop or two of white milk in black coffee will make it a dark brown.

A drop or two of black coffee in white milk will make it more of an eggshell white.

If we mix equal parts of black coffee and white milk, we get a light tan color.



Similarly, we can do this with statistics. If the average age of Company A is 40 yrs old and the average age of Company B is 30 yrs old, then when the companies merge into one, the average age of the employees will be somewhere between 30 and 40.



The way LSAT tends to test this is by saying, "The average age at Company A is 30 yrs old. After they merged with Company B, the average age of the resulting company was 35 years old."



That would allow us to infer that Company B's average age was something **higher than 35**. Because when we combine A and B, the result will be a number somewhere in between their two separate numbers.



So if we know that 35 is somewhere in between Company A's number (30) and Company B's number (?), then we know that Company B's number must be higher than 35.

**Causation** is one of the three main Reasoning Structures that permeate Logical Reasoning (along with Comparison, Conditional Logic). Here's a rough sense of how it usually shows up in various question types.

[Start](https://app.lsatlab.com/practice?type=LR&questiontypes=Principle-Conform&difficulty=5&tests=A%2CF1997%2C1%2C2%2C3%2C4%2C5%2C6%2C7%2C8%2C9%2C10%2C11%2C12%2C13%2C14%2C15%2C16%2C17%2C18%2C21%2C23%2C101%2C102%2C103%2C104%2C105%2C106%2C107%2C108%2C109%2C110%2C111%2C112%2C113%2C114%2C115%2C116%2C117%2C118%2C119%2C120%2C121%2C122%2C123%2C124%2C125%2C126%2C127%2C128%2C129%2C130%2C131%2C132%2C133%2C134%2C135%2C136%2C137%2C138%2C139%2C140%2C141%2C142%2C143%2C144%2C145%2C146%2C147%2C148%2C149%2C150%2C151%2C152%2C153%2C154%2C155%2C156%2C157%2C158%2C159&showall=true&studyactivityid=717&scheduleactivityid=13250530&milestoneid=173&returnurl=https%3A%2F%2Fapp.lsatlab.com%2Fstudy-plan)

#### Description

There are three varieties of **Principle-Conform**

- The stimulus is a principle (or pair of principles)
- The answers are principles
- There are no explicit principles, just implied/illustrated ones


**Principles in Stimulus**

When the stimulus is providing an explicit principle (or two), we're usually being tested on our ability to apply a conditional rule.



To find an answer that Conforms to this principle, we want an answer where the situation described matches the Trigger of the principle, and the judgment concluded matches the Outcome of the principle. (Even when the principles aren't expressed as conditionals, there will still usually be two things we need to make sure match up).



**Principles in Answers**

It's essentially the same mindset as a Principle-Strengthen question. We read the argument and then anticipate the principle underlying the argument by pre-phrasing "IF evidence, THEN conclusion". We expect the correct answer to *Link *language from the Evidence to the Conclusion's language. All that's different is that because the question stem says *conform *to the author's reasoning, we can't pick an answer that's overly strong. (That usually doesn't matter, but occasionally it does)



**Implicit Principles**

These feel similar to Parallel reasoning questions, because we're given an argument in the stimulus, and then each answer choice is its own argument on its own unique topic. We're looking for an underlying similarity to connect the correct answer to the original argument.



On Parallel questions, those similarities come more in the form of technical / structural similarities (i.e. "Since the original argument had a *Most *premise and a *Some *premise, the correct answer's argument will also have those features").



But with Principle-Conform it's more about the underlying gist or rule-of-thumb. We don't try to diagram the arguments. We just want to articulate a general moral to the story or operating principle that's guiding the author's thinking, and then we try to find an answer that matches that.

to pass this **level**.
