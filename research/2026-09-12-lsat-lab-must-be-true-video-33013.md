## The inference mindset · 00:00:17 to 00:00:49

Must Be True belongs to the inference family. Treat the stimulus as accepted information rather than an argument to challenge. Do not search for a conclusion, premise, flaw, or assumption. Ask what the supplied information forces you to conclude.

## The three conditional skills · 00:02:22 to 00:11:14

1. **Take contrapositives.** Every component changes sides and changes signs. Compound `and` and `or` relationships also change when negated.
2. **Chain rules.** A valid chain needs either the same idea in opposite positions or opposite versions of an idea in the same position. The second arrangement becomes visible after taking a contrapositive.
3. **Recognize when a fact triggers a rule.** If a fact establishes the sufficient condition, infer the necessary condition. If a fact denies the necessary condition, apply the contrapositive.


## What a bridge is · 00:03:03 to 00:07:47

A bridge is the common condition that lets two rules interlock. In `A → B` and `B → C`, the first rule ends at `B` and the second starts from `B`. That makes `B` the bridge and permits `A → C`.

Merely repeating an idea is not enough. `A → B` and `C → B` both end at `B`, so neither rule continues from the other. They do not chain.

For a longer chain such as `A → B → C → D`, a correct answer may test `A → C`, `B → D`, or `A → D`. The test does not always use the full head-to-tail inference.

## When to take the contrapositive · 00:08:18 to 00:10:07

The contrapositive often hides both kinds of inference. It can expose the middle term needed for a chain or show how a fact activates a rule. Do not turn every rule into unnecessary paperwork. If the inference is already visible, use it. If the rules are not connecting, contrapose one and check again.

## What to resist · 00:11:15 to 00:12:45

- Do not chain rules that lack a valid interlocking condition.
- Do not reverse `A → B` into `B → A`.
- Do not negate `A → B` into `not A → not B`.
- Build enough repetition that identifying and contraposing conditionals becomes automatic.

## Certainty creates the conditional · 00:13:00 to 00:15:18

Trigger words are clues, but certainty is the test. “All” can create a conditional guarantee. “When” does not create one if the main clause says something happens only “often.” Likewise, “some,” “most,” and “can” do not establish the certainty required for a standard conditional rule by themselves.

## Diagram when the notation will do work · 01:21:45 to 01:23:07

Diagramming is useful when it makes the structure easier to inspect. In a longer chain, it helps expose intermediate inferences and makes illegal reversals or negations easier to see. It can also make the contrapositive of the full chain available. If the relationship is already obvious from the language, the diagram need not become extra paperwork.

## Test a conditional answer from its trigger · 01:25:52 to 01:27:59

For a conditional answer choice, inspect the trigger first. Ask whether the stimulus ever establishes that idea in the trigger position, either directly or through a valid contrapositive. If it does not, the answer cannot be derived. The same positional check works on the proposed result. An idea appearing somewhere in the stimulus is not enough; it must appear in a usable position.
