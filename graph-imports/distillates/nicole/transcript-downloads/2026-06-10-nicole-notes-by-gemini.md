# Nicole Spinelli, 2026-06-10

Foundations session: the hostile-perspective philosophy, validity as binary, the four-step argument framework, then a hard role question worked end to end.

## LSAT concepts

- **Validity**: "A valid argument is an argument where it's an impossibility to deny the conclusion, because we accept the evidence." Binary, never a spectrum: "anything that's not 100 percent guaranteed by the evidence is 0 percent valid." A slightly-off conclusion and an absurd conclusion are equally invalid.
- **Premise indicators (FABS)**: for, after all, because, since. Joshua's added refinement: these are two-way indicators. What follows is always evidence, and what sits immediately before is a conclusion.
- **Conclusion, two-step test**: (1) it must be the author's own opinion, (2) it must receive explicit support from at least one other statement.
- **Opposing conclusion versus intermediate conclusion**: an intermediate (sub) conclusion is still the author's; an opposing conclusion belongs to the other side. Nicole conflated the two and Joshua drew the line hard. Signal phrases: "it has been argued," "some biologists," "some people." "If they bring up any other person's belief, the author is rejecting it by default."
- **Referential language**: words like "this" compress a prior claim. "Think of that word like the plus button on your phone. You can expand it and see what it's referring to."
- **Role questions**: the task is never what the underlined statement does generally, but "what is the relationship between the part that is underlined and the conclusion." Take first person from the conclusion: "pretend the main character is the conclusion. What is this part doing to you?"
- **Technical vocabulary in answer choices**: "probably" means literally over 51 percent, more likely than not. "Sufficiency" means enough. "Hypothesis" means a potential cause, causation.
- **Attacking sufficiency versus asserting falsity**: a author who calls a claim "overly hasty" is not saying it is false, only that the evidence does not get you there. "The LSAT wants to test our ability to see gray. We naturally see black and white."
- **Strengthen and weaken**: once you have the loophole objection, strengthen means finding the answer that rules your objection out; weaken means finding the answer that points at it.

## Teaching moves

- **The hostile perspective, framed through defense law.** He opened with a role play: someone comes to you caught with a hand in the cookie jar. "Before you can defend the case, you have to know where the problems are in the case." Every question type is the same move: find the crack. "Whatever you find with that crack, that's the most valuable asset on the test, because now you can do with it what you want." Expose it to weaken, patch it to strengthen. He positions this as deliberately unlike other prep: "the way I would go about it is very different than maybe other places."
- **The bridge image for strengthen.** You do not inspect a bridge optimistically. You look for where a natural disaster would break it, "not because you want it to happen, but you have to see where it could go wrong."
- **The jurisdiction analogy for accepting evidence.** Nicole is from Miami; Joshua is in Canada. "You wouldn't come to Canada and say, in Florida we have stand your ground laws." You either fight the LSAT or accept its world. "The verdict you're fighting for is the conclusion. We never accept the conclusion. We always try to deny the conclusion while simultaneously accepting the evidence. That tension is everything on the LSAT. Everything else is just for fun."
- **The border metaphor for scope.** "Pretend the conclusion is crossing the border. You cannot cross the border without declaring. If there's anything mentioned in the conclusion that's not mentioned in the evidence, we have a problem, because you snuck something in."
- **The Florida trap, built to fail.** He wrote "Florida is in the USA, therefore Nicole is in the USA," and let her call it valid. Then: "But if you're from Florida, does that mean you're in Florida?" He used the miss to name the underlying habit: "we autofill when we read. We don't even consciously do it. What you're going to do is unwire it." Nicole then repaired the conclusion herself ("therefore Nicole is from the US").
- **The four-step framework, stated as universal.** (1) Identify the conclusion, recall and rephrase it. (2) Identify the evidence by asking "why should I believe that?" (3) Attack: say the mantra "just because [evidence], that doesn't mean [conclusion]," then answer "what if?" with a specific scenario. (4) Prephrase the answer. He stressed the mantra should be spoken aloud: "you'll be shocked how often it naturally will just come to you."
- **The objection as a comprehension safety check.** His strongest argument for step 3: "You can't attack an argument and see the crack without really understanding it. If you can't see the problem, that means you missed something. You misread something." And: "never go into the answers without your objection." He extends it to stamina self-monitoring: losing the ability to find objections tells you when you have stopped absorbing.
- **"Every argument is very stupid."** Repeated several times as a confidence tool. "There's no such thing as a good argument on the LSAT. They just hide it very well." Spoiler framing: "every conclusion is invalid. Every single one. Without exception."
- **Correcting an over-clever objection.** Nicole tried to attack the T-Rex evidence itself ("what if size makes it faster?"). Joshua blocked it: "we can't do that, because they told us it did. That's in the evidence." Objections attack the gap, never the premises.
- **"Slow down, buddy."** His plain-language gloss of "overly hasty inference," repeated until it stuck. "He's not saying you're objectively wrong. He's saying come on man, relax, you're getting way too carried away."
- **Letting her hear herself say the answer.** Nicole reasoned aloud that the conclusion was "too fast for one piece of evidence, the evidence is not enough," while simultaneously rejecting answer C for referencing sufficiency. Joshua had already made her define sufficiency as "enough," then pointed out the collision: "I'm using only strictly your definition. You are saying the answer choice without realizing it." He laughed rather than corrected, and let the contradiction land.
- **Role questions as a second language.** "There's almost a language you have to learn that's very particular to role questions. You can learn it in theory, but a lot of it is hearing the language, like learning Italian."
- **Deliberately deprioritizing timing.** "Time is the least of your concerns right now. You should not be thinking about time whatsoever." Volume and depth first; stamina builds like a muscle. He warned against overtraining: "the return on the seventh question versus the first diminishes dramatically. You're just putting yourself through pain." And named the trap: "when I'm doing something difficult, it seems like I'm doing something productive, but those aren't always the same."

## Worked examples

- **Florida validity toy argument.** "Florida is in the USA, therefore Nicole is in the USA." Invalid, because being *from* Florida does not put you *in* Florida. Nicole produced the weakening scenario herself: "Nicole is actually in Argentina." She then identified "in" as the load-bearing word. Joshua added a parallel invalid version to prove there are no degrees of badness.
- **PT 150, Section 2, Question 23, Tyrannosaurus Rex** (role question). Nicole brought it in as a question she and her study partner had both failed to resolve; she had found it via Google on a GMAT Club page and Joshua initially doubted it was a real LSAT item before pulling it up. Structure worked out live: sentence one is the opposing side's conclusion, flagged by "it has been argued," with "since it would not have been able to chase down its prey" as their evidence. "This, however, is an overly hasty inference" is the author's main conclusion, where "this" refers back to the scavenger claim. The author's support is that the T-Rex's prey, if larger, would probably have been slower. Joshua mocked that support as barely an argument ("if it had dark skin and a hat, it would have been me"); Nicole supplied "if it walks like a duck and quacks like a duck, it's a duck," and later "it could also be a goose," which became the shared shorthand for the whole question. Answer analysis: A ("logically inconsistent with the conclusion") is the trap because the author never claims the scavenger view is false. B ("probably false") fails on the technical meaning of probably, over 51 percent. C, calling into question the sufficiency of the evidence, is correct, and Nicole articulated it in her own words before she recognized it in the answer.
