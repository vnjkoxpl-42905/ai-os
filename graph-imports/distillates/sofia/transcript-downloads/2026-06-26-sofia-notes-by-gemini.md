## LSAT concepts

- **Read for why, not what.** "Your goal is not to read the what. Your goal is to read to understand the why." The content is interchangeable ("we could be talking about kangaroos, we could be talking about the World Cup"); the task is structural reading, "which is very counterintuitive to what you've learned in college, high school, and everything else in academia."
- **The four moves (the "SEER" method).** Every paragraph, and the passage overall, falls into one or at most two of: **compare** (setting two competing ideas or theories side by side), **explain** (defining a concept, giving background or context, describing a belief system without judging it), **evaluate** (the author affirms or criticizes; "the author is stepping in to judge something, this is where the author's voice often comes out"), **recommend** (the author proposes a solution, a prescription). Label each paragraph, then label the passage.
- **Evaluate versus recommend.** Both are the author's opinion; the difference is criticism versus proposed solution. "It's easy to criticize. Recommendation is when the author is actually proposing a solution." Structurally, recommendation almost always follows an evaluation, usually in a later paragraph.
- **The main-point hierarchy.** Recommendation beats evaluation beats explain. If the passage contains an evaluation, the main point must nod to that evaluation. If it also contains a recommendation, the recommendation is the main point instead. If there is neither, and the passage compares, the main point is the comparison plus the side the author takes.
- **Comparison always has a winner.** "Every single time there's a comparison, the author is going to take one of the sides." If the side is never stated outright, default to whichever side the author spent more words on.
- **Author's voice is the whole game.** The evaluation or recommendation only counts if it is the author's. "If they tell you about somebody from Timbuktu and their beliefs, that's not the relevant point. I want to know what the author thinks." He named identifying the author's voice as the hardest RC skill to acquire and the LSAT's favorite sleight of hand.
- **Relationship questions, not content questions.** "What is the purpose of the second paragraph?" is really "what is the relationship between that paragraph and the main point." A word-meaning question asks the word's purpose in its paragraph, then that paragraph's relation to the main point. Zoom in, zoom out.
- **Main point versus main purpose.** Main purpose asks only which move the passage makes. Main point asks the move plus its content.
- **Main-point answers stay general,** but generality is the tiebreak, not the rule. The non-negotiable is that the answer contains the recommendation. Sofia's own framing, which Joshua endorsed enthusiastically: "think of every answer for RC as a necessary assumption question." Conservative, must-be-true answers.
- **Inference means must be true.** Not a logical guess. Every RC answer must be traceable to a specific line.
- **Context locators.** For a word-in-context question, "look at the sentence right after it, and especially the sentence right before it. The actual sentence it's in is usually not the one you have to worry about."
- **Prephrase before reading answers.** "This is make or break to get good at RC. We want to know what the answer has to have. If you don't have that clearly, explicitly stated, the questions take you for a ride."

## Teaching moves

- **The grocery list opener.** He wrote "eggs, milk, onions, pasta, olive oil" and asked what it was. She said ingredients. "Your goal isn't to remember what's first, what's the milk, what's the onions. Your goal is to say why did the author take the time to write this list." That list became the running touchstone all session for what not to do.
- **The contract analogy, extended and reused.** "Read it as though it's a contract. No lawyer in their right mind would ever try to memorize a contract, that would be a ridiculous waste of energy. The passage is not going anywhere." He let Sofia produce the point herself, asking what she would do with a contract; she answered "the underlying rules, the simple version of the rules someone needs to follow," and he took it. He returned to it twice more, once for structure ("this is where we find out about your restrictions to be out after a certain time") and once for evidentiary discipline: "Show me the paperwork. It's not a free reading exercise, it's a contractual reading."
- **The sister analogy for relational questions.** Sofia has three older sisters. "Is Katrina a younger sister or an older sister?" She answered "in relation to me older, in relation to Natalie younger." He landed it: "She's not inherently a younger or older sister. Same with the second paragraph. It's what it is in relation to the main point."
- **The boss pitch analogy for why passages sound balanced.** "You wouldn't go to your boss and put the paper on the table and say we're going to do this. You'd say I investigated this option, I asked somebody else, and after examining all my options, this is the best one. Even if you know the outcome, you present like you've done your research." Same with RC authors: they perform open-mindedness but have exactly one point.
- **Two cavemen.** For comparison passages: "think of it as two cavemen arguing. Me good, me bad. Which side is the author taking?"
- **Active reading defined operationally.** "When people say to actively read, the way to actively read is by taking that guess. Guess by the first paragraph, then test against your guess and update it as you go. That's how you remember everything."
- **Praising her instinct even while correcting her answer.** When Sofia said she had learned that generality does not matter after all, he stopped her: "Let me hold you off. You actually had a good instinct." He then built the explicit two-level hierarchy (recommendation is non-negotiable; among answers that have it, prefer the general one) rather than letting her overcorrect.
- **Naming her current method honestly.** "You haven't learned any methods, so you're just freestyling it. By vibes." Sofia adopted the word and used it herself for the rest of the session. He paired it with the diagnosis: "people that are bright get away with it by pure cognitive horsepower, but then the passage gets tricky and you get slaughtered, which is what happened in the last passage."
- **Deprioritizing time, twice, firmly.** "The last thing in the world you should be worried about is your time. Time comes with competency. Focusing on getting faster doesn't make you faster. Getting better makes you faster."
- **Practice tests are the mirror, not the gym.** His argument against her taking another full PT: "Looking in the mirror doesn't make you stronger. You want to look in the mirror to see if you're making progress, but it's not the place where you get stronger." Plus the review-latency argument: after a full test you cannot remember your reasoning on section 2 question 16, but after one passage you remember it because it was three minutes ago. Plus material conservation.
- **Underline the supporting line for every answer.** "You'll hate it at first, but it's absolutely going to change the game. You'll be surprised how often you'll be like, wait, I completely read that wrong when I underlined it."
- **Pacing himself deliberately.** "I don't want to overload too much in one day because often I have a problem with doing that on the RC days. Tell me if I go too fast." He said it twice and deferred the question-type taxonomy to a later session.

## Worked examples

RC section, the eyewitness interviewing passage (four paragraphs: police interviews, the cognitive interview, hypnosis, instructed eye closure).

- **Passage mapping.** Sofia's independent first pass: P1 explain, P2 explain, P3 evaluate, P4 evaluate (she was torn on recommendation but "I don't see any prescriptive words"). Joshua's corrections: P1 explain, setting up the problem, police want to maximize cooperative witness recall and the cognitive interview is one technique for it. P2 explain, with a note of evaluation because the author calls the procedure complex and says even trained officers struggle: "whenever the author is giving any sort of critique, that's always under evaluate. We don't have to lose our minds, we just note it." P3 compare or evaluate, both acceptable, the author introducing hypnosis "just to tear it down" (easier to administer but does not improve accuracy, may deteriorate it, produces false confidence, and does not work on everyone). P4 recommendation: instructed eye closure is easy, universal, and accurate, so it delivers the cognitive interview's benefits without its costs. Sofia arrived at "so this is a recommendation" herself.
- **Question 6, main point.** She had been split between A and E on vibes, chose E, then switched to A because E's mention of instructed eye closure felt too specific. Wrong. E is correct because it captures the recommendation, which outranks generality. Her own summary: "instructed eye closure is the reason why I was turned off by this answer, but it's actually why the answer is correct."
- **The "except" question.** She answered D correctly and quickly. Her method, unprompted: having organized the information mentally, she could go straight back to the relevant paragraph. Joshua flagged that "according to the passage" questions are localized: just find it.
- **The "alibis" word-in-context question.** She had originally answered B, treating alibi as an isolated term. Applying the new relational method live, she switched to C and was right. Her reasoning path: alibi's role in its paragraph is to show why these interview methods are used at all, and that paragraph serves the main point about the most effective procedure. B additionally fails because the passage is about eliciting information from cooperating eyewitnesses, not from suspects.
- **The hypnosis consequences inference question.** She was between B and E, leaning E on "most notably," and had originally answered A. Joshua made her prephrase first: the paragraph explicitly gives two consequences, accuracy may deteriorate and hypnosis creates a "false confidence" effect, so the answer must nod to one of them. A fails because "accuracy may deteriorate" says nothing about the ratio of accurate to inaccurate information: "is that 99 percent to 90 percent, or 100 percent to 6 percent? We have no idea." Sofia restated it herself: "we know that it's inaccurate, but we don't know if it's more inaccurate than accurate." B fails on attribution, since the false confidence belongs to witnesses, not interviewers. C is never mentioned. E fails because the passage says some witnesses are not susceptible at all, not that they become less susceptible over the process. D is correct: if hypnosis makes a witness fully confident in incorrect information, asking how sure they are tells the interviewer nothing.
