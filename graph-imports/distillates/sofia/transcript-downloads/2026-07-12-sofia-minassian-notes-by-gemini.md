# Sofia Minassian, 2026-07-12

A near two-hour session: a full untimed RC passage (Chicano theater / Luis Valdez) worked question by question, where Joshua teaches profile-passage structure and the skeptical elimination method, then thirty minutes walking her through the LSAC accommodations application form.

## LSAT concepts

- **Profile / spotlight passage** — Joshua uses "profile passage" and "spotlight passage" interchangeably. Signature: "the first paragraph especially will tell you this person is good and they'll probably list, almost every single time, the reasons as to what makes them good or what makes them impressive... they'll give you a road map." His Rosa Parks illustration: three named qualities up front, then a paragraph each. "Each paragraph will typically be addressing one of the features they identified in the first paragraph." Main point usually sits in paragraph one. Calibration: "for the most, like major 70%, I wouldn't say all, but majority."
- **Passage type vs paragraph move** — two different layers. The passage as a whole gets a type (profile/spotlight); each paragraph gets a move (explain, evaluate, recommend, anticipate). In profile passages "the first category will usually be explain... almost always."
- **The value of categorizing** — "for you to have a framework to be able to see past the information and to categorize it... with every single categorization there's certain features of that type of passage."
- **Anticipation from paragraph structure** — Sofia predicted paragraph 2 would move to the Chicano theater movement from paragraph 1's closing; Joshua endorsed it. Caveat delivered immediately: "these are like majority, but we don't want to fall into mindlessly falling off course." Sofia: "we don't want to be super rigid."
- **Meaning-in-context method** — refresh the paragraph's category first, then use the sentence before and after, then "come up with a word or phrase, either one of the two, that we could substitute in for the same word prior to choosing your answer."
- **Cause and effect in RC** — the "palpable immediacy" question turns on which thing causes which: the skits had immediacy *because* they were based on the participants' personal experiences. He explicitly bridges from her LR work: "because you went to the LR curriculum, you know how there's causation."
- **No half-right answers** — "we can never accept a half-right answer. Never ever... they'll throw in a highly attractive portion of the answer, but there'll be a part you're just like, I don't know about that part." And: "if there's one thing off, you have to eliminate it."
- **Skeptical mindset over best-answer mindset** — "the skeptical mindset is what you want to adopt, not which one is the best. Which one is trying to trick me? All of them are trying to trick me. Which one is one I can't sniff out any sort of blood out of?"
- **Author-stance isolation (agreement questions)** — "most of these questions, it comes down to realizing most of the answers the author doesn't even have a point of view on them." Procedure for two-party agreement questions: take each party in isolation, ask what the author thinks about each answer independently, eliminate everything he has no stance on, then run the second party against the survivors. "If the author doesn't have a stance on something, you can't have an agreement towards it."
- **Written notes as retention, not ritual** — the P1/P2/P3 note method exists so you stop re-reading: "people, when they don't do that, end up reading the passage like three times during the time they're doing the questions."

## Teaching moves

- **The sticky-note elimination drill.** The centerpiece of the session. When down to two: "take the time to not just think of what's wrong between the two answers... Take your piece of paper. Usually sticky notes are useful. Make a bullet list of all of the issues that come to mind." He wants the exact word or phrase named, and accepts vibes as a starting point only: "even if it's just a vibe, get it out of your head and put it on paper." The mechanism: "when you have to take pen to paper, you'll struggle to say why the answer is wrong. You'll be like, ah, it just doesn't feel right. That's not enough." And the payoff: "you won't be able to write something wrong with the right answer. Even though you might not think it's right, you won't be able to say why it's wrong. And that's going to be the correct answer."
- **Cut off the "what I like about it" instinct mid-sentence.** Sofia started "The thing is what I liked about C —" and Joshua interrupted: "I'm going to cut you off. I only want to know your criticisms. Only your criticisms." He explains afterward: "you turned into the different mode. You went into which answer is the best answer."
- **But don't manufacture objections.** "Don't force it by the way... often people will find ways to find why C is wrong. They'll do some retroactive like, I don't like the word major, I don't like the word efforts. It's like, you don't believe that though. You're just saying that because you have to find a way." He praised her precisely for failing to find a flaw in C.
- **Ask for a top three, not an answer.** On the hardest question he changed the frame: "I actually want you to tell me your top three answers," then "our goal is not to see which one is the right answer. We're trying to find which one of those are the wrong answer. Let's attack it."
- **Demand a justification for every answer change.** "I always want you to give me a justification for why you switch, because the worst thing is choosing an answer based off of lack of confidence on your own answer."
- **Repeat the question back as a pressure test, then defuse it.** "I kind of said it, not in a way to say are you sure, but I wanted to make sure I heard you right." Later, explicitly: "if I push back, I don't want you to take it as me saying you're wrong. I just want to make you feel uncomfortable." Sofia read it correctly: "You're challenging me because at the end of the day, not being 100% is not being 100%."
- **Use absence of a dilemma as evidence.** "Were there any other attractive options? I'll take that as a no, because if you had, we would have said it... that tells me that you're actually correct. Because whenever you're down to two options, that's where your issue kicks in."
- **Deliberately let her wrestle.** "this is a really tricky question. That's why I wanted you to wrestle with it, which you did." And "I like to see your brain turn and twist."
- **Have her state the main point before any question is shown.** She did, then chose the main-point answer instantly. He named the causation: "you kind of essentially said the main point before you even saw the question."
- **Concrete de-abstraction of the trap.** On why "addressing lines directly to the audience" doesn't follow: "basing a skit on your real life struggles doesn't mean that you suddenly start speaking your lines directly to the audience... he's not saying *you*."

## Worked examples

Passage: Chicano theater. P1 Cesar Chavez's unionizing and the grape boycott, transitioning to the Chicano theater movement and Luis Valdez, peaking in the 1970s (move: explain / table-setting). P2 how the actos were created, from striking farm workers improvising their own experiences; comic and political, inspiring social action, with "palpable immediacy" (move: explain). P3 Broyles-Gonzalez criticizes crediting Valdez alone as inventor of the acto, pointing to the earlier carpa tradition; the author's corrective is that it was a collective accomplishment while Valdez's contribution remained crucial (move: evaluate).

- **Main point** — Sofia answered C off her own pre-formed statement: "Valdez was crucial, very very crucial, but in general it was more of a collective accomplishment that made a distinctive genre connecting both carpas and the European tradition." She diagnosed E's trap herself: E attributes the collective-endeavor point to Broyles-Gonzalez when it is the author's.
- **"Palpable immediacy"** — Her initial instinct was E, she switched to B on a re-read, then talked herself back to E under questioning. B's fatal clause: "addressing many of their lines directly to the audience," never stated in the passage. B's bait: "the sense of intimacy." Her own diagnosis: "I have a subconscious reason why I don't like the other one, but I can't explain why."
- **Function of the second sentence of P1** — A, with no competing attractive answer. She got it intuitively; Joshua supplied the shortcut she had skipped: the paragraph is categorized "explain," so the sentence has to serve explaining, which kills answers offering evaluation or anticipation.
- **Actos vs carpas** — D, "both often involve satire," answered without re-reading because both were noted as satire in her P1/P2/P3 notes. She correctly refused the tents/farms inference: "it says carpas were performed in tents for the working class, but we don't know if they were initially performed on farms."
- **What the author would agree with** — D, that the theater form did not require formal training, as the only textually supported option. She rejected the others as opposite or out of scope, including that Chavez was never discussed in those terms.
- **Author + Broyles-Gonzalez agreement** — C. She isolated the distinction that decided it: the author has a stance on what shaped the *acto*, not on what shaped *carpas*. "Just because he mentioned carpas doesn't mean he talked about the shaping of it."
- **Teatro Campesino support question (hardest)** — She was stuck between A and E, gave a top three of B, C, E. B killed: the passage names the San Francisco Mime Troupe as one influence on Valdez, not as the background of other members. E killed: "we don't even know if the criticism is coming from the United States." C survived with no findable flaw and was correct — international recognition of the union came before the theater's apex, and "the earliest efforts is what made me reattracted to it."
- **Final question** — B, directly supported by paragraphs 2 and 3 (acto as brief comic statement built on satire). Answered with confidence and no re-read.
