## LSAT concepts

- **Viewpoint tracking.** Add a label for each speaker to the paragraph map (V1, or initials like CM and PG), plus their single most identifying position, and mark it visually with a check or an X. Then tag every paragraph with whose view it carries.
- **Treat each viewpoint as an LR stimulus.** Once you have two speakers, dissect each the way you would an argument: conclusion, premise, intermediate conclusion, background. "Does the evidence they present lead to the conclusion?" In this passage both sides read the same evidence and draw opposite conclusions.
- **Main point of a comparison passage is one side, not a summary.** "We don't want to fall into the trap of summarizing both sides. That's not what the main point is. The main point is literally one sentence." The answer may nod to the losing side but must land on the winner. Rule of thumb for identifying the winner: whichever side the author spends more words on.
- **Every RC answer must have an underlinable line of support.** "It's not enough to say ah, I get the vibe of Garber. If you can't do that, do not choose an answer."
- **Answer strength: weaker is better in RC.** "All of the answers for RC should be very weak. The weaker the better, because it requires less of a burden of evidence for the answer choice to be correct." Look for the answer that requires the least work to prove.
- **Quantifier definitions carried over from LR.** "Most" means 51 to 100 percent, so it can technically mean all. "Many" means anything above one, so it too can mean all; its floor is what matters, which makes it very easy to prove. "Could have expected" is weaker still than "did."
- **Unqualified claims default to universal.** "If they don't qualify by saying at least, some, many, or could, the default is universally certain. The LSAT wants you to read it as a person that's an extremist." A hedge like "at least for a time" is what makes an answer safe.
- **Meaning match, not word match.** Seeing a word from the passage repeated verbatim in an answer choice is a warning sign, not a comfort. "Word match is the worst. You should be terrified of seeing a repeating word. Unlikely to be the right answer."
- **Word-in-context questions**: read the sentence before and the sentence after, then the sentence itself; the target sentence is usually not where the answer lives.
- **Descriptive versus prescriptive, again.** "Acceptable" is a value judgment; "standard" is a description of what typically recurs. Joshua grouped "ought" with "must" and "all" as maximum-strength language: "ought statements have to assume a universal goal."
- **Passages are written by reasonable, rational people,** and answers should reflect that. Ask whether a thoughtful person would actually assert this answer choice.
- **Paragraph-purpose questions are relational**, restated by Sofia and endorsed: you look at the paragraph's relationship to the whole and to the main point, never in isolation.

## Worked examples

RC passage on the 17th century Dutch tulip market and whether it was a speculative bubble. Two named viewpoints: Charles Mackay (yes, it was a bubble) and Peter Garber (no, it follows a standard pricing pattern).

- **Passage map.** P1 explain (defines speculative bubble: a large upward price move driven not by fundamentals, meaning the earnings from the asset, but by speculation about what someone else will pay, followed by a dramatic decline) plus a comparison at the end introducing both scholars. P2 explain, Mackay's view: rare bulb varieties sold high, common varieties low, then all prices collapsed. P3 explain, Garber's view: the same rise and crash, reinterpreted as the routine pattern for any new plant variety, since the original bulb sells high, becomes fashionable, is propagated cheaply, and the price falls within thirty years. Sofia mapped it correctly on her own read but worried she had over-labeled everything "explain."
- **The shared-evidence insight,** which Sofia produced herself: both scholars accept the same price rise and fall; they differ in interpretation, and only Garber brings reproduction rates into it.
- **Main point question.** Sofia first prephrased a two-sided summary and was corrected. Correct main point: it was not a speculative bubble, since Garber is the side the author gives more space. She chose D and justified it exactly: it takes the comparison into account, leans to Garber, and says the evidence does not support the bubble reading.
- **Inference question, what Garber would agree with.** She chose D, "most people who invested in Dutch tulip bulbs were generally rational in all their investments," anchored on the word "rational" from the passage. Wrong: "most" requires 51 percent or more, "all their investments" reaches investments never mentioned, and the supporting line only says the high prices for original bulbs are not irrational. Correct answer B: many owners "could have expected to at least recoup" their investment, which needs only more than one person, and only an expectation, not an outcome. "That's very easy to prove."
- **Another inference question**, answered correctly. Her reasoning: the answer said "at least for a time," which she liked for its generality and provability, and she located the supporting line about 1636 price rises attracting speculators. Joshua: "at least for a time is beautiful." He contrasted the failing choices: one asserted the rise was "not due to" a factor, which requires zero percent contribution, and another used "the only," which one counterexample destroys.
- **Paragraph-purpose question**, answered correctly by combining the map (P2 is explain) with the relational step (its role is to lay out one scholar's position so a second can disagree).
- **Phrase-meaning question: "standard pricing pattern."** She was between C ("merely acceptable") and D ("regularly recurs in certain types of cases"), and chose C. Correct answer is D. Standard means normal, typical, routine. "Acceptable" is a value judgment; as Sofia put it once corrected, "acceptable has more emotion in it," while "regularly recurs" is just identifying a pattern.
