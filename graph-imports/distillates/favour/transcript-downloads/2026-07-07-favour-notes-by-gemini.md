# Favour A, 2026-07-07

Initial consultation with a Canadian applicant targeting Ontario law schools. Joshua ran a full Main Conclusion question as a diagnostic, then moved to admissions strategy, target score, and onboarding.

## LSAT concepts

- **Main conclusion, two-part test**: the statement must be (1) an opinion of the author and (2) explicitly supported by at least one other statement. "I should be able to say why does the why should I believe this? And I should be able to find another line at least one line that explicitly tells me why the author believes it."
- **Facts cannot be conclusions**: "a conclusion can never be a fact because it's not disputable. It's just the way the world is." Example given: "If I say that Canada is the second largest country by land mass in the world, um that's not an argument. That's just a fact."
- **Presented-as-fact is enough**: real-world truth is irrelevant. "if they're not actually facts, that's not important... They treat it as a verifiable, measurable like statement and it's something that they're saying is true. Then we treat it as a fact."
- **We concede the facts**: "we're not allowed to dispute the facts, right? We accept the facts. We're conceded to the facts."
- **Speculation about motive is always opinion**: "How can I know somebody's motivations? That's just ridiculous if you think about it." Favour's own formulation, which Joshua called "phenomenal": "if they're trying to like say something about another person's like motivations without having like reasonable grounds, that's an opinion cuz like you can't really speak on someone else's motivation because you don't know." Joshua adds it is "commonly a flaw of many questions" and "it's always going to be an opinion statement no matter how they phrase it."
- **Referential phrasing**: pronouns and phrases like "this purpose," "they," "their effect" are "loaded with some sort of context that they were referring to earlier." The fix is substitution: inject the full referent back into the sentence. "that keeps us very orderly and we don't get lost if they use another referential phrase to refer to something else." Joshua calls the device "so devious that you don't catch on."
- **Recall then rephrase**: "We want to be able to... recall it and then rephrase it. If we can't do that, then we're not reading. We're just like sounding out words some level."
- **The answers live on the left**: "the answers to all of your questions um are always going to be on the left side of your screen... not on the right side" — i.e. in the stimulus, not the answer choices.
- **Hypothesis-first reading**: read as little as possible, then predict where the stimulus is going. "Normally" or "usually" signals a coming reversal: "it sounds like I'm about to say what conventionally happens, but I'm going to come back with something that this case is different."
- **A wrong prediction is a win**: "if you get it wrong that means you know what's right you can't know what's wrong you know what's right... you can update your hypothesis... not like you're not forced to lock into the hypothesis."
- **"Why should I believe this?"** is the collapse-everything question for finding support structure.
- **Testing potential is not doing the thing**: distinguishing "influencing popular opinion" from "testing their potential to influence popular opinion" is what the whole question turns on.

## Teaching moves

- **Diagnostic first, no stakes.** He opens by having her work a question cold: "I don't really care if you get the question right or wrong... pretend like I'm not here. I'm just going to be a flat on the wall." Then: "the goal here is never about getting the question right because the questions are always like you're never going to see this question again... it's more about like what is the process to getting the right answer."
- **Asks for the simplest possible restatement before anything else.** "in the simplest fashion possible... What did the stimulus say?" He uses the quality of that answer as the predictor: "whenever I ask a student they can give a thorough answer towards like what this is saying and being able to restate it in language that they understand, they almost certainly will get the answer right."
- **Line-by-line read-aloud with interruption, warned in advance.** "I'm going to interrupt quite a bit. So, sorry in advance."
- **Talk to me like a friend.** "Just talk to me as though you're like a friend of mine. We just had like a... you know you're telling me the story."
- **Removes the screen to force recall.** "I might just take it off the screen. I'll let you look at it for another second."
- **Traces her own words back to her.** When she hedges on whether a factual statement can be the conclusion: "Well, according to your what you told me, I'm only taking what you said. You told me facts can't be conclusions... So, I have to listen to what you said."
- **Chases the source of a word she introduced.** She paraphrased with "induce"; he asks "Where does that idea come from? What part of the sentence?" to force her to locate the textual basis rather than drift.
- **Picks the question specifically to break her platform's habit.** "the reason why I chose this question is because I know... ## Worked examples

**Main Conclusion question, political campaign mailers.** Stimulus structure as reconstructed in session:
1. "Normally political candidates send out campaign material in order to influence popular opinion, but..." — background/status quo. The word "normally" is flagged as a reversal signal.
2. "the recent ads for [Epson's] campaign were sent to too few households to serve this purpose effectively." — Favour initially argued this was opinion because "too few" and "effectively" are disputable. Joshua overruled: whatever the threshold number is, "we can't dispute the fact that there is a number of houses... he's saying there's a requirement of houses to be met and he didn't meet that." Therefore factual, therefore not the conclusion. "This purpose" is expanded to "the purpose of influencing popular opinion."
3. "The ads were evidently sent out to test their potential to influence popular opinion." — an inference about the candidate's motive, so speculation, so opinion. This is the main conclusion.
4. "They covered a wide variety of topics and [the] campaign has been spending heavily on follow-up to gauge their effect on recipients." — support for line 3. "Why should I believe that? Because of the last line."

Favour originally answered **B** (a restatement of line 2, the "but" sentence). After the walkthrough she identified line 3 as the conclusion and picked **C**, correct, in under ten seconds. Joshua's read: "the way they put the conclusion there was right beside a statement that looked like it could be the conclusion."
