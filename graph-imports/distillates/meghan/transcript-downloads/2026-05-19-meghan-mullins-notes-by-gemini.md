# Meghan Mullins, 2026-05-19

## LSAT concepts

- **Necessary assumption**: the unstated link that must hold for the argument to work. Joshua's hard test is "Assumption can't have been stated." A candidate that restates a premise is disqualified.
- **Sufficient assumption**: the answer that makes the argument airtight. "The argument has to be perfect. We're not allowed to have arguments that are good."
- **Conditional logic**: a bridging answer must carry both halves, premise side and conclusion side. Here, "somebody should only be able to be fired if they can be replaced." Half a bridge is not an answer.
- **Qualifying language**: "believes," "perhaps," "may" weaken a premise. "Whenever the LSAT adds a qualifying claim like believes or perhaps you really want to pay attention to that."
- **Strong language in answer choices**: "very," "good," "bad," "should," "most," "all" are strong claims to treat with suspicion on necessary assumption. The trap answer died on "very reasonable" when only "reasonable" was required.
- **Should**: "Should is a powerful statement... it's saying the tradeoff still leads to one side."
- **Main conclusion**: when an author reports a third party's decision rather than asserting one, that decision is the conclusion.
- **Reading comprehension vs logical reasoning**: RC is "a broadstroke approach," LR "requires the highest level of detail." Together they are 66% of the score, so a point of LR is worth roughly two of RC.

## Teaching moves

- **The paper drill.** Before answer choices, write three lines: evidence, conclusion, assumption. Read the stimulus first, then write, with the question out of sight. Two minutes, then read it back verbatim.
- **Attack before you defend.** He forces a counterexample before the answers, even on justify questions. When she protested that the question asks her to justify: "you can't fix something unless you know what's broken." Backed by a lawyer analogy: "If you're representing me in court... you still want to know what's wrong with my case to be able to defend it."
- **Bridge building with a docking port.** He annotates her shared screen, writing "irreplaceable" on one side and "should not be fired" on the other. "We have a docking port for the chain to be connected to from the evidence, but it has to take us home."
- **The train analogy** for answers that get the premise but not the conclusion: "You can get on the train, but you want to make sure the train takes you to the end point."
- **Line-by-line stimulus interrogation.** "What is that line telling us?" after every sentence, correcting imprecision on the spot: "Is he underpaid? We have to be precise here. What does it say?"
- **Name the diagnosis, then predict the symptom.** "You know what to look for... but it's not crystal clear." Then: "I can almost guarantee you get down to the last two answers and then you choose the wrong one. Does that happen to you?" She confirmed.
- **Anti process-of-elimination.** Allowed but treated as a training hazard: "that is the worst place you want to have with regards to your training because it gives you ability to be vague and to not have hard lines."
- **Absurd concrete analogies** to test entailment: Epstein and Weinstein for "a unique skill does not mean never fire him"; her friendship with Jade for "knowing more facts does not make your side right"; "I'm under the impression that the table is brown" to unpack "impression."
- **Volume reframe.** "Volume is good, but it's only good if it's thick. Good volume is good, not just volume as a default."
- **Reward pushback and validate the wrong answer.** "Oh, I like this. Now I know you're actually engaging with me." On the trap: "You're not a crazy person for choosing C whatsoever."

## Worked examples

- **Assumption question, Ted and Tatiana (7Sage level 4, Q17 of her last LR section).** Ted believes he is underpaid, keeps short hours, may be irreplaceable; supervisor Tatiana knows others cover for him and decides not to replace him. Her written assumption ("no other employee can do the work as well as Ted") was rejected as a restatement of the premise. Path: strip to evidence = irreplaceable, conclusion = should not be replaced; generate a counterexample (he could have faults that outweigh his value); name the unstated conditional joining them. Her chosen answer carried only the irreplaceable half.
