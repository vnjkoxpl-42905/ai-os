## LSAT concepts

- **Paradox**: "there's two irreconcilable facts... two facts that don't seem like they should be able to coexist. And your goal is to see how can I create a world that could reconcile both of those worlds to be true."
- **Paradox stimuli are never arguments**: "remember, paradoxes are never arguments. So you're not looking for a premise or conclusion."
- **Anatomy of a paradox stimulus**: background facts, then a pivot, then the surprising claim. "The surprising claim, just to give a little tip, is almost always the final idea." Pivot words to watch: "however, none, nevertheless." On seeing one: "whatever is said after that, you're essentially building a bridge with whatever is said prior to that."
- **The framing question**: "why would we have expected something different from this last surprising claim? Then you want to frame that tension between the two ideas."
- **The teeter-totter / balance sheet image**: "there's some tension there, almost like a teeter totter. You want to think about what could balance a balance sheet... what could have been added to this scenario that would have made a difference."
- **Paradox resolution is causal**: "you need something that boosts the population right around the same time... you want something to be a difference-maker. You need a causal difference-maker. So back to causation." On the mosquito item: "we need an alternate cause that would have counteracted what the birds would have done."
- **You do not need the specific mechanism, only a shape**: "whatever, however that manifests, it doesn't really matter... fill in the blank with what that does. Who knows? It could be a million different things." Balanced with: "you want to ideally have some sort of visualization of how that could look, how that would actually show up."
- **"Many" is a weak quantifier**: "many just means more than one. Don't let many bait you into thinking it's more than it is."
- **The "so what?" test on an answer choice**: "if it was true that birds were eating insects that eat mosquitoes, what would that mean to mosquito population?" Simulate the answer, then check direction.
- **Answers that restate the stimulus do nothing.** On the shrimp question, answer B described genetic variation *within* one reef population: "the differences discussed in the stimulus are between the populations of shrimp, not between individual members of one population."
- **Minimizing a difference is not explaining it.** On answer A: "minimizing genetic differences between the shrimp populations does not eliminate them. It doesn't explain why those differences exist."
- **Strength of wording — the four exceptions** (stated as a general rule and flagged as important): "paradox questions, strengthen, weaken, and sufficient assumption are the only questions on the LSAT where you want the strongest, most conceptually strong answer. The rest of them you want very weakly worded answers... every other question type, ideally weaker is better, except for these few, because you want that one that has the most impact."
- **An answer that only levels the books is not enough** when the stimulus says *substantially* more. "C is annoying because C kind of does the job for us, but it doesn't do enough. It's not that it should just level out. It should be why one has a lot more. So it needs to be much stronger."

## Teaching moves

- **Open cold with a question, no preamble.** "I'm going to just open up with a question right off the bat, and I'm going to have you just work through the question first, and then we can go backwards from there." Two to two-and-a-half minutes on a visible timer.
- **Ask what the type means to her before defining it.** "What's the first thing your mind goes to when you see paradox questions?" She said "connect the dots"; he took it and sharpened rather than replaced it.
- **Ask for the objection, not the endorsement.** When she was between two answers: "tell me the problems with both of them that could potentially rule it out."
- **Accept the silly hypothesis when it has the right shape.** She proposed the mosquitoes "like the fruit more and they tell their friends to come." Joshua: "yeah, exactly. Like that's all you're really looking to do. You need the answer to essentially do something that boosts the population of mosquitoes."
- **Concretize the scenario as a lived situation.** For the infrastructure question: "imagine you're in charge of city roads and bridges. You have two options: do a little bit of maintenance all the time, or wait for things to completely fall apart and do a massive radical rebuild." Then the question in plain terms: "why on earth do we skip the cheaper, smarter option only to end up paying for massive expensive rebuilds later? We need an answer that explains this kind of weird human behavior."
- **The mission-statement intervention** (the session's main prescription, and the reason it exists). Meghan reported that her first read is usually right and then she talks herself out of it. Joshua rejected the "just trust your gut" fix and located the real cause: "I think you'll second guess yourself after you forget what you were looking for initially. Once you see it, you're like, oh, I can see why it's not. It's never that you don't see the answer. It's only after you lose track of what you're trying to solve for." The fix: "underline the part you're solving for. Don't try to keep it in your short-term memory as you're reading questions." And: "write down, before you go into the answer choices, a bullet point for what am I trying to solve for. Even just four or five words... so whenever you need to reset, you can look at it. Having a mission before you go into the answer choices."
- **Name the dual-task load.** "Trying to remember what you're looking to solve for and also trying to understand the answer choice, doing those two things at once can be very difficult... you can get muddled with your thoughts. It's very normal, that actually happens very often."
- **Test the intervention immediately on a harder item.** He restructured the next question: read the stimulus alone, state the mission out loud before seeing any answers, then go to the answers. She got it right first try. His readout: "this is actually a harder question than the other one, which is a good sign, because when you have a very clear mission prior to going to the answers, you have clarity of thought. That's really what you're aiming for."
- **Reframe the speed tradeoff.** Her worry was the clock. "You're not saving yourself time by trying to have two tabs open versus solving for one thing. Once you solve for that it becomes a domino effect and you also get the answer right. If you spend half the time to get the answer wrong, you could have spent zero time on it and got the same value. Getting the answer wrong means it literally was a waste of your time. You don't get a half point."
- **Apologize for a rule taught late.** On the strongest-answer rule: "sorry, I should have said that earlier, but stronger answers are always better."
- **Rewrite the schedule live and have her copy it down.** "We're going to make a modification in real time."
- **Specificity requirement on error logs.** For the next day's section: "every time you choose a right answer or wrong answer, write a very concise one-line statement of what you want to do differently. That's a specific action. Not just, oh, read more closely. That's good, but what exactly would have led you to doing? Get as specific as possible. Avoid generalities."
- **Warm up before the section.** Deliberately easy items first: "they won't be difficult, they'll warm you up to starting your section."

## Worked examples

**Fruit trees, birds, and mosquitoes (paradox).** Stimulus: to reduce the mosquito population in a resort area, hundreds of trees bearing fruit attractive to birds were planted. Over the years the trees matured, attracted a variety of bird species, and greatly increased the summer bird population. As expected, the birds ate many mosquitoes. However, the planting had the very opposite of its intended effect.
- Unpacking "opposite effect": the intent was fewer mosquitoes, so the mosquito population must have *risen*.
- Tension named: birds up, birds eating mosquitoes, mosquitoes up anyway.
- Prediction generated before the answers: the trees or the birds must do something that boosts mosquitoes, and it must be timed with the planting.
- **C is correct**: the birds ate many more insects that prey on mosquitoes than they ate mosquitoes. The birds removed the mosquitoes' predators, and that effect outweighed their direct predation. "They have less predators even though the birds are still there."
- **D rejected** (below-average precipitation since planting, and drier weather keeps mosquito populations down): points the wrong way. "We need to explain an increase in mosquitoes."

**Indonesian shrimp genetics (paradox).** Stimulus: populations of a shrimp species at 11 different Indonesian coral reefs show substantial genetic differences from reef to reef. This is surprising, because the area's strong ocean currents probably carry baby shrimp between reefs, which would allow the populations to interbreed and become genetically indistinguishable.
- Expectation vs twist framed as: "we expected a melting pot, but there was no melting pot."
- **A rejected**: differences among the shrimp populations are much smaller than between shrimp and other marine species. Irrelevant comparison class; minimizing a difference does not explain it.
- **B rejected**: individual shrimp within one reef's population differ genetically despite widespread interbreeding. Wrong level of analysis; Meghan spotted this herself ("I feel like they're just saying what the passage said").
- **C is correct**: before breeding, shrimp of this species migrate back to the reef where they were hatched. The currents scatter the larvae, but breeding re-sorts them by origin, so the gene pools never mix. Meghan's own phrasing: "they all get mixed up and then they all go to their own little homes and they don't mesh."

**Continuous maintenance vs radical reconstruction (paradox).** Stimulus: transportation infrastructure can be managed either by continuous maintenance or, when maintenance fails, by radical reconstruction; continuous maintenance is less expensive, yet it is never performed.
- Mission written first, per the new protocol: "why continuous maintenance never happens even though it's less expensive."
- **E is correct**: for long periods the task of regular maintenance lacks urgency, since the consequences of neglecting it are very slow to manifest. It explains the behavior via time horizon rather than cost. Meghan's restatement: "it's just not worth it to do yearly maintenance on something that takes so long for it to crumble anyways."

**Red meat and dietary fat (paradox).** Stimulus: people who reduce their consumption of red meat do so to cut fat, and red meat accounts for roughly half the fat in a typical diet. However, on average those who have reduced red meat consumption consume substantially *more* fat than those who have not.
- Pivot word "however" flagged, and the mission stated: explain why the reduced-red-meat group ends up with more fat.
- **C rejected**: reduced-meat eaters consume as much of other high-fat foods as non-reducers do. This only equalizes the two groups; the stimulus says *substantially more*. "It shouldn't just level out. It should be why one has a lot more."
- **D is correct**: people who reduce red meat consumption tend to replace it with foods higher in fat. "If you take red meat out of someone's diet and replace it one for one with something fattier, by definition they have to have way more fat. It's not only leveling it out, it's adding more fat than would have been there already."
