# Meghan Mullins, 2026-04-14

## LSAT concepts

- **SMA framework** — Joshua's new review protocol, introduced this session and mandatory from here on. **S**tructure (name the premise and conclusion), **M**ethod of reasoning (is any logic employed: conditional, causal, or comparative, and name the two factors and how the relationship advances), **A**ssumption (the gap between evidence and conclusion). The assumption test: "think of a conceivable scenario where the conclusion could be false although the evidence is still granted as true." He added that failing one of the three is itself diagnostic: "that means we know where to attack."
- **Assumption** — "an assumption is a premise, but it's an invisible premise. Something he didn't say but he's using as evidence without saying." Corollary he made explicit: if it is stated, it cannot be an assumption by definition.
- **"Presumes without providing justification"** — decoded as simply meaning "assumes." The test is whether the author actually assumed that thing.
- **Past-to-future flaw** — inferring that what has held in the past will hold going forward. "Just because it's happened before doesn't mean it's going to happen again."
- **Irrelevant information / missing the point** — rebutting a claim with a response that never touches it. Distinguished carefully from the fallacy it superficially resembles.
- **Unproven vs proven false** — appeared as a decoy answer choice; Joshua named it and added it to her study list under the label "untrue versus proven false."
- **Group swap / equivocation between ignorance and disapproval** — "you cannot actively disapprove of something if you don't know it even exists."
- **False dilemma (method A fails therefore its opposite works)** — the flaw in the advertising question, and the target flaw in a parallel flaw question.
- **Parallel flaw** — chosen deliberately as a question type because it forces explicit articulation of the flaw rather than pattern matching.
- **Straw man** — named for the answer choices in the pesticide question that moved the target to harm to people or domestic animals.

## Teaching moves

- Blocked her from jumping to the method of reasoning before naming the structure: she opened with "I see the word cause," and he said "we're going to avoid that. We're going to first say the structure, premise, conclusion."
- Added two live-reading habits on top of SMA: after each claim, (1) **react** to anything distinct or any clue, and (2) **predict the function** that claim will serve in the argument.
- Praised adversarial reading as a habit even when the specific push was wrong. When she challenged "large enough," he corrected her (the author is only reporting what astronomers believe, which is not attackable) but said "I like that you're pushing right now. You're being pesky. That's a good habit to develop."
- Held her to complete paraphrase, then conceded when she was right that a sentence resists paraphrasing: "your goal isn't to completely just change it for the sake of changing it."
- Pushed the **uncharitable reading** doctrine harder than in prior sessions, all the way to base assumptions: "we're not here to be charitable. Even the most underlying basic assumptions, like the desire to live, are not necessarily granted. I'm okay with dying, I've lived a long life, why do we assume we have to do that." Later, on the unionization question: "you're being too nice. Don't be as friendly with these guys," and "we always want to assume the worst. We don't want to assume them being reasonable. They're not reasonable people."
- Taught unfamiliar answer-choice vocabulary by decomposition rather than definition. On "bold prescription": what does bold mean, what does a prescription do, a doctor prescribes, so it is a recommendation or solution. Then immediately: "every word counts, let's check the part that follows it," where the answer actually died.
- Killed answer choices by the **opposite test**: choice B on the meteorite question describes the author as assuming past occurrence does *not* affect probability, when he is assuming exactly the reverse. "He's doing the opposite of what the answer choice is saying."
- Rehabilitated an answer choice she had distrusted on a word ("specific prediction" for "the near future") by testing the word's real range: "if I could tell you the end of the world will happen in the next three years, is that specific? If I'm able to do that, that means I know something."
- Used homely analogies to make absurd arguments feel absurd: the pesticide argument became "your dog keeps going into my yard and digging holes. That's impossible, my dog is a purebred." The unionization argument became a surprise party where 90% of your friends do not know about it and you conclude the majority disapprove.
- Reframed difficulty as the arguments being worse than she assumes: "when you really understand what the arguments are saying, you're like, this is ridiculous. They're less reasonable. **They're dumber than you think they are.** When you realize how dumb it is, you start to understand."
- Warned against over-reading modifiers: "we don't want the adjective to sway us to make something more than it is," on "mere lack of approval."
- Asked her to produce the takeaway herself rather than supplying it. She offered "go with your instinct" and "read it literally"; he endorsed the second as the bigger one.
- Justified the parallel-flaw assignment by mechanism, not just difficulty: parallel flaw "forces you to engage with the argument structure and the flaw... people are often trying to match just as a matching game. But if you can identify the flaw in the stimulus and you're looking for the same flaw in the answer choices, it's a really good practice type of question."
- Set the standard for fallacy knowledge: "I don't want you to just have a vague remembrance of it. You need to be **razor sharp**, so even for the answer choices when they're complicated, you can see through the BS and say that's that fallacy, that's wrong, that's not what I'm looking for."
- Assigned a deliberately uncomfortable comprehension drill and said so upfront: "this will be annoying, you're going to hate it, but it's really going to be for your benefit."

## Worked examples

- **Meteorite / funding flaw question.** Premise: according to some astronomers, Earth is struck by a meteorite large enough to cause an ice age on average once every 100 million years; the last such incident was nearly 100 million years ago. Conclusion: we can expect such a strike in the near future, which clearly warrants funding to determine whether there is a means of protection. Meghan was torn among A, C, and D, and chose C. Structure work: she flagged "according to some astronomers" as a hedge, "causes" as the causal marker, and identified the past-to-future leap herself. A dies because nothing establishes "high probability" (the premise gives frequency, not likelihood). B dies as the exact opposite of what the author assumes. C is correct: moves from average frequency to a prediction about when the next event will occur. Joshua also flagged the buried assumption that a coming catastrophe warrants research at all.
- **Pesticide runoff flaw question.** Neighbor claims the author's pesticides are reaching her farm via runoff; the author says she is wrong because he only uses organic pesticides, there is no evidence they harm people or domestic animals, and he is careful not to spray on her land. Meghan diagnosed it correctly as irrelevance ("I don't care what pesticides you use, they're going into my yard") but had selected A. A is the unproven-vs-proven-false fallacy, real but not the central problem. B fails because his carefulness concerns spraying, not runoff. C correct. D and E are straw men that shift the target to harm to people or animals, which the neighbor never raised.
- **Graduate student unionization flaw question.** Premises: a small minority tried to unionize and the majority of graduate students remain unaware of the attempt; most of those who are aware believe a union would not represent their interests or would not pursue them effectively. Conclusion: they should not unionize since the majority obviously disapprove. Two stacked problems Joshua drew out: the group swap (even if every aware student objects, that could be 5% of the population and leave a 95% approval rate) and, one step further, that unawareness is not disapproval. Meghan had chosen E, having had B selected until the last second. C fails because the author does not argue it is a bad idea because people are unaware, he argues it because the majority supposedly disapprove.
- **Advertising parallel flaw question.** Premise: using rational argument in advertisements does not persuade people to buy the advertised products. Conclusion: advertisers who replace rational argument with non-rational appeals to emotion will persuade people to buy. Flaw: method A fails, therefore its opposite succeeds, ignoring that both may fail and that other options exist. Meghan articulated it herself ("but what if they both don't work"). She initially picked C (using a computer has not improved students' writing skills, therefore students should not try to improve their writing by using a computer) and correctly changed off it, reasoning that C only advises stopping the failed method and never proposes the opposite alternative, so it lacks the second term.
