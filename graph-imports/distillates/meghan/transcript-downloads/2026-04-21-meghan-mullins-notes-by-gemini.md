# Meghan Mullins, 2026-04-21

## LSAT concepts

- **Parallel reasoning** — matching structure, never content. "You're not going to find matching information, but matching reasoning."
- **Principle** — Joshua's operational definition: a principle is a **golden rule**. "When you see the word principle, think, can I come up with a rule of thumb or a golden rule?" Applied by generalizing the stimulus into a portable maxim before touching the answers.
- **Method of reasoning** — the shared spine of both question types this session: identify the rhetorical move (blame shifted from institution to individual; short-term benefit, long-term penalty; harm plus remedy; effort backfires; protective measure backfires) rather than the subject matter.
- **Reading for structure over content** — the session's central thesis. "The content is a **masquerade**. It doesn't matter what they're talking about. The content shouldn't make it harder or easier if you can see past it."
- **Cost-benefit comparison structure** — the stimulus compares a world with X implemented against the same world without X, not two different items against each other. This distinction killed an otherwise plausible answer choice.
- **Backfiring effect** — a well-intentioned measure producing an unintended consequence that outweighs its benefit. Distinguished from an ordinary expected tradeoff.

## Teaching moves

- Opened every question the same way: **make her tell the story of the stimulus before touching the answers.** "The best test of whether you actually understood the underlying reasoning is, if I took away the stimulus, tell me what it said as a story, in the simplest terms."
- Used her own paraphrase quality as a live diagnostic and told her so: "do you see how in the last question you were razor sharp with the answer?" and later, "I knew right away. I didn't even see the answers. When students are able to give me this is what it means, it's almost a certainty that they probably got it right." Paraphrase fluency as a predictor of accuracy.
- Stated the general law he is teaching toward: "whenever you get a question wrong, it means there was something fundamentally you didn't understand about the stimulus. It's almost always going to be an issue of understanding the stimulus."
- Reframed her stated weakness on science passages as a misdiagnosis. She said dense unfamiliar terminology trips her up; he located the cause in attention: "the only reason is because you're worried about the details. You're worried about the information versus, what are they trying to get across? What's the reasoning?" And the remedy: "turn it into an internal narrative... you won't even remember the content at times."
- Made the substitution test explicit: the stimulus was about a university scandal, the answer about a government scandal. "You could have been talking about a scandal in a marriage or a friendship. The point is the point behind it."
- Rewrote formal language into blunt everyday speech at every step: the media saying the university has low standards became "your school is bad"; the falsifying professor became a "rotten apple"; the president's move became "almost like a PR run"; a conflict of interest became "kind of convenient, right?" and "looks kind of sketchy."
- Checked her against a specific, easily-missed nuance: the correct parallel answer had to preserve the president's **disclaimer**, not just the good-outcome conclusion. C failed because "C is taking accountability completely," while the stimulus shifts blame to the individual. "That's not the same thing."
- Insisted on the same distinction for the disclosure question: the stimulus recommends disclosure, not prohibition. "He could have said we shouldn't allow them to invest, but he didn't. His choice was to say let them invest, but tell us."
- Taught **giving yourself examples while reading** as a comprehension aid, and modelled it with a student whose name he struggles to recall.
- Modelled thinking aloud including his own confusion, rather than presenting a clean solution: "I don't even know what that means, but they're saying this is the best way. I don't even understand how that makes sense. Yeah, but let's say that's the case." Then, one line later, "okay, now I understand. It's pretty dumb, but the idea is..."
- Held her back from premature judgment while still validating the instinct: "looks like kind of a scam. We don't know yet though. Let's not jump the gun. We don't want to be judgmental, but it looks sketchy."
- Noticed and named argument order: the screen-saver argument "is built completely upside down. They're giving us the verdict, then the evidence for that verdict."
- Distinguished a backfire from an expected tradeoff using a homely case: pizza delivery taking longer than cooking is not a backfire, "it should take more time, there's no conundrum here." The pattern he wants is "I bought a tool to save money, but the tool caused a new problem."
- Redirected her toward volume of understanding rather than volume of questions: "the focus is not to do more questions. The focus is to understand what is happening on the stimulus side."
- Closed with the skills-not-content argument: "you could be an engineer and take this test, you could be a music student. The test is not testing your ability to know these things. It's a skills test. Don't let the content intimidate you."

## Worked examples

- **University standards parallel reasoning question.** Stimulus: when a faculty member's falsification of research was uncovered, the media treated it as evidence of the university's low standards, though in truth it was the dishonesty of that one faculty member; but since vigilance about academic standards is always necessary, it is good that standards have become a topic of discussion. Meghan chose C (a government scandal attributed to lack of oversight and corruption; despite the harm, it is good the scandal occurred). Wrong: C accepts full accountability and omits the "one bad apple" disclaimer. Correct answer B: the scandal was attributed to lack of oversight although the true cause was simple corruption, and the resulting discussion is good. B preserves the structure of a mistaken attributed cause corrected by the true cause, followed by the silver-lining conclusion.
- **Credit card skipped payments parallel question.** Stimulus: some credit card companies let cardholders skip payments for up to six months, but it is almost never in the cardholder's interest because finance charges accumulate during the skipped period and cost more in the long run. Joshua's generalization: "there's a temptation up front, then a catch, therefore a long-term penalty. It's a trap." Correct answer C: unwise to use highway maintenance funds for constructing new roads, because if funds are not used for maintenance the total maintenance cost will be greater long term. Meghan got it right.
- **Scientific journals disclosure principle question.** Stimulus: in scientific journals, authors and reviewers have praised companies in which they hold substantial investments; these scientists' potential conflict of interest calls the integrity of scientific inquiry into question, so there should be full public disclosure of scientific authors' commercial holdings. Joshua's generalization: where there is a potential for cheating or collusion, the remedy is making everything public, "no backdoor sneaky stuff." Meghan was torn between C, D and E and missed it. D is the trap: it forbids investing, while the stimulus only requires disclosing. Correct answer E: financial advisors should inform clients about incentives they receive for promoting investments in particular companies.
- **Memory and recall principle question.** Stimulus: the best way to recall a word or name you are struggling with is to occupy your mind with other things, since the more you strive to remember it, the less likely it is to come to mind. Golden rule Joshua extracted: "if forcing something makes it worse, the best strategy is a mental diversion." Meghan read the stimulus without seeing the answers, paraphrased it cleanly, then picked D immediately and correctly.
- **Screen saver parallel question.** Stimulus: buying elaborate screen saver programs to prevent monitor damage can cost a company far more in employee time than it saves in electricity and monitor protection, because employees cannot resist playing with the flashing graphics. Structure: verdict first, then evidence; a protective purchase that backfires through an unforeseen human factor. Meghan was between C and D and chose D, correct: a complicated hotel security system may cost more in customer goodwill than it saves in losses from theft. C (pizza delivery taking longer than cooking) fails because it is an expected tradeoff, not a backfire. She had also considered E (an electronic keyboard may be cheaper to buy than a piano), which fails structurally: it compares two different items, while the stimulus compares one world with X against the same world without X.
