# Meghan Mullins, 2026-03-17

A level-five weaken drill review that turned into Joshua's fullest treatment of conditional logic to date: contrapositives, the knowledge-versus-action distinction, the rules/facts split, and a strategy for "weaken EXCEPT" questions.

## LSAT concepts

- **Conditional vs causation**: named as distinct families that "have a similar feel to them in some sense." Joshua's definition: "conditionals more have to do with rules, so it's understanding what do you know to guarantee to be true if you know one thing to be true." His example: "if I know you're 100% in California, then I know 100% you have to be in America. It's not like California causes you to be an American." He also flagged that either side can be the cause: "the sufficient condition can be the effect and the necessary condition can be the cause or the vice versa. There's no method to that."
- **Conditionals in weaken questions are rare**: "weakening questions are so rare to find conditionals because conditionals almost exclusively deal with causation. But when you're dealing with level five questions, you can just the freak show. Sometimes they decide what they do with them as they wish."
- **Contrapositive as the only inference**: the session's most-repeated rule. "There's only one inference, one implicit statement you can take away from any conditional, the contrapositive. Everything else you don't know." Mechanically: "you're flipping the sides of the conditionals and you're negating both sides. Two step."
- **Read for the contrapositive as you go**: "you always want to do the inverse as you read because they're almost guaranteed to play with the contrapositive. They're never going to use the conditional as it is."
- **Knowledge vs action** (the session's core insight): a conditional whose necessary condition is *knowing* something guarantees only knowledge, never the corresponding act. "There's one thing to say that they would have had knowledge about what he would have liked versus there's one thing to say he would have took a certain action. If a certain [action], there's no wiggle room for that. It's the wiggle room in the first conditional." And: "knowing something, you can know something and still not do it."
- **Could-be-true vs must-be-true**: "the thing that you take from any conditional stimulus is that you really want to know two things. Things that you know that could be true, and things that you know that must be true... that line in the sand is usually where there's typically confusion for most people, mixing up could-be-truths and must-be-truths."
- **Rules vs facts**: the organizational split for conditional stimuli. Rules are the conditionals; facts are the explicit statements. "Knowing your conditional rules on one side and knowing your facts on one side and then analyzing the interrelationship between the two." Then: "which one of these rules can get triggered by this set of facts?"
- **Expect only some triggers to fire**: "if there's multiple rules, expect one of them to get triggered. Expect one of them to get triggered, maybe not both."
- **The past does not indicate the future**: named as a standing answer-elimination rule. "We don't ever want to accept the answer that talks about the past as being an indication of the future. We can never assume [it] unless there's actually explicit reason to believe."
- **Puzzling facts signal causation**: "whenever something's puzzling, instantly expect always causation, because causation is trying to purport an explanation for something that usually is a mysterious set of facts or something out of the norm."
- **Weaken EXCEPT**: reframe rather than invert. "You really just ask yourself which one of these weakens the argument the least. Not necessarily has to strengthen the argument, it just has to weaken it the least." And the prediction about the credited answer: "almost always the answers that weaken the least are going to be answers that don't actually strengthen it. It's going to be answers that have no impact at all. Very rarely you're going to find answers that just do the opposite, because that's too obvious."
- **EXCEPT answers split 3-1 by mechanism**: "typically three of them do the job one way and the other one does the job a different way, which makes it harder to understand."
- **Negating answer choices as a tiebreaker**: "if you ever decide to, one check is to negate the answer choices and see which one has a bigger impact. If I took this away, that answer often will be the right answer."
- **Strengthen/weaken named the hardest family again**: "that's the tricky part about why strength and weaken questions I would say are the hardest LSAT question type, period. You have to essentially get into the mind of the author... not only what they're saying but all the conceivable implications of what they're saying, which is not always black and white, because you have to know where not to go too far with it."

## Teaching moves

- **The sibling analogy for knowledge vs action.** His device for the flowers question, returned to three times. "Siblings will try to mess with you a lot, right? They might know you like it. Doesn't mean they necessarily will give it to you. 'Oh, I know he likes it, so I'm going to give him the opposite.'" Compressed later to: "I can know what you want. But it doesn't mean I'm going to get it for you."
- **The cookie jar / law book analogy for rules vs facts.** "I come to you and I tell you all the things that happened to me today. They caught me with my hand in a cookie jar. They saw the crumbs on my beard. What does the law say? Then you're going to look at my facts, you're going to look at the law book, and say which one of these laws... ah, hands in the cookie jar, he's definitely going to serve some time."
- **The Mars bar in the ancient cave.** Built to make the bacteria-contamination answer intuitive. "If you found a Mars bar in an ancient cave, would it make sense if the geologist said, aha, this proves that caveman ate Mars bars? We'd be more likely to believe there's probably a hiker in there yesterday."
- **"Bookmarks of thoughts."** His name for the conditional-question skill. "This is really the trick: keeping it organized and keeping bookmarks of thoughts. This is the conditional game. If you don't keep your bookmarks of your thoughts as you go, you're cooked. It's just one wire you trip over."
- **Turn the conditional into a story.** Offered as the alternative to diagramming. "Turning it into a story usually helps a lot. When you understand what they're trying to say versus trying to just like once you get the conditional, you're like, okay, what does that actually mean in practice?"
- **Diagram only at the extremes.** "I'm not a big diagramming guy, but for extreme questions like this, it's useful to diagram." Reserved for stimuli with too many moving parts to hold.
- **Ask her to restate the stimulus in two or three sentences** when she is visibly lost. Used at the petroleum question after she said she was confused; he took her attempt and then rewrote it in plain terms: "most people believe oil comes from ancient dead plants and animals. Some scientists argue it's actually just non-living carbon. However, oil contains biomarkers that are only produced by living things."
- **Don't paraphrase for its own sake.** When she apologized for reading the stimulus back nearly verbatim: "you can. It's really sometimes harder to try to change what's been said. Sticking to what they say can be useful, because sometimes it's harder to do it the other way around because they just said it straight up."
- **Naming when the test betrays common sense.** On answer D of the flowers question: "in a regular world it completely makes sense what you chose. Somebody with common sense would say D. But they're so maniacal, they almost want you to betray your common sense."
- **Rating and disowning bad questions.** He judged the petroleum item worthless as practice: "there's not really much you can take from this question that will give you a strong sense of, oh, I get this skill. It's more like just mud. Just mud being thrown everywhere and you're rolling with the blows... I just don't like this question at all." Contrasted with the flowers question, which he rated highly despite difficulty: "the other question, I liked it, it was just really hard."
- **Normalizing failure at level five.** "There's no shame right now to make a mistake here." And: "if you get all the level five questions right or most of them, then it's like I shouldn't be here." He repeatedly called the flowers question "ruthless" and admitted he nearly misread it himself: "even as I read it the first time, I had to read it again. I caught it and I was about to say it out loud."
- **Level five practice trains everything below it.** His answer to her worry that level five was a poor use of time. "When you're practicing level five questions you're getting better at every question below it, because it's the same thing but just a harder level of the same thing." Balanced against the caveat that level five resists pattern-learning: "part of the reason why they're so hard is that they're outside of the scope of what you should expect."
- **The clearance standard.** He named an explicit, non-perfectionist bar. "Level four clearance is where I can say that you're usually good to go. Five just means you perfected it." Operationally: clearing level five is 4/5; "if you get to three out of five, move on. Don't worry."
- **Write the conclusion AND the mission plan.** The prescription he closed on, extending the previous session's one-sentence conclusion habit. "Not only are you trying to rephrase the conclusion, but rephrase the mission plan. You want to have a mission plan. If you can't articulate that, you don't want to keep [going]." For EXCEPT: "you want to find the one answer that doesn't provide a clear excuse for the theory. When you have that clear mission game plan going into these questions, except questions are actually not bad."
- **Writing now means writing less later.** "As you practice writing it down, what's going to end up happening is you're going to naturally not have to write it down as much. It's an inverse effect. Where you write more, the less you'll have to write in the future."
- **Scoped to difficulty.** "Don't be afraid to write things down for [level five] questions. Does that mean you should do that for level three questions? No."
- **Offering her the agenda before imposing it.** "What I wanted to do is conditionals, and that was what I had planned, but I always like to give you a chance to, if you have something on your heart that's like I really just want to do this, I always give that room."

## Worked examples

**The florist / Drew's flowers (weaken, level five).** Stimulus: if the flowers Drew received had been sent by someone who knows Drew well, that person would have known Drew prefers violets to roses. Yet Drew received roses. On the other hand, if the flowers had been sent by someone who does not know Drew well, that person would have sent a signed card. Yet Drew received no card. Therefore the florist must have made a mistake, either sending roses instead of violets or sending flowers intended for someone else.
- Rules/facts split: rules are the two conditionals, facts are "received roses" and "received no card."
- Contrapositive 1: if they did not know Drew prefers violets to roses, then they do not know Drew well. Meghan's first attempt reversed the terms; Joshua corrected.
- Contrapositive 2: if no signed card was sent, the sender knows Drew well. This trigger fires cleanly. "There's no way around that. The line in the sand is essentially drawn that he has to be a certain person that knows him."
- The trap: the roses fact appears to trigger conditional 1 in reverse, but conditional 1 only guarantees *knowledge*, and knowledge does not force the act of buying violets. So the two conditionals do not actually conflict, and the florist need not have erred.
- Weakening target restated: "our goal would have to be to say nope, he's not wrong, he did the right thing. So that means none of those two scenarios are relevant."
- Meghan chose **D** (the florist has a reliable record). Joshua: attractive, common-sense, and wrong on the past-does-not-predict-the-future rule.
- **B is correct** (some people send flowers for reasons other than a desire to please). It exploits exactly the knowledge/action gap: a sender who knows Drew hates roses may send roses anyway, out of trolling or indifference. "It could be just trolling them, like I told you my siblings will do. Or maybe they just didn't feel like it, or it's someone who doesn't care."
- **C rejected** (a stranger would be unlikely to send Drew flowers): "the fact that a stranger would be unlikely to send Drew flowers doesn't really move the needle at all, because there was no card" — the card fact already excludes a stranger.
- Joshua's summary of the item: "it's actually just that one swap they made there. If you did catch the first nuance, the rest would have been easy."

**Petroleum and biomarkers (weaken).** Stimulus: the dominant view is that petroleum formed from fossilized plant and animal remains deep in the crust. Some scientists hold it formed not from living material but from deep carbon deposits dating from Earth's formation. Their theory is refuted by the presence in petroleum of biomarkers, molecules indicating the past or present existence of a living organism.
- Task restated: "we need to defend the theory that petroleum came from deep carbon deposits. Given that petroleum has biomarkers in it, how can we argue that petroleum came from deep carbon deposits from when Earth formed? We need to explain how it got biomarkers in it."
- Meghan chose **B** (living organisms emerged long after Earth's formation). Joshua: this strengthens, not weakens. "B says life started late, but it doesn't say life never existed. Even if life started late, it still could have died and turned into oil later on. The argument is about what is in the oil, not when it started. B doesn't give us a different reason for why those life signs are there."
- **D is correct** (bacteria currently live in the deep crust). It supplies an alternate source for the biomarkers: modern contamination rather than ancient fossils. "It could just be non-living oil with some modern bacteria kind of just hanging out there."

**Cave paintings and diet (weaken EXCEPT).** Stimulus: puzzling cave paintings; archaeologists hold the paintings depict the painters' current diets, but this cannot be right, because the painters must have eaten sea creatures on the long journey to and from the islands, and no discovered painting unambiguously depicts such creatures.
- Joshua pre-framed the two families of defense before touching answers: "either we need to find answers that make it seem like seafood was not part of the painters' current diet, or answers that will allow us to explain why there aren't paintings of seafood even though they ate seafood and painted their current diet."
- **A defends** (the painters hunted and ate land animals on the islands): the current diet simply did not include sea animals.
- **B defends** (parts of the cave paintings did not survive the centuries): "maybe they did paint sea animals but those paintings were just destroyed. This would provide a perfect explanation for why we don't see sea animals today." Meghan objected that the destroyed paintings could equally have been the current-diet ones; Joshua redirected to the standard that we only need an *excuse*, not a proof.
- **D defends** (the painters were skilled at preserving meat): they could have brought land-animal jerky, so no sea animals were eaten. "If they were great at preserving meat, they could have brought land animal jerky from home."
- **C is correct** (the discovered paintings depict many land animals). "We already knew they painted land animals. Telling us they painted land animals doesn't explain why the sea animals they supposedly ate are missing from the walls. It doesn't fix the hole in the theory. C is essentially pointing out a boring observation that leaves the missing sea animal problem exactly where it started."
- On why this format is hard: "you're doing inverse thinking twice. You're taking the opposite side of the author, which is the side of the people, but then you're trying to find the answer that weakens that explanation the least. It's almost like triple except."
