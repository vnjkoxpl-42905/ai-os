# Meghan Mullins, 2026-04-03

## LSAT concepts

- **Flaw** — identified as Meghan's single biggest weakness from her analytics. Joshua's structure for attacking one: identify evidence, conclusion, and any intermediate conclusion, then match against the known fallacy list. "Half the game of flaw is the answer choices."
- **Causation vs correlation** — causation is words with "movement" or "force" behind them (produce, promote, lead to, make); correlation is static overlap (tend to, more likely, less likely). Joshua's key correction: "the strength of relationship has nothing to do with whether it's a cause or correlation. That's the trick 90% of students fall into."
- **Part vs whole / whole vs part** — part to whole assumes what is true of an individual member is true of the collective (one superstar player, therefore a top-tier team); whole to part is the inverse (top team in the league, therefore every player is top tier; "Starbucks is a nice organization" does not make each individual Starbucks nice). Joshua calls part to whole "arguably up there one of the most common ones."
- **Conditional logic, sufficient vs necessary** — described as the LSAT's "favorite flaw," guaranteed to appear. Sufficient is "good enough to trigger an outcome" but never the only route. Three presentations taught: the subset model (in A therefore in B; knowing B tells you nothing), the requirement checklist model (GPA + LSAT required for law school: meeting them gives you a shot, not the outcome), and the many roads model.
- **Many roads flaw** — an argument gives three guaranteed routes to D and then infers that taking none of them means D is unreachable. "All roads lead to Rome. Just because you don't take that one road doesn't mean the other roads cannot take you there."
- **Unproven vs proven false** — concluding something is false because it has not been proven true. Joshua's frame: "the truth or falsity of God being real is independent of my ability to prove it."
- **Circular reasoning** — a real fallacy but "almost never the right answer" on flaw questions; it exists mostly as a wrong-answer decoy. Recognizing it is valuable precisely so you can eliminate it.
- **Ad hominem and sampling** — flagged as two more decoy fallacies to know for elimination purposes; deferred to a later session.
- **Reconcile / explain (paradox)** — the type Meghan flagged as half her recent misses; not taught in depth this session.

## Teaching moves

- Framed the fallacy list as **"famous flaws"** or "classic fallacies": "the most common patterns of irrational thinking that humans do... it's almost a prerequisite to human nature." Sold their real-world value first, then the LSAT value.
- Motivated the homework with stakes rather than instruction: not knowing the fallacies is **"really living life on hard mode"** and Joshua warned "this is going to kill you." Later: "you're going to have to drill these till your eyes bleed."
- Positioned partial mastery as still worth it: "knowing it like 80% or even 50% will give you incredible returns."
- Taught **trigger-word pattern recognition as a life hack**: the word "studies" should reflexively cue causation, because "all science is a study of causation." He got Meghan there by Socratic ladder rather than telling her: what is a study for, name one, cancer research, what are they trying to find, "what's causing the cancer" — then "exactly, what's the word you just used?" He then claimed the payoff explicitly: "I could get 50% of these questions right by just reading that first word."
- Mirror rule for conditionals: any "if" or conditional indicator in a flaw question should pre-load sufficient vs necessary. "You'll be right like 50% of the time" off the first word alone.
- **Deliberately led her into a wrong answer** on the many-roads example ("if you get to D, what do you know has to be true?" She said you have to take one of the roads. "I don't know that. And I led you on... the reason why I asked it that way is because I want you to remember this.") Baiting the intuitive error to make the correction stick.
- Insisted on **radical simplification of arguments**: "if I told you this is a court case, what's the court case here?" and "I don't want to hear any of that. All they're really saying here..." He refuses to let her recite the passage's wording back.
- **Cross-reference every premise against the conclusion** as a reading discipline: "does this prove what I'm trying to prove here? We know it won't, but we want to see where. Because you'll find the flaw as you do that."
- Told her the answer choice must do two jobs, not one: correctly name the flaw AND accurately describe what the author actually did. "It also has to describe the action of the author appropriately. If it misses that, even if it's the right flaw, we still can't take it." Applied to eliminate an answer that said the author denied something he never denied.
- Corrected a partial-answer objection: an answer choice does not have to capture every flaw in the argument. "There could be three flaws in one argument... doesn't have to point out all of those."
- Kept her honest about answer-choice **extreme language** ("always true"), but did not let her use vibes as the reason: "there could be nothing descriptively wrong with an answer choice."
- Firmly separated **causation from conditional logic** when she tried to import the left-arrow/right-arrow diagram into a causal argument: "I know what you're trying to say and they're similar, they have a similar vibe... but causation has more fluidity. Something can be a causal factor but not a sufficient condition." Used smoking as a contributing factor rather than a guarantee.
- Reframed the discouragement of immediate answer feedback as training: reviewing in the moment "warms up the muscles," and losing confidence in practice is useful because "on test day you'll be more prepared. It's like, what do I do when I don't feel like I'm in a rhythm."
- Diagnosed the analytics honestly rather than flattering the data: a 25-question adaptive drill "biases towards questions you're not going to be good at... all the questions you get are set up to be the ones you need the most work on," so it is a poor proficiency read but a good trend read.
- Escalated difficulty on purpose and said so: "I chose a harder question here just to push you because you got the other one."
- Prescribed a specific study mechanic, not just "review": flashcards for the definitions **plus** seeing the flaws live inside questions with the fallacy list open beside her.
- Built heavy personal rapport throughout (Kanye concert, Catholic school, undergrad, his own law-firm internships), and answered her direct question about his own scores openly.

## Worked examples

- **Business ethics / risk-taking flaw question.** Premises: studies show high-risk-propensity individuals "tend to have" fewer consciously held ethical principles; people with a strong desire for social acceptance "tend to have" more. Conclusion: business schools "can promote" ethical behavior by promoting the desire for social acceptance and discouraging risk-taking. Reasoning path: "studies" cues causation, "tend to" marks both premises as correlation, "promote" marks the conclusion as causal, therefore correlation-to-causation. Meghan initially wavered between A and C, chose C, correct. Joshua used A to teach that extreme language and surface-similar wording are not sufficient grounds to eliminate.
- **Astrology as art and science flaw question.** Conclusion: astrology is both an art and a science. Evidence: the scientific components are the complicated mathematics and astronomical knowledge needed to make a chart; the art is the synthesis of many factors and symbols into a coherent statement about an individual. Meghan first guessed D, then walked to the part-to-whole answer with Joshua's prompting: having a scientific component does not make the thing a science. His analogy: "having an artistic trait doesn't make you an artist." He also worked D at length with a cooking analogy, that chemistry being required to bake a cake is not the argument that makes chemistry a science. Answer C was eliminated because the author never denied that astrology involves other things.
- **Patricia's garden flaw question.** Conditional premise: if a garden does not receive plenty of water and sunlight and is not planted in rich soil, it will not be productive. Patricia met all three. Conclusion: her garden will be productive. Meghan identified the conclusion correctly and, prompted, spotted "if then" as the conditional indicator and named sufficient vs necessary. Joshua mapped it back onto the law school checklist: meeting the requirements "just says you have a chance, it gives you the opportunity to even have a shot," not the outcome. Meghan chose D, correct.
