# Meghan Mullins, 2026-03-09

## LSAT concepts

- **Causation**: defined as explanation. "All causation, by the way, means explanation. It's the explanation for some event, how it happened." Causation tells you how; correlation tells you what.
- **Correlation**: descriptive, pattern-only. Two forms taught: temporal/sequential (one thing happens, another follows) and statistical (two things overlap frequently, or frequently fail to co-occur). "All it is, correlations are patterns."
- **Causal language detection**: the test is motion, not strength. Words implying movement, flow, or influence ("makes," "leads to," "contributes," "has an effect," "in direct response to," "in consequence") signal causation. Static descriptive verbs ("are") signal correlation.
- **Strength is not the same as causality**: an explicitly taught trap. "Strength has nothing to do with the claim, because a weak claim can be causal." Even "partially contributes" is a full causal claim.
- **The standard causal argument structure**: "the cookie cutter formula is they're going to bring a correlation in the evidence, usually through a study, and they're going to make a claim of causation in the conclusion."
- **The four ways to attack a causal conclusion**: alternative cause (C causes A or B), reversal (B causes A), both, or no relationship at all.
- **Causal chains**: when A leads to B leads to C, expect the question to test the indirect A-to-C relationship. "They're going to most likely get us to reaffirm relationship between thing A and thing C because they indirectly have a causal relationship."
- **Most strongly supported / must be true**: near-identical types. "Most strongly supported questions are almost always very close to being must be true questions, if not they are must be true questions disguised." The credited answer is the weakest, softest claim, the one requiring the least burden of evidence.
- **Strong answer-choice patterns to distrust**: comparative or relational claims ("A is better than B"), claims about someone's intentions or motivations, and quantifiers like "most." Joshua distinguished strong *language* from strong *ideas*: "the more specifics, the more dangerous things are, because it requires a stronger burden of evidence."
- **Repeating concepts as the linking point**: for must-be-true and most-strongly-supported, find the term that appears across statements. "This is like thinking of it as the railroad, and you want to see what's at the beginning of the railroad and the end of the destination."
- **Referential language**: pronouns and phrases like "this" that call back to an earlier term. "The referential language is massive."
- **Reading comprehension as inference**: "reading comp is just basically a bunch of questions that are most strongly supported or must be true questions. That's all reading comp is, just must be true questions with a passage instead of a stimulus."

## Teaching moves

- **The prehistoric forest story, for why causal inference is intuitive and wrong.** "If somebody goes into a forest and every time somebody goes in a forest they never come back out, you don't have the time to think about, but maybe it's not because of the forest. No, you'll die, don't go in there. There's no time to negotiate with that." Framed as evolutionary: "it's almost one of the most intuitive fallacies."
- **The coffee example, run three ways.** Every time you drink coffee you feel energized 30 minutes later. The trap: "people generally read this and be like, oh, so coffee must be the reason. That's not technically what that means." Then the escalation to partial contribution, which Meghan initially defended as safe. Joshua's response was emphatic: "it's a bad claim to make. Nope. Bad, bad, bad, bad. Very bad." Then the implicit form, which is the hardest to spot: a conclusion saying "if you want a better workout, you should drink coffee" is causal without ever using a causal verb, "because they're assuming that coffee is giving you energy."
- **"They're two islands."** His image for two facts with no established relationship: "we have no idea if coffee and energy has anything to do with each other. It could be completely coincidence."
- **Read literally, add nothing.** "The biggest thing is looking at what they're saying exactly as they say it, not adding your implicit judgment to it." Paired with the disposition: "we want to be pessimistic. We want to think of the worst."
- **The reading-are-intelligent example, used to force the counterintuitive read.** "People who read are 10 times more likely to be intelligent. Do we know if reading makes you more intelligent? No, we have no idea. It could be reading makes you dumber."
- **Correcting a specific misconception on the spot.** Meghan said "I thought contributes to was correlation." Joshua corrected it immediately and gave the general test rather than the item answer: "all you have to think about for causation is, does the term connecting the two ideas imply any sort of motion or any sort of flow of one thing to the other."
- **Stop at every complete thought and rephrase.** The reading protocol he ran on both stimuli: read a line, stop, "what do they tell us," push for content not labels. When Meghan gave a vague answer ("a bunch of things being overlooked"), he refused it: "more than just like something else happening, I want more specifics. What is the content matter telling us? How does that fit into the pie?"
- **Concretizing an abstract stimulus with a worked-out example.** When Meghan could not tell whether the history passage discussed two things or four, Joshua built the distinction out with real content: general trends are "maybe there's a progression towards equality," slavery to abolition to equal rights to women's suffrage. Specifics are "the specific details of the characters, who are the people, what time frames, what happened in those specific events." Then the compression: "they went from speaking in generalities or progression of history to speaking on the particulars."
- **Unknown vocabulary is fine.** On "mitters": "I have no idea. But we know whatever that thing is, that." Modeled not stopping.
- **Instant review methodology.** For LR and RC drills: answer one question, review it immediately, then move on, rather than completing a section then reviewing. Rationale: immediate feedback and correction, and "preventing them from having to recall details from earlier questions." Take as long as needed per question.
- **Rest is prescribed, not permitted.** Repeated twice. On travel days: "Friday, Saturday, Sunday, you can just ignore it completely and just take a break." On working during downtime: "don't push it too much. When you take time away it gives you that feeling of hunger to get back in. If you're always just exposed to it, it can feel draining. You don't feel that juice to jump into it when you get back. When you deprive yourself of it, it's good to deprive yourself of it."
- **Difficult reps over volume.** He revised the Friday five-section plan, judging it too grueling, in favor of a single section built entirely from levels three to five, to "provide difficult reps and prepare for high-stress situations."
- **Admitting the item is imperfect.** On the car wash question: "it's not perfect actually. It's the weakest one of all the bunch." And on the annoyance: "they point towards a direction and they want you to finish a sentence for them, which is why C makes the most sense. He's saying usually, but we don't know that technically speaking. I understand why you're supposed to, annoying question here."
- **A misheard answer, handled with humor.** Joshua heard "B" when Meghan said "D," and had begun preparing a correction. "Thank God. I was more like, okay, let me see how I can go at this with her." He took the blame: "I probably should have double checked."

## Worked examples

**Historical writing / ominous parallels (most strongly supported).** Stimulus: a new trend in the writing of history emphasizes details of historical events and motivations, replacing the previous emphasis on overarching trends and movements, with the result that the latter are often overlooked. In consequence, the ominous parallels between historical trends and current trends are also overlooked, which lessens our ability to learn from history.
- Meghan answered D cold and was correct.
- Joshua's chain analysis: "in consequence" is a causal signal. The change in emphasis causes the parallels to be overlooked, which causes reduced ability to learn from history. A to B to C, and the answer tests A to C.
- Her difficulty was in seeing that only two categories exist (general trends versus event details), not four.

**Brushless car washes / mitters (most strongly supported).** Stimulus: in modern brushless car washes, cloth strips called mitters have replaced brushes. Mitters are easier on most cars' finishes than brushes are. This is especially important with the new clear coat finishes found on many cars today, which are more easily scratched than older finishes.
- Meghan chose B. Correct answer is C.
- **B fails** because "in direct response to" is a causal and intentional claim. "The problem with B is that it's speaking to someone's intentions. We don't know why someone did something." Meghan identified "a direct response" as the key phrase and named it as causation herself.
- **A and E fail** on "more," a relative statement: many cars have clear coat finishes, but more than older finishes is unsupported.
- **D fails** on the same comparative problem, brushes more effective than mitters for cleaning. Nothing establishes either as the better cleaner; the stimulus speaks only to gentleness on finishes.
- **C is credited**, that modern brushless car washes usually do not produce visible scratches on cars with older finishes. Joshua walked her to it: mitters are gentler than brushes, newer clear coat finishes scratch more easily, so if the more scratch-prone finishes are handled well, older finishes are even less likely to scratch. He conceded it is not airtight: "this is why this is not a must be true answer. It's not guaranteed based off of what we know. But this is the one that we have the most support for."
- Linking concept across all three statements: mitters.
