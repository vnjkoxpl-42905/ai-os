# Meghan Mullins, 2026-06-03

Final session two days before her Friday LSAT. Remote-test logistics, answer-choice strength calibration, necessary and sufficient assumption technique, and a taper plan for test day.

## LSAT concepts

- **Necessary assumption**: the answer that, when negated, collapses the argument. Joshua's whole treatment runs through the Jenga image: each answer choice is a block, negating is pulling the block, and only one pull makes the tower fall. "Take away the word not. Does the argument collapse?"
- **Necessary assumption, answer-strength rule**: strong quantifiers are near-automatic eliminations. "If you see most in a necessary assumption question, it's almost a 95% chance it'll never be the correct answer." Same for "majority." "Should" is nearly as bad unless "should" already appeared in the stimulus.
- **Sufficient assumption**: look for the new concept that appears in the conclusion but never in the evidence; the correct answer must link it. Test by plugging the choice in and asking whether the conclusion is now guaranteed.
- **Claim strength / quantifier calibration**: the session's core skill. Unqualified claims default to certainty ("kids after school go home to play with their friends" means every kid on Earth). "Tend to" and "more often than not" mean over 51 percent and are therefore strong. Relative terms like "much more" are weak because "much more" could be one percent. "Some" means at least one, so treat it at the floor.
- **Question-type to answer-strength mapping**: strengthen, weaken, sufficient assumption, and paradox want the strongest answer available. Every other LR type, and reading comprehension, wants the weakest and fluffiest.
- **Conditional logic, "unless"**: technically move it to the sufficient and negate, but Joshua prefers the plain-English read: "unless introduces an exception. The first thing before the unless is always the truth except for that one situation."
- **Descriptive versus prescriptive**: a stimulus that says "if X is not preserved, Y will never happen" makes no claim about what anyone should do. A "should" answer choice is therefore out of scope.
- **Parallel reasoning**: flagged as a potential time sink, since you are reading six arguments to answer one question.
- **Reading comprehension pacing**: roughly 17 minutes per passage as the ceiling, but aim for 15 on easy passages to bank time for hard ones.

## Teaching moves

- **The Jenga analogy, taught by forcing her to visualize rather than memorize.** "I want you to make it very visual. I don't want you to remember it as a textbook definition because you're just going to confuse yourself more than you need." When she recited it back wrong ("something that when it's placed in the argument, destroys it"), he did not correct verbally; he walked her back through the physical game: "When you're playing Jenga, what are you doing?" until she produced "taking out pieces."
- **Reverse-teaching checkpoint.** Twice he stopped and said "I want you to explain that back to me. You're my teacher now." And later: "Repeat it. I want you to say back to me." Meghan repeating "Take out the word not. Does the argument collapse?" was the intended endpoint of a 40-minute stretch.
- **Diagnosing the real bottleneck.** He located her failure point precisely: "The biggest indicator for me with you is when you're able to tell what the stimulus says without looking at it, you're going to get the answer right." When she fumbles the paraphrase, he predicts the miss. His instruction follows from it: "when this is foggy, avoid the answer choices."
- **Make it common sense, not statements.** "When you just make it common sense to yourself, like in a story, versus remembering just a bunch of statements, you are usually way better with it." He then narrated the daycare stimulus back to her as a story about workers upskilling out of daycare jobs.
- **The "not" scan as a mechanical shortcut.** Do not read answer choices in order; scan for the word "not" first. Negating them costs nothing (just read past the word), they are disproportionately often correct, and if you are out of time it is the best blind guess: "necessary assumption is very friendly to the word not."
- **"Bravado versus gentle."** His own vocabulary for claim intensity, offered as a substitute for the Loophole framework he could not remember the name of. "I don't mean the strength of how good of an answer it is, I mean literally the veracity. Is it a very strong, bravado claim, or is it a very gentle claim?" His pitch: rank by bravado alone and "you'll increase your accuracy by like 20 percent" on two-answer splits, for very little work.
- **Play the floor.** "Treat it at the weakest possible. Play it like worst case scenario." Some means one; likely means it might not happen at all.
- **Naming her temperament back to her as a constraint on the plan.** "You're not a patient person. You like to make motion." He said it with a hedge ("I don't know how to say this without being rude") and then used it as a design rule, cutting an answer-ranking exercise short because "you like to have that motion, you don't want to linger too long on one question."
- **Permission to lose a question.** On a level-five parallel reasoning item: "these are not the ones you should worry about. You don't want to sacrifice the forest for the trees." He repeatedly told her the section she chose was "the hardest section in LSAT history" and that missing items there carries no diagnostic weight.
- **The Library of Alexandria as the negation payoff.** To show why negating "not yet studied" destroys the rainforest argument: "if we don't get the books in the library, we'll never get to read those books. But then what if I told you, wait, we already read those books? Then we have no problem."
- **The border metaphor for sufficient assumption.** "The conclusion is like the border. You can't bring new things over the border without declaring it."
- **Live demonstration of a heuristic on an unseen question.** For the sufficient-assumption "life hack" (the idea stated immediately before the conclusion is almost always the one the answer links), he opened a random question he had never seen, guessed from the heuristic alone, and got it right in front of her.
- **Pause the clock as the real timing fix.** "Don't let your thinking be on the clock. They can't stop you from thinking, and that's the biggest game changer for people who ever have issues with time." He framed pausing as the discipline problem, not a strategy problem: "a lot of students get caught in the moment and they don't pause their section."
- **Reframing habits versus skill.** "The hardware is there, the software is there." Her remaining gap is habit: misreading details, rushing. He named patience as "your biggest place of improvement."

## Worked examples

- **Teenagers, caffeinated carbonated beverages, broken bones** (strengthen-type, answer-strength drill). She had picked B. Joshua ranked all five by intensity: A uses "tend to," meaning over 51 percent, therefore strong and correct. B says teenagers engage in risky activities "much more often," which is relative and therefore weak. C uses "some," meaning at least one, therefore weakest. Because strengthen questions want strong answers, A wins on intensity alone.
- **Economy growth and daycare shortage** (necessary assumption, level 5). Conclusion: a stronger economy makes daycare harder to find. Evidence: better economy means more working parents (more demand) and daycare workers leaving for better-paying jobs (less supply). Meghan located the gap herself, using McDonald's workers as the example: nothing rules out new workers moving *into* daycare jobs. The credited answer contains "not" (new daycare workers will not be significantly greater than those leaving); removing "not" reproduces exactly her objection and kills the conclusion. Choice A ("most new jobs will pay well") negates to "less than 50 percent," which Joshua dismissed as arbitrary: "is 50 percent good or bad? I don't know what that even means." Choice C ("likely to decrease") negates to nothing meaningful because "likely doesn't even mean it's going to happen." Choice D was the "unless" answer.
- **Tropical rainforest medicinal plants** (necessary assumption, level 5). Conclusion: if the rainforests are not preserved, important medicines will never be developed. Joshua walked her line by line, forcing a plain-English rephrase of each sentence, then made her notice the conclusion's strength: "never be developed." Credited answer A hinges on "not yet been studied"; negated, the substances have already been studied, so the one-shot opportunity claim collapses. B was eliminated on the "most" rule alone. E ("the rainforests should be preserved") is a prescriptive answer against a purely descriptive stimulus, plus "should" is too strong: "he might just be a super villain. He might say yeah, those people need to get sick."
- **Unseen sufficient assumption question on artistic merit**, used only to demonstrate the "link the last idea before the conclusion to the new concept in the conclusion" heuristic. Joshua answered it cold and correctly.
