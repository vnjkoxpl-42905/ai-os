# Meghan Mullins, 2026-05-26

A short Reading Comprehension session (joined mid-passage) working the McKay/Garber Dutch tulip-bubble passage. Joshua lays out his full RC method: literal reading of answer choices, pausing to recalibrate before every answer set, and line-by-line rephrasing.

## LSAT concepts

- **Reasonableness test for RC answers** — "all of the answers that are the correct answers should always reflect a reasonable person's thought process." Corollary: the trap answers are the ones that pass a casual read but fail a literal one.
- **Taking answer choices literally** — "trap answers on the outside, they look very reasonable at face value, but you have to learn to take them very literally. You do not let them fill in the blank for you." The test is to imagine what the world would have to look like for the answer to be true.
- **Scope creep as the trap mechanism** — an answer that generalizes from the passage's specific case to all cases is dead. Of choice C: "they're saying in every single market ever to have existed since human time... If you go to China, you go to Malaysia, you go to Kenya, it's like what? That's insane."
- **Strength calibration / burden of evidence** — "we want the answer choice that's the softest, the weakest answer choice, because that requires less burden of evidence." Conservative hedges like "at least for a time" are a good sign: "that's pretty conservative, which is good."
- **RC answers are must-be-true answers** — "all of the answer choices for RC should be a must-be-true style answer, must strongly support it, so it should be very safe, very weak answers."
- **Inference (RC)** — deliberately deflated: "all inference is is taking what they believe, what they're fighting for, what they believe to be true. An inference is a conclusion for one side that they take off the evidence." Paired with the instruction "don't let the language intimidate you."
- **Paragraph function** — establish what the paragraph as a whole is doing, then ask how each line serves it. "just like a conclusion, each of these lines should be serving the purpose of directing that main point." Here: paragraph 2 = McKay's side, examples that it was a bubble; paragraph 3 = Garber's rebuttal, that it was not a bubble.
- **Context window for detail questions** — "read the sentence before. The answer will almost always come from the sentence before and the sentence after."
- **Vagueness disqualifies** — B on the third question loses to D because "commonly agreed upon criterion" is unanchored: "where do we see a criteria? ... It's kind of just out there."

## Teaching moves

- **Make her state what the answer would mean in the real world before judging it.** "Let's take this very literally... what would that entail? What would that mean if that was correct?" Then he supplies the concrete instance: "if you see a price of shoes at the store and that price is overpriced, they're saying that's not overpriced as long as it's not a bubble. What type of ridiculousness is that?"
- **Read the answer choice as if the passage doesn't exist.** "Let's pretend like we don't know what the stimulus even says, what the passage says. Let's understand what it actually entails for that answer choice to be correct. Read it out loud."
- **Catch the substitution error in her paraphrase.** Meghan read D as "they were justified in investing in Dutch tulips." Joshua: "Nope. Nope. That sounds like that, but that's not what it's saying. It's close." Then walks her back to the actual words — *all* of their investments — "they're talking about their RRSP, they're talking about every single investment they ever made."
- **Inverted drill.** "we won't go into the stimulus. I want you just to tell me what each answer choice — we're going to go in an inverse way... let's look at the answer choices and see which one's the strongest, which one's the weakest, and how ridiculous each one would be."
- **The pause, taught by demonstration then named.** Before answers he stops her: "remind me, before we go into the answers, we always want to have a refresh... what does McKay think?" She had been about to answer with the wrong scholar's view and caught it herself: "Oh s---. I'm thinking of the other one." He then points at the result: "See how efficient you are when you took a moment to pause to think of what is McKay's side... You did it in like two seconds." The framing: "you'll be surprised how much your brain goes into like a foggy state when you don't do that. Taking a pause before every question makes your life way easier."
- **Protect earned points.** "The hard part is remembering or knowing which side each believes. Once you know that, don't let yourself lose points for stupid mistakes, mixing up which side is which."
- **Name the cost of skipping prediction.** "Do not look at the answers before you have a premeditated thought of what I'm looking for. That is what's going to kill your energy and time because you're going to end up reading the passages three times." He adds a confidence argument: "your confidence will diminish because you're like, I don't know, okay, I'm just going to choose this one. And that influences your confidence as you go."
- **Say it out loud, not in your head.** "you think you get it until you're forced to say it out loud. You're like, uh, something to do with that — and you'll find yourself in circles."
- **Acknowledge the cost up front.** "RC at first is the most exhausting... it just requires for you to put the work up front of forcing your brain — it's going to exhaust you to go line by line by line and then rephrasing each line as you read it. And then what ends up happening is once you do that, the answers become very easy."

## Worked examples

- **Tulip passage, trap-answer autopsy** — C said (in effect) if there is no bubble then prices are appropriate, stated over all markets. Killed for scope: it makes a claim about every market in history, not the Dutch tulip market. D said most 17th-century Dutch tulip investors were "generally rational in all of their investments." Killed because "all of their investments" is an absurd claim about their entire financial lives, not about tulips.
- **Question 4 (McKay's view)** — Answer B, "the prices of certain varieties of Dutch tulip bulbs during the 17th century were at least for a time determined by speculation," wins on conservatism. C rejected as too narrow (implies tulips were grown only in the Netherlands, unsupported).
- **Paragraph 2 function question** — Meghan flagged this type as confusing. The right answer explains the basis on which one scholar (McKay) makes an inference that another (Garber) disagrees with. She talked herself out of "undermining" and "outline the errors" once she reconnected to her own earlier read that paragraph 2 exists to show McKay's side.
- **Paragraph 3 / standard pricing pattern** — Chain rebuilt from the surrounding sentences: the price increase did happen; it was fundamentals, not a speculative bubble; Garber argues a standard pricing pattern occurs for new flower varieties (few exist, so they're expensive, then prices fall as they propagate). Prediction: an answer saying this pricing is normal. Meghan picked D. B rejected for the unanchored "commonly agreed upon criterion."
