# Meghan Mullins, 2026-02-25

## LSAT concepts

- **Sufficient assumption**: taught from the plain meaning of the word. "Sufficient assumption, we're saying that assumption is good enough for the conclusion to be true." Joshua's shorthand: "it's a slam dunk, a guarantee." It may overshoot; more than enough is fine.
- **Necessary assumption**: the counterweight, and Joshua flagged the counterintuitive part directly, since Meghan said "sufficient is like more necessary than necessary is." Necessary is the bare minimum required "to even have a shot at" the conclusion being true. He warned about its texture: "that's why necessary assumption is tricky, they're almost too straightforward, they're too obvious at times."
- **Sufficient assumption vs sufficient condition**: a distinction Joshua called out as an industry naming failure. "There is a difference between sufficient assumption and sufficient condition, those are not the same thing. Students will confuse that, which is honestly a failure of LSAT prep because they should have used different language." Conditions are rules; assumptions patch a gap in an argument. They overlap in practice, since you often use a rule to patch the gap, but they are different objects.
- **Conditional logic**: sufficient condition means if one condition holds, the necessary condition must follow. Meghan's own pocket example, New York City implies New York, which Joshua endorsed and extended (NYC implies America).
- **Main conclusion**: identifying it precisely, using indicator words ("so" is a conclusion indicator, "it follows that"), and the subject/predicate breakdown. Joshua's rename: "naming part and telling part."
- **Intermediate conclusion**: Meghan asked whether "overlooking a basic truth" was one. Joshua's test: ask what the support is. If you cannot name an explicit premise supporting it, it is not the conclusion in question.
- **Inference from quantifiers**: all/some translation. "Whenever you take language that's like every, if, or every, turn it to all, and think of some as just at least one."
- **Strengthen vs prove**: distinguished at the end. Which answer is most strongly supported runs the burden of proof in the opposite direction from which answer proves the conclusion.

## Teaching moves

- **Build from the everyday meaning of the word, not the jargon.** His opening rationale: "the things that not the more overcomplicated routes when you don't need to, because when you try to make it intuitive it usually sticks better." He asked "what does sufficient mean if I just said that outside of context," and Meghan supplied "like good enough," which became the definition.
- **The cash register analogy (the session's anchor).** A customer owes 20 dollars, pays 18.50, and is short 1.50. The 1.50 gap is the assumption, "the negative space between the difference between the amount that needs to be paid and what he's actually paying you." A sufficient assumption is any amount that guarantees payment: 10 dollars works, "you also have some extra change to grab a snack." A necessary assumption is the bare minimum, 50 cents, which sounds absurd: "if I have to pay you a dollar fifty, I have to obviously pay you 75 cents, it goes without saying." He then closed the trap: "if you say a dollar fifty-one, it's not necessary anymore."
- **The prosecutor frame.** The most portable move of the session. "The most useful mindset I find for most students is that you want to think of yourself as a prosecutor. You want to think of who is being charged and what am I trying to charge them with, and then whatever they give you, you want to fill in the gap." Formalized as a two-slot checklist: "you need a rule of law and you need a fact. In every sufficient assumption question, one of those two things will be missing."
- **Read to attack.** "We're reading to attack. It's weird, it's almost paradoxical, when you're reading, you're reading to attack the argument. You're taking the opposing counsel." Paired with the standard: "the evidence should be a 100% one for one guarantee, there should be zero possible way that you could disprove their argument."
- **The new-concept cheat code.** Taught as a mechanical eliminator: any concept appearing in the conclusion but absent from the evidence must appear in the correct answer. "That tells us right off the bat that answer must have the idea of murder in it, no matter what. Any answer that does not have that idea in it, we can just not even read it." He quantified the payoff on the spot: down to two choices, "if you wanted to close your eyes and do eeny meeny miny mo, you have 50% chance."
- **The inverse cheat code, repeated concepts.** "If you see it mentioned more than twice, it shall suffice is how you want to think about it. It's not likely to be the answer." The answer connects the two loose ends mentioned once or twice.
- **The shell game metaphor for assumption questions.** "There's a dangling swap. They're trying to do like the magic games where they put cups and they put some sort of coin in between and trying to swap them without you seeing it. That's exactly what's going on. Spirit and perfect."
- **Constitutes vs justifies, taught by role play, not assertion.** Meghan stayed stuck on why answer A was wrong across several minutes. Joshua tried three angles: a stealing example ("is taking something without asking stealing" versus whether it was justified), then explicit role play, "let's say I told you that you're a thief," then the summary image: "A is putting the cart before the horse." And: "if it's not murder, the conversation is irrelevant whether it's okay or not." He let her keep pushing back rather than closing it early, and conceded when she caught him misstating A's direction: "you're correct, it's actually the opposite of what I'm saying."
- **Handling unknown vocabulary.** He asked for her backup plan rather than supplying the definition. Meghan offered substituting a placeholder word; Joshua added reading the surrounding context for valence: "you can usually get the clues based off the words before and after." She then correctly read "fanatics" as negative without knowing the word.
- **Making her do the content before the structure.** When Meghan jumped to labeling ("probably the conclusion is"), he redirected: "you're kind of getting to the jumping to the point where you're just saying something versus something. What is the actual content matter if we're turning conversational? What are they saying?"
- **Crediting her formulation over his own.** When Meghan articulated the missing rule in her own words: "your answer is actually better than what I'm saying right now, because you're just saying what they're thinking."
- **Highlighting the conclusion as a cognitive-load fix.** "You don't want to keep more tabs in your brain open than necessary." He moved her from occasional to consistent highlighting: "it's those small things that make a big difference."
- **Main conclusion practice as a force multiplier.** When Meghan complained that main-point questions never appear in her random sets, he reframed the point: "main conclusion practice is actually not for the question type, it's for all of the other ones. Same with role questions, they're like a super booster of all question types. It's going to be like a bomb that hits 10 different question types."
- **Category and trait as an organization discipline** for quantifier-heavy questions: "you want to think category, trait, category, trait. Keeping those two categories sharp in your head makes it very clear."
- **Prove-forward, not answer-backward.** "If you try to go to the answers to try to connect them, people can get away with it sometimes. But what exactly am I proving and what exactly do I need to link, that's the easier way to keep it sharp."

## Worked examples

**Regime of Q (sufficient assumption).** Stimulus: anyone who thinks the terrors of the ancient regime of Q were exclusively the work of fanatics overlooks a basic truth, the regime was made up primarily of ordinary people enthusiastically seeking paradise. The regime executed many people in pursuit of this goal, but it later became clear that paradise as they defined it is unrealizable. So at least some of the ordinary people of Q were in fact murderers.
- Question stem read as sufficient assumption. Joshua's stem rule: "if they say the word if and valid, we always want to identify it with sufficient assumption."
- Conclusion is the last sentence, flagged by "so."
- New concept in the conclusion: murderer. Absent from the evidence, so it must appear in the answer. Two choices survive.
- Where the evidence stops: execution in pursuit of a goal later found unattainable. Where the conclusion starts: murder. "Unattainable leads to murder," so the bridge must connect those.
- The evidence gives facts but no rule. What is missing is a standard. Joshua's framing: "murder might be different in Canada versus what it's like in New York versus what it's in Brazil, there might be different categories for what we consider to be murder."
- **A is wrong** because it is a moral claim about whether murder is justified, not a definitional claim about what murder is. "A is assuming it is murder and then saying, yeah, but it's okay."
- **C is correct**: execution in pursuit of what is later found unattainable constitutes murder. "C is descriptive. A is more of like morals."

**Spirit / material bodies (sufficient assumption).** Premises: all material bodies are divisible into parts, and everything divisible is imperfect. Therefore all material bodies are imperfect. Conclusion involves the spirit not being a material body.
- Meghan chained the premises herself into "all divisible things are imperfect," which Joshua flagged as an inference and praised.
- She identified the new-concept rule unprompted: "I feel like the answer should have spirit in it," because the first two sentences say nothing about spirit. Joshua: "that was literally my next question."
- Subject of the conclusion is the spirit; predicate is not being a material body, treated as the criminal charge.
- Meghan derived the needed link herself: "if it's not a material body, then it has to be perfect, because material bodies, all of them are imperfect." The answer connects spirit to perfect.
- "Material bodies" appears three times, so by the repeated-concept rule it is not the answer's payload.

**Students, part-time jobs, walking to school (sufficient assumption, quantifier).** Premise: every student who walks to school goes home for lunch. Conclusion: some students who have part-time jobs do not walk to school.
- Meghan produced the correct missing piece almost immediately, "some students who have part-time jobs don't go home for lunch," then talked herself out of it and had to be walked back.
- Joshua drilled the translation habit: every becomes all, some becomes at least one.
- **Answer C fails on group mismatch**: it describes students who do *not* have part-time jobs. "The entire goal is to prove something about kids who do have jobs. Knowing what the jobless kids are doing for lunch gives us no proof."
- Meghan asked a good counterfactual: if the stem instead asked which answer is most strongly supported, would E be correct? Joshua confirmed. "The burden of evidence is coming from the answer choice side, but we're trying to do the opposite job. E would be literally perfect for that question type."
