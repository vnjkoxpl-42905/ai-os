# Meghan Mullins, 2026-04-28

Three flaw questions worked in depth (director intent, contemporary art, ancient apples), producing the scope rule for flaw answers, the never-concede golden rule, and a diagnosis that Meghan's conclusions carry too much noise to test answers against.

## LSAT concepts

- **Flaw answers must attack the author, not the actors in the story.** The session's opening rule, arrived at through a near-miss. "That answer choice is attacking who? Who are we supposed to be addressing?... You're right about Jenkins, but we're not here to argue with Jenkins. We're here to argue with the author." Joshua's compression: "you have to look at the reaction. You're reacting to a reaction. You're not reacting to the actual situation."
- **Effect does not establish intent**: "the result of something happening, the effect, doesn't mean you meant to do it... the outcome doesn't justify that you meant to do it. It just tells you that it happened."
- **Existence is not access**: the flaw in the artworks argument. "The existence of food existing doesn't mean that he has access to it... existence of something and access are not the same thing." The author "is assuming that everyone has a magical unlimited access to all of the art in the world."
- **Presentism**: named as the flaw in the apple argument. The author assumes a 5,000-year-old cultivated apple would look like a modern supermarket apple. "That's probably presentism at its finest right there... taking a species to be huge takes hundreds or thousands of years of selective breeding." Compressed: "the author forgets how time works. He's assuming that time doesn't account for the differences in size."
- **"Fails to consider" means the author completely forgot it**: "fails to consider just means the author completely forgot this idea." It supplies an excuse the author never entertained, not a claim the author denied.
- **The golden rule: never choose an answer that concedes.** "Never ever choose an answer choice that concedes... you can never choose an answer that says you're wrong, or that admits the other side's right, because then whatever they say after that doesn't matter. It's already lost." Scope: "this goes to every flaw question, every question that's a strengthener or weakener. Never choose an answer that concedes defeat even if it gives a qualification at the end. Never."
- **Answer choices are hypotheticals to be simulated**: "you don't know any of these answer choices are true. All strengthen and weaken questions are saying, if we accepted this new piece of information, what would that mean to the argument?"
- **Attack the exact claim, not the adjacent one.** On the artworks item: "we don't want to extrapolate from that any further, or create alibis. We want to make it just strictly, specifically what he claimed. We don't want to attack similar-sounding claims." The generalized warning: "the thing about flaw questions is that they allude to things that seemingly have a relation to what's being said. They're related in ideas. It's probably something maybe the author would think, maybe, but it's not specifically what the author said."
- **Negate the opposing side to find the conclusion.** For an argument that says a group "is mistaken": "just negate the other side. Whatever they believe, don't think about it too generally. What is the other side specifically claiming, and just say that's not true."
- **Vagueness in your own conclusion is what gets you caught**: "the vagueness is where they catch you... when you hear 'are mistaken,' you're thinking, oh yeah, the other team is wrong. But when you keep it that vague, it can be confusing as to the answer choices." The remedy: "in one sentence, what exactly is the author proving? And then test that sentence against the answer choices, not the general abstract idea."
- **Commas mark a pause to process** (carried forward from 2026-03-24 and applied again mid-sentence): "we see a comma. Pause. Pause. So what do they tell us?"
- **Contemporary means modern.** Flagged because she had not decoded it.

## Teaching moves

- **Credit the student's reading even when it costs him the clean answer.** Meghan had read D as describing the *director* acting against his own interest. Joshua worked out what she meant mid-sentence and conceded: "it does actually, you're actually correct. It does kind of weirdly describe a potential. I didn't even catch that angle and now I'm following you." Then: "D is actually more sneaky than I took it. I almost didn't even realize what that would even have intended to be. The LSAT intended you to see that. I didn't even catch that." He rejected the answer on scope, not on her reasoning.
- **Reframe overthinking as an asset needing steering.** She called her own habit a defect ("I make alibis for every single answer"). His response: "that's actually a good thing... we need to be able to use that thinking, that overthinking, to almost maneuver to always stay focused."
- **The lawyer-and-client frame for scope.** "We are a lawyer. You're taking on the case of your client. You're not taking the client's friend's case, or the other side's potential case. You're here to take the author's, which is your client's case, and address it as such."
- **The dog-in-the-kitchen analogy for intent.** "You come home and you see your dog just made a huge mess in your kitchen. The result is a huge mess. But do you think that your dog was thinking, I want to make a huge mess in the kitchen?"
- **The murder-defense analogy for the never-concede rule.** "Let's say you're my lawyer and I'm facing murder charges. What are you trying to prove? If you went to the judge and told them 'even if he did it,' I would look at you like, no, that's not my lawyer. I don't want my lawyer to say, yeah, maybe he did kill that person in blind road rage, but he's a decent guy." Then straight to the rule: "the moment that you said that he did it, you already lost the game."
- **The food-in-Africa analogy for existence vs access.** "If somebody is hungry and you told them, hey, there's so much food out there that exists, why eat at this shelter? Would that be a reasonable thing to say? There's a lot of food in California. What does that mean for the kid? There's a lot of food means literally nothing. It does not help." Extended to the artwork: "what if I want to go down a street and just check out a local artist?"
- **The listen-to-all-the-music inversion.** Built to expose the argument's absurdity: "if I told you, Megan, listen to all the good music ever, you'd say I couldn't, it wouldn't be possible. So then I said, okay, we're not making any more music now. You'd be like, what? How does that work?"
- **Hide the stimulus and make her recall it.** For the artworks question he had her read one clause, then navigate away to a different question number before restating it. "I want you to really force yourself not to look at the question as you do it." Same technique as taking the stimulus away on 2026-03-24, now formalized.
- **Reject her own vocabulary when it is borrowed, not owned.** She said "contemporary artists think that they can make people feel aesthetically fulfilled." His reply: "I don't like that language. I want more natural language. I don't hear about contemporary, I don't hear about aesthetically." He made her rebuild it as "some artists think that their art can make people feel happier." And the standing rule when a phrase is opaque: "if you don't know what it means, just avoid using it."
- **Ask her to predict the author's move before reading it.** "What do you think the author is going to say about that?" She said "no," which was right, before ever seeing the conclusion.
- **Prescribe articulating why wrong answers are wrong.** His stated fix for overthinking: "it's being able to tell me why the answers that are wrong, why they're wrong. That's going to really help you. If you can't even do it perfectly, just attempting to do it, and trying to make it as tangible as possible, will improve that ability not to overthink, because you'll notice patterns of ways you make mistakes."
- **Predict more aggressively.** "We need to get you to a habit of predicting better, or predicting more aggressively. If you have a prediction, you have a mission going into the answer choices... when you have a sharp prediction — this is the problem with the argument — that makes your life way easier." His read on where she loses it: "it's not like you didn't understand the argument. You explained the actual issue pretty clearly. But when you went into the answer choices, you're losing what you had initially. It's somehow like the answer choices are keeping you captive."
- **Normalize the LSAT's strangeness rather than defending it.** "A lot of these questions don't make sense. They're so out of order in a very subtle way. If a normal person came to you and made this argument, you would look at them like, I don't know what planet you're from." And: "it's hard to explain a lot of the LSAT questions to people who ask me what makes them challenging. It's just they're awkward."
- **Admit when an explanation is failing and restart it.** On answer B of the apple question, after several failed attempts: "I'm trying to think of a way to make this like, I don't think I'm explaining it well." He then restarted twice, including with a two-apples picture, before it landed.
- **Stop when the burnout is real.** She said she was losing it; he agreed rather than pushing. "Even as I was explaining it, I was realizing, okay, we're spinning circles. Whoa, my brain's hurting now. And it's about apples. Like no one cares about apples."
- **Refuse extra work when it risks burnout.** She offered to do both the section and the drill the next day. "I'm worried about that. I don't think that's a good idea. You don't want to do too much, it hits a certain diminishing value. You don't want to get burnt out and just do it for the sake of doing it."

## Worked examples

**Jenkins and the movie (flaw).** The author infers that the director Jenkins intended an antisocial outcome from the fact that viewers of his film behaved antisocially.
- Flaw: inferring intent from effect.
- **D rejected** (people can act contrary to their expressed interest): the reading Meghan defended is that Jenkins, who has an interest in preventing antisocial behavior, acted against it. Joshua conceded the reading is available and still rejected the answer on scope: it critiques Jenkins, not the author's inference about Jenkins.
- **E is correct**: the author concludes from the mere fact that an action had a certain effect that the effect was intended by the person who performed the action.

**Contemporary artists and existing art (flaw).** Premises: there are already more great artworks in the world than any human could appreciate in a lifetime, capable of satisfying virtually any taste. Conclusion: contemporary artists, all of whom believe their works enable people to feel more aesthetically fulfilled than they otherwise could, are mistaken.
- Conclusion sharpened by negating the artists' claim: the artists claim their art adds value; the author claims it does not.
- Flaw: the argument treats the world's stock of great art as if everyone had unlimited access to it. Abundance somewhere is not availability to anyone.
- **D is correct**, the accessibility answer.
- **E rejected** (the volume of existing art affects the fulfillment derived from new art): a misread of the author's position. "The author isn't saying that looking at a new painting feels less good because old art exists. He's just saying it's redundant, because you could have looked at the old one instead." Joshua rated E genuinely close: "even as I see it, I'm like, it kind of looks like it fits a little bit."

**Wild vs cultivated apples (flaw).** Stimulus: wild apples are considerably smaller than cultivated supermarket apples. In one region, archaeologists searching for remains of cultivated apples from 5,000 years ago, around when people first began cultivating fruit, found only apple remains the size of wild apples native to the region. Conclusion: apples were probably not cultivated in that region 5,000 years ago.
- The author's assumption: cultivated apples 5,000 years ago would already look like modern ones. Presentism.
- **A rejected** (fails to consider that even if a plant was not cultivated in a given region at a given time, it may have been cultivated nearby): concedes the conclusion in its own wording. The never-concede rule.
- **B is correct**: fails to consider that plants cultivated for only a short time may resemble their wild counterparts much more closely than plants cultivated for a long time. Joshua's plain version: "if they had just started cultivating apples 5,000 years ago, the first few generations would probably still resemble the wild ones, because the first seeds are from wild ones. So the fact that these ancient apples were wild-sized doesn't necessarily mean the apples were wild. They could have been in the early stages of cultivation."
- **C rejected**: the author is not assuming apples must be one size or the other. Meghan got this one herself: "it wouldn't be C because he's not taking that for granted. He's just talking about two different kinds of apples."
