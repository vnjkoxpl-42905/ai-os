# Meghan Mullins, 2026-05-12

A mixed platform-debug and teaching session: the first half is Meghan walking Joshua through everything broken in her custom bootcamp on lsatprep.study, the second half is two worked Flaw questions that establish Joshua's core framing for flaw work — fallacy recognition plus sufficient/necessary discipline.

## LSAT concepts

- **Flaw question approach** — Joshua splits flaw answer choices roughly 50/50: "half of the flaw questions are going to be just addressing a particular fallacy that we should know," the other half is "more of a open-ended fluid style of answer choice which will be like just your ability to understand why the argument is wrong. It's not into a fixed format template like the flaws are."
- **Anticipating the gap before the answers** — "the answers should be solved for, especially flaw questions, should be solved up front." If no named fallacy is visible: "given the evidence is true, how can we argue that the conclusion is not true?" And if you can't produce that: "we should not go into the answer choices just looking for the like you know hoping for the best."
- **Sufficient vs necessary (the number one trap)** — "The number one fallacy on the LSAT is going to be the conditional sufficient versus necessary." The specific error: negating the sufficient and concluding the necessary fails. "The sufficient isn't the only way. The if is not the only way. It's just one way. One guaranteed way. Doesn't mean there's only one guaranteed way to get there."
- **Contrapositive** — "flipping the gate." From `poodle -> dog` the only valid inference is `not dog -> not poodle`. Meghan initially answered that not-a-poodle means not-a-dog; Joshua corrected it with the golden retriever.
- **Conditional as a trigger** — "Whenever we see a conditional in any sort of stimulus, for flaw questions even more than any other question, automatically your mind should be anticipating for it to be inappropriately applied... they should only catch you off guard if they don't go that direction because it's their number one trick."
- **Ad hominem** — "a point is not right or wrong based off who says it... anybody who is like an evil or a not appropriate messenger is never going to be the reason why a conclusion is wrong." He explicitly connects it to why it feels natural: "a lot of the fallacies, they very intuitively make kind of sense to our day-to-day life, but... that has nothing to do with logic."
- **Sample size / unrepresentative sample** — "we don't know what they are — how many students, which students, are they representative? We have no idea. The sample is not good."
- **"Should" as moral language** — "should is a mighty word. That's not a small word. Should carries a lot of baggage with it." It introduces good/bad, "a completely different conversation than what is and what isn't." An answer choice that imports "should" when the author only described what *is* is wrong on that basis alone. "Should never ever get baited into the word should."
- **Distractor diction** — "recent vintage" in the Druid stones stimulus is deliberate: "vintage usually means old, right? So they put recent vintage to throw you off. That was literally on purpose... Nobody should ever write that in a stimulus or in a conversation."

## Teaching moves

- **Make him articulate the argument before touching answers.** Joshua's standard opening on every question: "the stimulus gave us what? What did the stimulus tell us?" then "why should we throw the proposal away?" He restates her version back in blunt colloquial English — "the kids who support it, they're lazy, they complain a lot and they fail their classes and they don't even care about school, right?" — to strip the formal wrapper off the argument.
- **Push past a correct-but-vague answer.** Meghan said "find the flaw between the premise and conclusion." Joshua: "that's for sure. I mean like more like what does that mean specifically?" and then "what do we anticipate though?... how should we expect it not to add up?" He refuses to accept a definition that doesn't cash out into a procedure.
- **Flaw hunting as sport.** "there's always going to be multiple reasons why an argument is bad. It's actually good practice to see how many flaws you can pick out, almost like a sport."
- **Force the final answer before explaining.** "I give you one chance to give me your final answer here." He also asks for confidence level, then uses the contrast: "the last question you kind of perfectly articulated the argument and your answer was very high with a high level degree of confidence. Now let's do the same thing here."
- **Ask why the wrong answer was tempting, not just why the right one is right.** "give me an answer explanation for why you thought B was wrong."
- **Concrete analogy over abstraction.** The poodle/dog conditional, and the Trump/Fox News three-opinion model built out at length to make "range of opinions narrower than the consumers'" intuitive before returning to the logic.
- **De-abstract dense answer choices by chunking.** "you want to debstractify it... you want to take that two chunks, break it into two different chunks." His diagnosis of the failure mode: "when you look into an answer choice and just try to read it three times over and trying to understand it as the entire answer choice, you'll fall into a mistake of trying to absorb too much information."
- **Diagnose intuition-reliance as the ceiling.** "you can get away with not catching the fallacy on these levels of questions. But what's going to end up happening is when you do 10 of them... you might be able to get away with 40% of the times, but then the 60% of the times we miss it is frustrating. So having a razor sharp understanding of that is really going to help us."
- **Drill design principle.** He rejects open-ended practice for fallacy naming: "asking you to do it just broadly I don't think is as useful... versus when you have to actually identify, get clear certain levels with just specifically that one skill, which is I know what that fallacy is by name — I mean do it with time pressure." Stated goal: "We should be able to identify the fallacies with ferociousness."
- **Own the platform failure directly.** No deflection: "I'm sorry for that... it's slightly premature when I sent it to you."

## Worked examples

- **Druid stones (flaw)** — Meghan had answered E and was torn between D and E. On re-read she self-corrected: the stimulus makes a claim about Druid stones *in general* and then "jumps to this particular stone." The block was purely lexical: "very very old" vs "of more recent vintage." Joshua's read: recent vintage just means more recent than very old, and the phrasing is an engineered distractor.
- **Student proposal (flaw)** — Argument: scrap the proposal to let students choose their own classes, because the students supporting it are lazy, complain, fail classes, and don't care about school. Meghan caught two flaws unprompted: unrepresentative sample ("the students who are supporting that could be two students out of the whole school") and ad hominem, which she named herself. Joshua: "Look at you. You slammed off. That's two flaws you caught."
- **Media power (flaw)** — Stimulus: critics say media power to impose opinion is too great; that would be true if the range of opinions the media purveys were narrower than the consumers'; that assumption is untrue; therefore the critics' claim is false. Meghan answered C but was hesitant between B and C. B fails twice: it imports "should" (moral language the author never used) and it shifts to "important issues of the day." C is right because it names the sufficient/necessary error — "it falsely believes that because the first claim is not true that means the second claim is not true." Joshua's counterexample for how media could still have too much power: show three perspectives that are all unrepresentative of the actual audience.
