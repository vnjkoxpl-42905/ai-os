# Meghan Mullins, 2026-03-22

A single-question session: one sufficient assumption problem worked all the way down to the mechanistic conditional-chain method, plus Joshua's rule that a new idea in the conclusion must appear on the necessary side of the credited answer.

## LSAT concepts

- **Sufficient assumption**: identified by the question stem. "Follows logically is a fancy way of saying which one would be guaranteed to be true. Logically means with certainty." Its job: "we want something to say, if we accepted one of these five states of affairs to be true, this would guarantee the outcome they're trying to prove."
- **Question-stem triggers**: "if you see the word *if* in the question stem, or if true, or would justify if true, that means they mean it in a literal sense, would be guaranteed" — sufficient. "If you see *requires*, that's necessary. If you see *needs*, that's necessary."
- **Sufficient assumption is a conditional discipline**: "a lot of sufficient assumption is like 90% conditional reasoning, understanding of conditional reasoning."
- **Repeating and overlapping concepts**: the entry move. "When we're looking for sufficient assumption questions, we're looking for repeating concepts, overlapping concepts. You want to identify them... anything that's mentioned more than once, you want to pay attention to those." The concept that appears only once is the one needing a bridge.
- **The conditional chain as a train**: "all we ever have to ask ourselves is, if we jump on the train at any point, whatever follows it has to be true."
- **The evidence must sum to the conclusion**: "if we were to use math as a reference, whatever is in the evidence has to equal the conclusion. The evidence together has to equal the conclusion. We want the sum to be the conclusion."
- **You never grant the conclusion**: the point Meghan fought hardest. "We're not granting them the conclusion. We're only granting them the evidence... the conclusion is the case they're trying to prove." Complicated here because the conclusion sits in the second half of the first sentence: "why it's tricky is that the conclusion is a second half of the first sentence. It's not the whole thing. We don't accept the whole thing to be true."
- **`Thus` as a bridge marker**: "whenever we see thus, think of it as a bridge. We know whatever follows it is a conclusion, and the statement prior to it is evidence."
- **There is more than one valid sufficient assumption**: any link that completes a path from the conclusion's subject to the conclusion's predicate works. "It doesn't matter which one of these four is true. We just need the outcome to be loves music."
- **The new-idea rule** (the session's payoff): if the conclusion introduces a term that never appears in the evidence, that term must be the **necessary** condition in the credited answer. "If the conclusion includes a new idea of anger the majority and you have to prove that, the answer has to be on the right side. It has to be the necessary condition in the answer." Consequence: every answer that puts the new idea on the left is dead on sight. "If I just saw 'people who became angry,' whatever they say after it doesn't matter. I can just dismiss it. I don't even have to read the answer."
- **Conditionals cannot be reversed.** When Meghan asked whether the two sides could swap: "No. That's the really tricky part about this. It can't be swapped. Has to be in this order."
- **Unstated premises**: the answer choices in this family are premises the argument left out. "These premises are unstated premises."
- **Why this format is resurging** (Joshua's read on the test's history): "the LSAT used to minimize the amount of questions. For the last four or five years they stopped providing sufficient assumption questions with hard conditionals, because once you understand how to do this mechanistically, it's a cheat code, you can solve for every question, and they wanted to avoid questions that people can just do that. But once they took away logic games, now they're trying to introduce this style of question more often, because logic games are what we just showed you right now. It's kind of just the same thing. So you're seeing it more frequently now."
- **Vocabulary triage**: unknown words do not have to be decoded. "Whenever you see a word that doesn't really make sense or is not common, you can just put that word 'thing' and look at the words around it, or look at that individual word as a sentence, and ask yourself what word can I see in the root of it." Then: "so long as you know that whatever that thing is has to be true, that can be enough."

## Teaching moves

- **Run the intuitive and the mechanistic approach in parallel.** He announced the plan explicitly: "I'm going to do the intuitive approach and both of them at the same time so you can see them and you can find the approach that works better for you."
- **Diagram with condensed words, never single letters.** The most transferable rule of the session, and he demonstrated the failure first on purpose. "Part of it was to show you this is not how you want to. What does GC mean when you read this at the end? You're like, uh, let me look at the argument again." His instruction: write a short version of the actual idea ("creative genius," "seek controversy," "widely held beliefs"). "Seeing this will be visually more meaningful than writing a GC... otherwise it just becomes a bunch of algebra soup. It doesn't really make sense to us." He called it "a game changer for most people."
- **Be consistent with your own notation**, whatever it is. "There's no one way you have to, but we have to stick to being consistent with whatever way we want to represent it." He also enforced negation notation (a slash) rather than dropping the "dissatisfied."
- **The prosecutor framing for sufficient assumption.** "We want to look at it as though we were prosecutors: what's the one piece of evidence that, if I were to admit it at trial, would guarantee that person would be convicted?"
- **GC and AM as long lost lovers.** His image for the bridging task. "Think of GC and AM as long lost lovers. They want to see each other again. He wants to go see the love of his life. So we need to find a way to get GC to AM. We need a bridge. We need a train that will connect."
- **Train stations and destinations.** The dominant metaphor. "The conclusion is the destination. I'm starting off in the conclusion here and I want to get to the end point. I need a transportation to get this idea to this idea." And: "does the evidence by itself allow me to get there without anything else?"
- **Build a toy argument out of the student.** When the real stimulus stopped working, he abandoned it mid-question and constructed a throwaway one using her: premises Megan → tennis player, tennis player → athletic, dancer → loves music; conclusion Megan → loves music. He then had her generate valid sufficient assumptions herself (Megan → dancer; tennis player → dancer), and added a premise to show the earlier answers still hold. "Whatever train it takes us there, we're okay with." He returned to the real question only after she said "I see what you mean."
- **Recover unknown vocabulary from the root.** On "habitual assent," which she did not know: "always remember, I'm looking for any part of the word I do recognize. It's almost like treating the word as somewhat of a sentence." He walked her from "habit" to "constant, regular, repetitive," then used the modifier to constrain the noun.
- **Accept her analogy as the definition.** When she offered smokers as an example of the majority being angered, he took it: "that's a very good analogy there." He supplied his own alongside: "think of musicians, people considered creative geniuses. They don't subscribe to normal beliefs, and people criticize them for it."
- **Mock the test's writing when it obscures.** On "genuine creative genius": "all they're saying is creative genius. They just wrote it like a six-year-old. It's extra fluff."
- **Pre-empt the crash.** He predicted her homework experience in advance and told her not to read it as failure. "You're going to get home and you'll understand what your goal is to do. You're like, I didn't get any of them right. But expect that. I'm telling you right now. Don't feel like, oh, what's happening?" And: "this is the prep part for conditionals where students usually tap out. They're always like, my brain hurts doing this."
- **Name the payoff to justify the pain.** "Once you understand this, sufficient assumption should be your guaranteed question in the bank. These are my freebies... they become a joke. There's a moment where you'll have a like, oh, I see what's happening. Simple. Boom."
- **Distinguish practice scaffolding from test technique.** Repeated three times. "You're not going to do this on test day. It's just forcing you to explicitly see it. But when you're practicing it, it'll naturally visually be more clear."
- **Timed solo attempt first.** He opened by putting a ~90-second timer on screen and letting her work the question alone before any discussion, then asked her to narrate her process rather than her answer.
- **Rest as instruction.** "Take the rest of the day off because that was really exhausting mentally. Every time students go through that process, they're like, I don't know about all this stuff."

## Worked examples

**Creative genius and the majority (sufficient assumption).** Stimulus: the genuine creative genius is someone who is dissatisfied with merely habitual assent to widely held beliefs. Thus, these rare innovators tend to anger the majority. Those who are dissatisfied with merely habitual assent to widely held beliefs tend to seek out controversy, and controversy seekers enjoy demonstrating the falsehood of popular viewpoints.
- Conclusion isolated: "these rare innovators tend to anger the majority," signalled by *thus*, and sitting inside the first sentence rather than at the end.
- Chain built from the evidence: creative genius → dissatisfied with habitual assent → seeks controversy → enjoys demonstrating falsehood of popular viewpoints.
- The gap: "anger the majority" appears nowhere in the evidence. Meghan stated it herself: "none of those equal anger."
- Required shape of the answer: one of the three chain terms on the left, "anger the majority" on the right. "We want one of these to say: if one of these is true, then anger the majority is true."
- **B is correct**: people who enjoy demonstrating the falsehood of popular viewpoints anger the majority. It hooks the last link of the chain to the conclusion's new term.
- The 30-second route: every other answer put "people who became angry" in the sufficient position, so all four could be killed without reading past the first clause.

**The Megan toy argument (constructed teaching aid).** Premises: Megan → tennis player; tennis player → athletic; dancer → loves music. Conclusion: Megan loves music.
- Valid sufficient assumptions she generated or confirmed: Megan → dancer (jumps straight to the loves-music chain), and tennis player → dancer (routes through the existing first premise).
- Rejected: "dancers are singers" and bare "dancers love music," because neither connects Megan to the chain. "If I just said dancers love music, that wouldn't tell me enough. I need to get from here to here."
- Adding a fourth premise did not invalidate the earlier answers: "it doesn't change what we said before."
