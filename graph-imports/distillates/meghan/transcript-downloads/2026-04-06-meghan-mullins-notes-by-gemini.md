# Meghan Mullins, 2026-04-06

## LSAT concepts

- **Flaw** — Joshua's taxonomy of flaw answer choices: three categories. (1) Purely descriptive answers you must map play by play onto the argument. (2) "Fails to consider" / "author overlooks" answers. (3) Answers that are not classic fallacies at all, just a specific description of what this author did. "Not every mistake that you make in your reasoning is a type of fallacy."
- **Causation vs correlation** — reinforced from the prior session. "Whenever we see the word study, right away call it out." Studies produce data, data shows correlation, the argument then asserts cause. "Correlation never implies causation." Also taught the reverse-causation variant: X and Y correlate, the author says X causes Y without considering that Y may cause X.
- **Sampling bias** — a politician generalizing from five items he personally bought. "You can't use your own personal experience. You're talking about the nation."
- **Unproven vs proven false** — sharpened against sampling. The distinguishing test: it is an *internal critique* of an opponent's evidence with no counter-evidence of your own. "Once you give your own evidence, you're not going towards unproven versus proven false."
- **Ad hominem** — attacking the person instead of the idea. "It's almost always going to be exclusively used in one context. It's going to be talking about hypocrisy... look at his track record. His track record is separate from his ideas."
- **Appeal to emotion** — flagged as a decoy: "this will almost never be the right answer." Joshua also corrected a category error: "appeal" is not itself a fallacy, it is a family; ad hominem is an appeal fallacy.
- **Conditional logic, sufficient vs necessary** — chained requirements: democracy requires free choices, free choices require well-reasoned opinions, well-reasoned opinions require reading skills. The conclusion flips the chain.
- **"Unless"** — taught as an exception introducer, not a diagram. "Whatever is said prior to the unless is what's always going to be the case, and there's only one exception, which is whatever follows the unless."
- **Double negation in a conclusion** — "not unfounded" means justified. Joshua isolated it as the entire difficulty of the hardest question of the session.

## Teaching moves

- Named the answer-choice vocabulary problem as a **language-learning problem**, not a logic problem: "fails to distinguish, fails to establish, takes for granted, it presumes... they have a very specific meaning but they're just things we kind of have a vague feeling for. It's a language. Once you learn the language, it's very doable." Reassured her that "a lot of students have that exact issue."
- Gave a concrete operational rule for "fails to consider" / "author overlooks": **simulate it as true**. "Let's take it and pretend like it's true. What happens to our argument?"
- Rebuilt the "studies means causation" trigger from memory rather than restating it, walking her back through the cancer-research example she had generated herself two sessions earlier until she produced the word "cause."
- Had her **read the stimulus aloud and call out clues in real time** as she went, rather than reading silently and then discussing.
- Made her **work all five answer choices independently before discussing any**, then went back through them.
- Turned wrong answers into a fallacy-identification drill: for the hardest question he went A through E asking "what fallacy is that?" and had her name sampling, ad hominem, causal (effect mistaken for cause). Building elimination fluency, not just answer-getting.
- Told her explicitly when an answer choice has **no fallacy at all** and that leaving it unlabeled on homework is correct, resolving a specific confusion she raised.
- Anti-diagramming stance on conditionals: "remember we want to make it conversational. We don't want to turn it into if this that. You can do that to get through it, but it's useful to just think about what are they telling us as a common sense way to think about it. Makes it way easier to engage."
- Taught **term repetition as the conditional-chain detector**: "we always want to recognize when they repeat a term that was said earlier." Then walked the chain backward by question: what does democracy need, how do we get free choices, how do we get well-reasoned opinions.
- Used a **Donald Trump analogy** to unlock the Clem Key argument: "he's saying the elections are rigged... any claim against me is wrong. Why? Because they have a political agenda." Then asked what the author thinks about that.
- Taught the **stalemate principle** as the heart of unproven vs proven false: "We just know his criticism is not valid. It doesn't go as far as to say that what they claimed was actually true. It's a stalemate. It's one of those things where people are better off just saying I don't know."
- Debunked plausible-sounding wrong answers by literal restatement: "if I said to you, people who have differences in opinions are not aware of the differences in opinions, doesn't really have a connection. A lot of the answer choices in flaw is just realizing how good they are at hiding ridiculous nonsense with attractive wording."
- Escalated deliberately again and announced it: "I'm going to up the ante here. I'm going to go to a really hard question. No pressure."
- Ran the session "rapid fire" for volume, explicitly trading depth for reps given the test date.
- **Pushed back on the test date itself** without steering: "Why are you taking this test? Period... I'm not saying it to push you one way or the other, I'm just going to push back like this." Then gave a real market read: June is standard for a September application cycle, April is awkward, "you might be my only April student."
- Pre-loaded the emotional frame for a likely mediocre score: "don't be discouraged... you just haven't given yourself a fair complete shot. This would be more just to get your feet wet. That's what I would look at it as for you." Also flagged the inverse: a good score means she could have gone higher.

## Worked examples

- **PT 146, Section 1, Question 9 (prairie plants).** Field study: the more plant species a plot had, the more vigorously plants grew and the better the soil retained nutrients. Conclusion: more plant species "improves" a prairie's ability to support plant life. Meghan flagged the "more of this, more of something else" pattern, then with prompting recalled "studies means causation." Correct answer A: infers from correlated X and Y that X causes Y without considering whether Y causes X, i.e. a prairie's capacity to support plant life may be what produces more species. Meghan got it right and translated the abstract wording into the stimulus's own terms unprompted.
- **Politician on consumer prices.** Economists claim price increases averaged under 3%; the politician says they are mistaken and cites gasoline up 10%, auto insurance 12%, newspapers 15%, propane 13%, bread 50%. Meghan diagnosed the flaw correctly on her own ("his own shopping around, sample's too small") but had originally selected C, unproven vs proven false. Correct answer D, sampling. Joshua used the remaining choices as a labeling drill: A is ad hominem, B is not a classic fallacy at all, C is unproven vs proven false, and an emotional-appeal option that is near-never correct.
- **Democracy / literacy conditional chain.** Democracy depends on free choices; choices cannot be free unless made on the basis of well-reasoned opinions; in the information age reading skills are essential to forming well-reasoned opinions. Conclusion: a highly literate society will be a democratic one. Meghan named sufficient vs necessary correctly and chose A. Joshua taught "unless" as an exception introducer here, and had her check E, which states that a condition under which something occurs is a condition under which its prerequisites occur. His ruling: "that's true. That's not a flaw. They're playing with conditionals, but they're talking a lot and not saying much."
- **Smith's proposed ordinance.** Conclusion: Smith's proposed ordinance does not merit consideration, because four years ago Smith ran a store that used bait-and-switch tactics. Meghan chose A, ad hominem, correctly, after debating B. Joshua confirmed B fails because the speaker never claims the ordinance itself is false.
- **Clem Key complaints (hardest question of the session).** Clem Key argues the complaints against his company are unfounded because the complainants disagree with his widely known political views and are therefore biased. The author concludes the complaints are "not unfounded," citing that political bias would not prevent a company from treating someone badly. Meghan initially misread the double negative, then was torn between A and E. Joshua walked her to B, unproven vs proven false: showing Clem Key's dismissal is invalid does not establish that the complaints are true. Labeling drill on the rest: A mixes up an effect (being biased) with a cause, C is ad hominem, D is sampling, E is not a fallacy and dissolves on literal restatement.
