# Meghan Mullins, 2026-03-24

One sufficient assumption question that exposed a foundational gap: Meghan had conflated sufficient/necessary *conditions* with sufficient/necessary *assumptions*, which forced Joshua to stop mid-question, rebuild conditionals from scratch, and rewrite the study schedule on the spot into a conditional-reasoning boot camp.

## LSAT concepts

- **Question type vs condition type** (the session's central confusion, and its resolution): "Keep sufficient condition and sufficient assumption completely separate in your head. Pretend like they're not even related." Sufficient/necessary *assumption* are question types, describing the task. Sufficient/necessary *condition* are parts of a conditional statement, describing a rule. "Sufficient condition is not a question. It's a rule... conditionals can be in all types of questions." Joshua acknowledged the naming collision is the problem: "I get the confusion is the wording. I'm saying sufficient assumption, sufficient condition back and forth."
- **Guaranteer vs requirement**: his renaming of the pair, offered specifically to break the collision. "Guaranteer versus requirement. The guaranteer is sufficient." He also noted "versus" does not mean opposed: "I don't mean like they're against each other. This is just two components."
- **Every conditional contains both**: "anytime you see a necessary condition, there by definition has to be a sufficient condition. They're always together... you need to have both of them to be a conditional statement."
- **Order is not fixed**: "it's not required of you to have the sufficient come first and necessary after. That's not the case."
- **All conditional reasoning reduces to one skill**: "all conditionals are is keeping in mind which one's the guaranteer, which is the requirement, and not getting tricked up. That's the whole of the conditional game... and they're going to try to trick you and say oh that's the guaranteer part even though it's the requirement part, or vice versa. That's all conditionals are. It's literally what they are."
- **Trigger and outcome**: his simplified vocabulary once "state of affairs" proved to be noise. "What's the guarantee? What's the requirement? What's the trigger? What's the outcome? That's the simplest way, versus trying to overcomplicate, because conditionals take time to learn in a very detailed manner."
- **The asymmetry of a met requirement**: "because you met the requirement doesn't mean it happened. You're eligible. It makes it so it's eligible." Stated the other way: "a requirement being met doesn't guarantee the outcome is going to happen, but a requirement not happening is guaranteed that it's going to fail." And: "if the outcome does not happen, the trigger cannot have been pulled. Impossible. But don't mix that up with saying if the outcome has happened, the trigger was pulled. There's other ways. Somebody else could have pulled the trigger."
- **Indicator words fix the sides**: "if always introduces the sufficient condition. Whatever is followed by the then will be the necessary condition." Non-if phrasings translate: "it doesn't have to say if-then, it could say requires. Whatever follows requires is the then part, and the if is the part before." *Precondition* is also a necessary-condition marker.
- **Question-stem identification**: *if assumed*, *if true*, *enables the conclusion to be properly drawn* signal sufficient assumption. Necessary assumption uses "heavier words" — *required*, *must the argument*. Joshua on the stakes: "students often mix up sufficient with necessary assumptions and it's devastating. It's a completely opposite job almost... you don't want to spend any mental energy thinking oh which one is it. You want to be able to identify it instantly."
- **Do not drop the qualifier**: the first premise is not "if there's a recession" but "if a country wants to emerge *rapidly* from one." "You do not want to leave that qualification, because if we're just saying if recession, that's not the specific claim they're making."
- **Commas split thoughts**: his rule for finding a conclusion buried mid-sentence. "Commas either do one of two things. Either they're lists or they're indicating a new thought is going to come." And: "people think a sentence is one complete thought. No, it's at *least* one complete thought... whenever I see a comma in a sentence, treat those two almost as two sentences."
- **`Since` introduces evidence** for whatever follows it.
- **The new-concept rule** (restated and hardened from 2026-03-22): "if I see a new concept in the conclusion, by definition it has to be in your answer for sufficient assumption." Operationally: "your goal for every sufficient assumption question is to read the conclusion and say, is there any concept mentioned here that's not mentioned earlier in the evidence? If it is, it must be in the answer." Payoff: "this question is a 30 second question. I don't have to read this answer."
- **Evidence and conclusion are separate entities**: "when I ask you anything about the conclusion, like is there any support for it, you're only allowed to appeal to these two pieces of information. You can't appeal to the conclusion to support the conclusion."

## Teaching moves

- **Stop the question when the foundation is missing.** The pivotal move of the session. Roughly fifteen minutes in he abandoned the stimulus, shared his screen, and rebuilt conditionals from the rain-and-wet example, because her questions revealed the gap was not about this item. He named the failure as his own: "this is good because I took it for granted... I realized we were speaking past each other at some point. I was like, oh, I just assumed that to be true."
- **Rename a term when the standard term is doing damage.** Twice. "Guaranteer versus requirement" replaced sufficient/necessary. And he dropped "state of affairs" entirely once she said it confused her: "I'm going to stop using the word states of affairs because I feel like it sounds confusing at this point. We're just going to say, if one thing happens, then what must also have happened?" She proposed "set of circumstances"; he accepted it.
- **Naturalize the if-then into first person.** "Put yourself in the driver's seat of the if person. If I do this, if I wanted this, then I would have this." Applied: "if I want to get out of a recession, I need money from outside countries."
- **Read the stimulus as a story, not as facts.** His diagnosis of why she gets flustered. "If I tell you to remember three random facts that are disjointed, it's tricky. But if I tell you a set of facts that are coherent, it makes it easier to remember and understand the moving pieces." He then had her build the story cumulatively, one sentence at a time, until she produced: "to get out of a recession, you need new investments. And to get those new investments, you need people to be confident in the policies."
- **Dumb it down deliberately.** "To fix a broken economy, you need investors to drop in cash. Simple. You want to turn it as dumb as possible. You don't want to make it complicated, because then it's a story in your head versus three different facts."
- **Take the stimulus away.** "This is why it's good to look away, because now you're like, oh, it was there in front of me the whole time. Taking away the stimulus forces you to see what do I actually understand, because when you're looking at it while you're reading it, you can see it" — that is, recognition masquerades as comprehension.
- **Prove by first disproving.** "It's a weird thing to do, but you want to disprove something before you can prove it. If I can find the holes in the argument, then I can address it." The lawyer framing: "for me to take you as a client, I want to see where your points of weakness are. I'll attack your case, say you look bad here, here, here, but let's figure out ways to address those problems."
- **The prosecutor standard, restated.** "Treat it as though we are prosecutors and the conclusion is the case we're trying to prove. We're trying to prove our verdict is guilty without a shadow of a doubt."
- **Don't over-explore the content.** On collectivism, where she wanted to argue the political philosophy: "we don't have to get too deep into this because it's not too important versus just understanding it... you don't want to go too deep, you don't want to get lost in that." Enough is "the masses versus the individual," with a concrete image: "let's say it would be more productive for one year to have everybody working on the oil rigs. You're willing to sacrifice individual needs for the needs of the collective."
- **Name her cultural default rather than correcting her.** When she objected that a country's goals and its people's goals need not conflict: "that's a great question... that's the westerner in you. In the western world we have more of an individual mentality."
- **Push for precision on pronouns.** She said countries that "favor themselves instead of the people"; he stopped and asked three times: "Who's themselves? Who's their own?" until she said "the country."
- **Have her repeat the rule back.** After landing the new-concept rule: "repeat that back to me. What are we looking for in every sufficient assumption question?" She reproduced it correctly.
- **Explain the long way first, then give the shortcut.** "The reason why I went through that thorough process is so that now, when I tell you this, you'll understand what I mean when you practice."
- **Rewrite the plan out loud when the diagnosis changes.** He did the schedule revision in the session rather than after it, thinking aloud and saying so: "I'm thinking on the spot right now. I need to think about it more... I didn't see that coming." He also refused to improvise the fix: "I don't want to spit something out and then regret it later."
- **Triage framing for the whole engagement.** "You're not in a place where you have the time for a fixed schedule. It's almost like we're trying to do quick operation now. If you were living in a normal world you'd say, oh yeah, we'll do a full blood test. This is more like, no, we're going to hit your knee and hopefully it works."

## Worked examples

**Recession, investment, and collective goals (sufficient assumption).** Stimulus: a country's rapid emergence from an economic recession requires substantial new investment in that country's economy. Since people's confidence in the economic policies of their country is a precondition for any new investment, countries that put collective goals before individual goals cannot emerge quickly from an economic recession.
- Premise 1 diagrammed: rapid emergence from recession → substantial new investment. *Requires* marks the necessary side. Contrapositive: no new investment → no rapid emergence.
- Premise 2: new investment → confidence in economic policies. *Precondition* marks the necessary side. Explicitly guarded against the reversal: "because there's confidence doesn't mean there's going to be new investment. We don't want to mix up the conditionals."
- Conclusion located after the comma, with *since* introducing the evidence: countries that put collective goals before individual goals cannot emerge quickly from a recession.
- The gap, stated at its starkest: "if I ask you what do you know about the collective group according to the evidence? Nothing. That's the problem." And: "it's not about not knowing their goals. I don't know about any countries that put collective goals. I don't even know what the collective goals are. I just don't know anything about collective goal countries, period."
- Answer-selection by the new-concept rule: only B and D mention collective goals, so A, C, and E die unread. **D is correct.**
- Joshua's timing claim for the item once the rule is internalized: 20 to 30 seconds.
