# Meghan Mullins, 2026-03-12

## LSAT concepts

- **Strengthen and weaken**: named the hardest question family. "I would say the hardest question types are strengthening questions, period." Reason given: you must hold the argument structure and handle whichever style of reasoning the stimulus uses.
- **SMACKR**: Joshua's five-step reset process, introduced this session. **S**tructure (categorize premises and conclusion), **M**ethod of reasoning (how they got from premise to conclusion), **A**ssumption (the unreasonable jump), **K**eywords (qualifiers in answer choices), **R**anking answer choices. Run in acronym order. "Every single question only boils down to those five features. If you can master those features and make them your strength, you'll never get a question wrong."
- **Method of reasoning as a predictive tool**: identifying it tells you what to expect in the answers. "If it's causation, I should expect either a third cause or reverse causation or maybe showing a way that they don't actually have any relationship at all." If conditional, expect sufficient/necessary confusion.
- **Comparative reasoning**: named as "a third family of reasoning" alongside conditional and causal. "All it's saying is they're taking one thing and comparing it to the other thing, and they're assuming that in a world where the option they perceive to be better is implemented, that world would be a better outcome."
- **Comparative arguments are always incomplete comparisons**: "when arguments are comparative in nature, we're usually being shown an incomplete comparison. Always." The attack is to find other similarities or differences between the two options.
- **The three-tier ranking system for strengthen/weaken**:
  - Tier one: attacks or bolsters the specific gap between evidence and conclusion, the assumption itself. Preferred.
  - Tier two: makes the conclusion less likely to be true, without engaging the evidence-to-conclusion link.
  - Tier three: attacks the premise or evidence. "One you never want to choose unless you have no options. 90% of times it will not come to that level."
- **The symmetry breaker rule**: for comparative weaken/strengthen, the credited answer cannot apply to both compared options. "If the answer choice can apply to both parties, then we have a problem. It's almost like it's neutralized."
- **Relative answers as a trap**: "most," "many," "all of the top doctors." "We want to avoid relative answers because it's hard to concretely say if they would benefit or if they're actually true."
- **Appeal to authority / popularity**: "the LSAT isn't too keen on supporting a plan just because other people are doing it."
- **Causation and correlation**: absence of correlation used to conclude absence of causation is as bad as the reverse. "Saying something does not have a causal impact, it's equally as bad as saying something does cause."
- **Reasoning types can coexist**: Meghan asked whether comparison and correlation are the same thing. "There could be more than one form of reasoning applied to an argument. It could be conditional and a causal claim, or it could be causal and comparative. They're not exclusive to one."
- **Conditional claims**: rule-based. If one thing happens, another must be true. Contrapositive named: if you are from California then you are in the US, and if you are not in the US you cannot be in California. Flagged as a topic to cover soon.
- **Control group**: clarified after Meghan's misreading. "Control group is just referring to the specific group that is receiving the placebo. It's not necessarily that control group means it's been regulated. It's just the group we're isolating for."
- **Weaken standard**: casting doubt is sufficient. "We're not here to say that the argument is impossible to be false. We're just trying to cast a doubt."

## Teaching moves

- **The mantra.** Joshua's verbal technique for weaken questions, delivered as a two-part sentence spoken out loud: "given that [evidence], how can we still argue that [opposite]?" Applied live: "given that using IPV would probably result in half as many vaccination-caused polio cases as OPV, how can we still argue we should not make IPV the more common vaccine?" His pitch: "it seems ridiculous, but say it as a mantra. You'll be surprised, hearing it, how much easier it'll come to think about what the problems are."
- **Pick a side, like a court case.** His fix for Meghan's reported problem of accidentally clicking the strengthener. "You want to keep in mind, what side is their pro and what side's your con. Almost like a court case. What side are you fighting for? They're fighting for IPV. We're fighting to show that IPV is not the move." And the compression: "I just need to find a way to show that IPV is not as good as they say it is."
- **Method of reasoning as transportation.** "Think of it as the method of transportation to get from one point to another. You can get to Canada by flying, you can get to Canada by taking a bus, driving, you could theoretically run. There's different ways."
- **"Throw some mud on the one side without it splattering on the other side."** His image for the symmetry breaker rule, delivered right after Meghan noticed that "slight risk" and "a few" felt like the same register of vagueness. He agreed the language felt equivalent, then relocated the real distinction: E says IPV carries a slight risk "like most vaccines," which could equally describe OPV, so it neutralizes.
- **Answer choices as simulation.** "We're taking the answer choices and we're saying, hypothetically, if this was actually true, what would it do to the argument? We're simulating it essentially."
- **The Pfizer/Kirkland analogy.** Built to make answer A concrete: one vaccine has known adverse events but prevents new strains; a replacement removes those complications but does not prevent new strains. "The first one prevents naturally occurring, the second one doesn't. So the second one could be conceivably not a good idea to switch to."
- **Doing the arithmetic of the weakening out loud.** "IPV was supposed to take us from 12 cases to 6. If IPV also causes at least three new cases of polio, we're definitely weakening. It's still mathematically possible that we come out one or two cases ahead, but that's already weakened the force of the evidence. We're preventing one to three cases by switching, not six."
- **Categorize the nature of statements, not just their role.** His instruction before the first reading: identify the types of statements as you translate. "If it's an opinion, it sounds like the author's conclusion. Dumbing down not so much the content, but also, what is he doing here?" Then immediately dialed it back so as not to overcomplicate: "you don't want to overcomplicate it. Hopefully I'm not making it sound way more complicated."
- **Attending to qualifiers.** He stopped her twice on "naturally occurring": "just make sure we understand the qualifier, naturally. They're not saying there's no new cases period."
- **The one-sentence conclusion habit** (the session's main prescription). "Whenever you're going to make a mistake or you find yourself in a challenging place, take the time, write out the conclusion that you're fighting for. What side are you fighting, in one sentence." His diagnosis of why: "it's so common, students will go into the answer choices and they have a vague recollection. They have a vibe, a feel for what the conclusion is saying, but it's not concrete, so they get shaken up when the questions get really hard." Predicted payoff: after five or ten forced reps, it becomes automatic.
- **Error post-mortem via SMA.** On every wrong answer, fill out Structure, Method, Assumption in one or two lines each. "You'll see exactly which one of the steps you had a problem with."
- **Naming the LSAT's uncharitability.** On the sugar substitute answer: "that's why the LSAT is sometimes very brutal. Even the basics of what you think a normal person would do, they don't grant that. You have to assume that they're crazy."
- **Validating the difficulty rather than minimizing it.** He checked the item's level mid-review and disagreed with it: "this is a really tricky question. Is this a level four question? That's very bizarre. It's definitely level five." Then: "this is ruthless." And on her near-miss: "you got to the last question. You're so damn close. It's frustrating because you're like, I'm literally one question away. But it's good. You're getting better as you go."
- **Crediting her framing.** When Meghan said "we want to make OPV look good or IPV look bad," he stopped and handed it back: "yes, exactly correct. You said it perfectly actually."
- **Rest, enforced with humor.** On weekend study: "don't do it. Don't be a party pooper. Saturday, Sunday is called Meg Gizzy time." And the rationale: "do not try to push the gas too hard, because what happens is you take away from the valuable time when you're going to need the gas." Also: "when you try to do two things at once, you're not really going to engage with the work like you want to."
- **Rapport.** An extended riff on her email handle (meglizzy) opened the session, and he used the nickname back later. She is 23.

## Worked examples

**Polio vaccines, OPV vs IPV (weaken), from her drill, question 5.** Stimulus: no new cases of naturally occurring polio in North America in recent years, yet approximately 12 new cases each year, all caused by the live oral polio vaccine (OPV). Substituting inactivated polio vaccine (IPV) for most childhood immunizations would cut vaccination-caused polio cases in half. Clearly it is time to switch from OPV to IPV as the most commonly used vaccine for North American children.
- SMACKR walkthrough. Structure: last line is the conclusion; evidence is the halving. Method: comparative reasoning. Assumption: that halving the vaccine-caused cases makes IPV better all things considered, ignoring possible downsides.
- Meghan chose **D** (most countries with comprehensive immunization programs use OPV). Joshua's verdict: not impossible, but weak. "The fact that most people believe something to be true doesn't make it actually true. This answer doesn't engage with the argument in the way we'd want it to. It's kind of an appeal to authority."
- **A is correct** (if IPV replaces OPV, at least a few new cases of naturally occurring polio will result each year). Tier one. It introduces a downside the argument never accounted for, and critically it isolates IPV, since the stimulus establishes OPV has produced zero naturally occurring cases. Joshua acknowledged the answer is weakly worded: "this is very weakly worded, which is a very scary sign. I look at that and I don't like that. But it doesn't take much to negate the supposed advantage."
- **E fails the symmetry breaker** (IPV involves a slight risk, like most vaccines). The "like most vaccines" phrasing means OPV could carry it too. "Answer choices that will negatively affect the side we want to negatively affect, we naturally get that green light in our head. But it has to be isolating the one variable we need to isolate."
- Joshua also floated a mechanism as background color: an inactivated vaccine may not trigger the immune response as reliably as a live one.

**Sugar and hyperactivity (weaken), the question preceding the polio one.** Stimulus: we can now dismiss the widely held suspicion that sugar consumption often exacerbates hyperactivity in children with attention deficit disorder. A study of three common sugars (sucrose, fructose, glucose), with experimental groups each receiving a type of sugar and a control group receiving a sugar substitute, showed no statistically significant difference between groups in thinking or behavior.
- Meghan flagged "causes" as the striking feature and identified the conclusion as an inverse causal claim, with the study's "no difference" serving as the correlation.
- Assumption isolated by Joshua asking directly: what are they assuming about the sugar substitutes? Meghan answered it herself: that the substitute does not itself exacerbate hyperactivity. "What if they just both have the problem? They're both elevated."
- Goal of the weaken restated explicitly: "our mission right now is we need to show that sugar does lead to hyperactivity."
- Meghan chose **A** (only one of the three sugars used was ever widely suspected of exacerbating hyperactivity), and guessed it was tier three. Joshua corrected: A actually **strengthens**, since it guarantees at least one known-suspect sugar was in the study, closing off the objection that they tested the wrong sugars.
- **D also strengthens** (the study included observation of each group in contexts that generally make children excited and active). "They're basically saying we put them in environments where they should have acted out, and they didn't act out. If anywhere they should have acted out, it was here."
- **C is correct** (consumption of some sugar substitutes exacerbates the symptoms of hyperactivity). Tier one, attacking "that invisible gap, that jump." If both arms of the study elevate hyperactivity, equal outcomes prove nothing about sugar. Joshua called it "a sneaky answer" because ordinary reasonableness would have ruled out the substitute as a factor.
