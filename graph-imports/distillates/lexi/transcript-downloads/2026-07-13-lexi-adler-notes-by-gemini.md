# Lexi Adler, 2026-07-13

## LSAT concepts

- **Main conclusion**: two-step test. It must be (1) the author's opinion, not a factual statement, and (2) receiving explicit support from at least one other statement. Joshua stresses the second half is under-taught: "It doesn't have to be receiving support from the whole stimulus. just one other line."
- **Main conclusion vs main point**: identical terms on the LSAT, along with thesis. "They're literally identical terms." Neither is a summary of the argument, which is the mistake carried over from English class. "The conclusion is only one. It's a tip of the iceberg."
- **Facts cannot be conclusions**: a statement the author presents as empirically measurable cannot be the conclusion, because "describing the world as it is. That's just not anything to discuss." Opinions take many forms: recommendation, prediction, and others.
- **Statements of motive are opinions, not facts**: claims about a person's internal state are inferences. "Could you ever truly know as a fact why I'm doing what I'm doing? Like, can you measure that like under a microscope?"
- **Not every sentence is a premise or conclusion**: background information, opposing viewpoints, concessions, and context all exist. Joshua flags treating every line as premise-or-conclusion as a common student error.
- **Referential phrasing**: words like "this," "they," "their" point back to earlier content, and the fix is to physically substitute the referent. Joshua calls this "notoriously good... very sneaky," and hardest in reading comprehension.
- **Active reading, operationalized**: the standard advice fails because "there's no specificity of like how do I actually operationalize that?"

## Worked examples

Main conclusion question, four-sentence stimulus about a political candidate (Epson/Ebsen) sending campaign ads. Lexi initially chose B; correct answer is C.

Sentence-by-sentence path taken:
1. "Normally political candidates send out campaign material in order to influence political opinions." Established as status quo, and as the setup for a coming contrast.
2. "But the recent ads for Epson's campaign were sent out to too few households to serve this purpose effectively." Referent substitution: "this purpose" = influencing popular opinion. Ruled out as conclusion because it is factual: a household count either meets a threshold or does not. Joshua: "whether it's 50, a thousand, a million, he didn't meet the number threshold... So it cannot be the conclusion."
3. "The ads were evidently sent out to test their potential to influence popular opinion." Joshua drills the gap between the normal purpose (influencing opinion) and this one (testing potential to influence). Lexi: "One actually happens and one is right." He then pushes on "evidently," which she connects to evidence and "clearly," and she concludes it reads as opinion. Confirmed by the motive argument: internal motives cannot be empirically measured, so this is an inference and therefore opinion.
4. "They covered a wide variety of topics and Epson's campaign has been spending heavily on follow-up to gauge their effect on recipients." Referents: "they" = the ads, "their" = the ads' effects. Lexi works out the function unprompted: "they're trying to figure out what specifically played a role in the effect." Joshua: "Slam dunk."

Resolution: sentence 4 supports sentence 3, sentence 3 is opinion, so sentence 3 is the main conclusion. Lexi arrives at answer C herself.
