# Thaniuska Vivas, 2026-03-09

A dense foundations session covering validity, the two-premise structure of a valid argument, sufficient and necessary conditions from scratch, and then conclusion identification (opposing viewpoints, concessions, FABS, where the conclusion hides). Joshua compresses two sessions' worth of material into one because a prior session ran short.

## LSAT concepts

- **Argument** — Thaniuska's memorized definition, endorsed: "a premise or set of premises followed by a conclusion. And the premises have to connect the dots and lead to the conclusion for it to be valid."
- **Validity is binary** — "validity is binary. It's not like, oh, something is pretty valid. It's either 100% guaranteed without exception that the conclusion must be true or it's 0%. There is no in between. We do not accept anything but 100%. If there is even one potential conceivable objection, instantly it's invalid."
- **The two-premise model of a valid argument** — every conclusion has a **subject** (the naming part, "the star of the show," the defendant) and a **predicate** (the telling part, "the charge"). A valid argument needs exactly two things: a **situational-fact premise** about the subject ("static premise"), and a **rule / criterion / definition premise** that qualifies the predicate ("bridging premise" or "active premise"). "Every argument is invalid — one of these is going to be missing. Either they're going to give you a rule without the facts, or they're missing a fact that falls into the criteria."
- **The conclusion is the baby of the premises** — "the land of validity lives upon the intersection of the premises... it has to be a restatement of both of the premises. If it brings up any new idea outside the intersection, then you have a problem."
- **The LSAT must define its own terms** — "terms that are factual or descriptions or even telling you what qualifies something to be something, if it's not stated on the LSAT, you don't give them credit for it."
- **Ambiguity as the flaw mechanism** — a fact stated as "damaged a building" cannot satisfy a rule about "burn a building down." Thaniuska's own diagnosis: "damage a building could be, oh, I punched a hole in the wall... it's too broad." Joshua's rule: "you want to stick to the language they use as close as possible, because when you use even words that are almost synonymous, you're introducing a great risk."
- **Conceptual overlap, not verbatim overlap** — the linking premise does not need identical wording. "We don't need a verbatim overlap of terms. We just need a conceptual overlap."
- **Sufficient condition** — "it's enough" (Thaniuska supplied this). "A condition that is enough to know about another condition"; the **guarantor**. Non-temporal: "a sufficient condition can happen after or before." Critically: "it's a guarantee to one outcome, but it's not the only way to get that outcome. This is literally the most common trick on the LSAT."
- **Unactivated conditional** — if the sufficient is not met, "the chain has not been activated... it's dead there. We can't make any claim whatsoever."
- **Necessary condition** — "the requirement." Meeting it tells you nothing: "you know nothing else, you just know that the necessary condition is met." Failing it kills the sufficient.
- **The only two inferences** — "with a conditional statement we only know what is said when the sufficient condition is met, and we know the one other thing, the contrapositive, which is when the necessary condition is not met. Those are the only two things." Contrapositive = flip and negate.
- **Conditionals are must-be-true only** — "conditionals only deal with must be true. There's no such thing as likely, probably... a possibility is not enough."
- **Sufficient assumption vs sufficient condition** — flagged as a naming failure in the prep industry: "this is a failure of the LSAT prep. They don't differentiate sufficient assumption from sufficient condition. The words sound so similar, so they sound like they're talking about the same thing, but they're different things."
- **Opposing viewpoint** — anyone else's opinion in an LSAT stimulus is always refuted. "The LSAT will never bring another argument just to say it... think of the LSAT author as a narcissist. He always thinks he's right and he never agrees with the other side... The purpose of them bringing up anybody else is only to serve the purpose of refuting them."
- **Concession** — "granting the other side, acknowledging they have some validity in their point." Introduced by "although." Structural payoff: "if I see a concession, instantly I know that the conclusion is about to follow... where there's smoke there's fire." Always followed by a pivot word (but, yet, however) and then the conclusion. Joshua's definition: "concession is essentially taking two steps back just to go three steps forward."
- **Where the conclusion lives** — for Main Conclusion and Role questions, "they're almost never the last sentence" (about 90%). Two expected places: the very first sentence ("because people don't expect to see it here"), or a rebuttal conclusion following an opposing viewpoint or concession. Structural reason: "expect that the conclusion will always have some evidence after it. Don't expect evidence then conclusion. Expect conclusion then the evidence."
- **Two tests for a conclusion** — "the author's opinion, and it has to be explicitly supported by another statement. These two checks have to be met. If one of these things is missing, it cannot be the conclusion."
- **FABS** — premise indicators: **F**or, **A**fter all, **B**ecause, **S**ince. "Whenever you see a premise indicator word, instantly you should know that it's a guarantee a premise will follow. There's no way around it. Sometimes conclusion indicator words can trick you because they can be subconclusions, but premise indicator words by definition have to include a premise." The indirect use is the valuable one: "whatever is said prior to the premise indicator word has to be a conclusion. So it's indirectly a conclusion indicator word too." And two back-to-back premise indicators: "the statement in between those two is going to be intermediate."
- **Referential language** — "they," "it," "this" hide the conclusion. "They'll put the *they are wrong*. It's very short. You'll see a full paragraph in the stimulus and they'll say *however they are wrong*. That part is the conclusion. People will naturally miss it because sometimes the conclusion could be three or four words." Fix: replace the pronoun with what it points at. "Ambiguity is the enemy of this test."
- **Rearticulating a rebuttal conclusion in the positive** — when the author says the other side is wrong, the real conclusion is the negation of their claim. "If I say some people believe running is good for you, but they are wrong — I'm implicitly arguing running is not good for you."
- **Four categories for every claim** — main conclusion, support (premise or subconclusion), neutral (background), opposing (concession or opposing viewpoint). "Every single statement on the LSAT will fall into one of these four. If you cannot categorize each statement as you go — think of it like surgical — then there's a problem."
- **LSAT absolutes** — "when the LSAT says *not*, they mean it universally. They only speak universally... not means never." Illustration: "if I say Canadians are nice, that would mean every single Canadian is nice. If there's even one Canadian that's not nice, that statement is wrong." Unless explicitly qualified, read every claim at maximum strength.

## Teaching moves

- **Open by asking her to define the thing, cold.** "I'll ask you what's an argument, just in a very concise fashion." She had it memorized; he named that as the win: "you hit it. I was like, she was ready."
- **Correct a reversed conditional the moment it appears, in slow motion.** She proposed "any act of arson involves burning a building down." Joshua: "you said any act of arson requires burning a building down. But that's saying if you're guilty of arson then you burn a building down. That doesn't mean that if you burn a building down that you're guilty of arson. Those are two different things." He then shows the fix is not another premise but reversing the relationship: "we want to take it and flip it around and say what do we know about burning a building down that leads you to a guaranteed outcome of arson."
- **Escalating analogy stack for one idea.** Sufficient/necessary is taught four ways in sequence: Nicaragua/Spanish speaker (built from her own background), landmines, a gun trigger, a dropped pen. Thaniuska supplied Minesweeper herself and he seized it: "that's probably a perfect example."
- **Personalize the example to the student.** Nicaragua for a Nicaraguan-American; law school admissions for a prelaw student; "T should be a bodybuilder" built from her own weightlifting. He hands her the construction: "give me a premise that would be for T."
- **"Be dumber."** His diagnosis of why conditionals break people: "the problem people often have is they overthink it. You want to almost be dumber. The dumber you are, the easier it is to understand this. Because when you start trying to be smart and think about it, you end up tricking yourself into seeing things that are not there."
- **Reduce it to a visual rule.** "If I ask you a question and I start off with saying anything but what would be on the left side of the arrow, you don't know it... The trigger has to be pulled. Either the contrapositive or the regular. If it's not, you don't know anything." Explicit permission to stop thinking: "You turn your brain off."
- **Name it as muscle memory, not insight.** "Muscle memory is one of the biggest parts of this... Everything else, you have to discipline yourself to say, I have no idea."
- **Let her choose the path when the session forks.** "We can carry on with the conditionals or we can go into the argument structure. What do you feel like you would rather do?" She picked argument structure; conditionals became a take-home drill.
- **Legal framing throughout.** Predicate as "the charge," subject as "the defendant," and the lawyer analogy for rule-plus-facts: "if you're a lawyer and somebody says, they caught me with my hand in the cookie jar, what am I serving? You'd have to know what the rules are. You have to look at the book of law."
- **Praise the specific move, not the answer.** When she wrote "anyone who does weightlifting at least once a week should be a bodybuilder": "Beautiful. You slammed on that... you want a step above, because the simple way is to say anybody who weightlifts twice a week, but you did at least once a week, which they'll do something more nefarious with."
- **Domestic analogy for concession.** The dishes-on-the-counter scenario, two routes to the same demand: the combative one ("pack your bags, you're out of here") and the conceding one ("listen babe, I know you work so hard... but I need you to not leave the dishes on the counter"). Then the LeBron/Jordan version for the LSAT form.
- **Correct her out of a habit of hedging.** She said "you're probably not from Nicaragua." Joshua: "You're guaranteed not from Nicaragua." She repeated it back corrected.
- **Note the harder-but-valid route.** When she reasoned to a premise without using FABS: "you understood it intuitively, which is useful too. It just makes your life easier. You did it the harder way, you got to the same outcome... but a lazy person could have just said, oh yeah, I see *for*, *after all*."
- **Signpost the load.** "I'm throwing a lot at you right now. So if I'm going fast or if there's something I'm not clear about, please stop me." And at the end: "I kept you long today, but I wanted to make up for the other day."

## Worked examples

- **Alex / arson (constructed)** — "Alex burned a building down, therefore Alex is guilty of arson." Invalid: no definition of arson. The needed second premise is the guaranteed direction, "anybody that burns a building down is guilty of arson," not the requirement direction. Variant run: give the rule but strip the fact ("Alex is guilty of arson") — missing premise is the fact that Alex meets the criteria. Second variant: substitute "Alex damaged a building" for the fact — now the argument fails on ambiguity, and the assumption is "damaged a building equals burned a building down."
- **Mike / University of Miami (constructed)** — "Mike wants to go to a great law school, therefore Mike wants to go to University of Miami." Missing rule: Miami is a good law school. Run in reverse to show the other failure mode.
- **T should be a bodybuilder (constructed)** — Subject T, predicate "should be a bodybuilder." Fact premise supplied by Thaniuska: lifts weights twice a week. Rule premise she supplied: "anyone who does weightlifting at least once a week should be a bodybuilder." Praised for making the criterion looser than the fact so the fact clears it.
- **Natural selection / perfect adaptation (real LSAT, Main Conclusion, described as level 5)** — "It is a mistake to think, as ecologists once did, that natural selection will eventually result in organisms that will be perfectly adapted to their environments. After all, perfect adaptation of an individual to its environment is impossible. For an individual's environment can vary tremendously; no single set of attributes could possibly prepare an organism to cope with all the conditions it could face." She flagged the combative first sentence on sight ("Oh, they're attacking already"). She located "after all" as a premise indicator. Two premise indicators back to back ("after all," "for") make the middle statement a subconclusion, leaving sentence one as the main conclusion. She then rearticulated it from the author's side: "natural selection will not eventually result in these organisms perfectly adapting to their environments." Answer B, which she nearly rejected for saying "never" — resolved by the LSAT-absolutes rule. She also noticed the semicolon substituting for a second "because": "I can tell they were trying to trick me."
