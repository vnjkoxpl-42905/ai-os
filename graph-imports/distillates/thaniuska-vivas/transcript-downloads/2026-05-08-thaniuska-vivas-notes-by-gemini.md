# Thaniuska Vivas, 2026-05-08

A conditional-logic session two months after the foundations session: Joshua re-grounds sufficient/necessary, lays out the three ways conditionals get tested, teaches the "unless" translation rule, and then runs four hard conditional-heavy questions. She goes four for four, including a level-five principle question.

## LSAT concepts

- **Necessary condition = mandatory.** Thaniuska supplied the synonym; Joshua locked it in: "it's literally it." A necessary condition "is a mandatory condition that has to be met for it to be possible," and meeting it tells you nothing. He also caught her earlier definition ("something that must happen if everything in an argument is true") as a description of a **necessary assumption**, not a necessary condition — "a conditional statement isn't an argument per se."
- **Sufficient condition = enough.** "A condition that tells you that's just enough to know about the other idea." Illustrated with Miami Law: knowing someone got in is enough to guarantee they took the LSAT and went to university — "I don't know what score you got, I can't tell you if you got a 180 or a 150. But I know you took the LSAT."
- **The three ways conditionals are tested.** Joshua's own taxonomy, stated as exhaustive: "there's not really any other way they'll test you with conditional understanding."
  1. **Checklists / requirements** — necessary conditions dressed up as a to-do list. "They'll bait you into thinking, okay, they met the requirements, so therefore they got into the law school. We don't know that."
  2. **Subsets** — categories with conceptual overlap; the failure mode is inverting which is the subset. "The LSAT is the big circle, the big piece of pie, and University of Miami Law School is the part that falls under it."
  3. **One way vs the only way** — "the most popular trick in the book." Gardening guarantees inner peace, but so might reading or working out. "If they tell you three different ways, that doesn't mean the three ways are the only ways."
- **Where those three show up** — Thaniuska predicted it herself: weaken and flaw questions. Joshua: "almost exclusively flaw questions, strengthening, weakening questions could be there too."
- **False dichotomy connection** — Thaniuska volunteered that one-way/only-way "kind of reminds you of the false dichotomy." Joshua endorsed the link: "it's the same sort of mechanism in our minds where we think if this doesn't happen, the other thing has to be. Not necessarily, it could be a third option, fourth option."
- **The "unless" rule** — three mechanical steps: (1) identify the logical operator, (2) identify the two ideas it bridges, in their natural form including any negation, (3) "take either idea and put it on the left side of the arrow. It doesn't matter which idea. You negate it. And then the other idea you put on the right side of the arrow and you leave it in its natural form. You don't touch it." Either choice produces equivalent statements; the tip is to pick the idea that is already negative so the double negative resolves to a positive sufficient condition, which reads more intuitively.
- **Second reading of "unless" — the exception frame.** "Unless introduces an exception... the first thing that's said beforehand is always the case with this one exception."
- **Why translate at all** — "all of it comes down to one thing: we're trying to bring it back into our native tongue." The if-then form is the currency: "we're trying to get the USD... the exchange rate is the USD, the golden standard, and that's the if-then."
- **The only two known outcomes** — repeated from the March session and now held under drilling. "Look at the left side of the arrow. Whenever I say if — if I don't start off the sentence with one of these two ideas, you just don't know it."
- **Structural analysis vs logical analysis** — a distinction Joshua draws explicitly: "the analysis of the logic is very separate from the analysis of the structure." The last sentence being the conclusion is structural; identifying conditionals and the missing link is logical. Both are needed.
- **Sufficient assumption and must-be-true reward conditionals** — "whenever you see them in sufficient assumption or must be true questions, it's a good sign, because you know that you can get the answer with full certainty."
- **Principle questions** — Thaniuska's method, endorsed: "almost like a reiteration of whatever is in the stimulus but more generalized." Joshua: "you're looking for a guiding principle... a generalized rule as to what would justify this argument."
- **Assumptions in two-conclusion arguments** — "whenever we see an intermediate conclusion, the assumption doesn't have to come at the end. It can come between the first premise and the subconclusion, or between the subconclusion and the main conclusion. The gap could be in two spots."
- **Conceptual overlap, not word match** — "a very popular trick they will do is they will rephrase the same idea. Instead of saying ulterior motive, they replaced it with intentions. But the word match is not what we're looking for. We're looking for a meaning match."
- **Don't grant undefined terms** — Thaniuska applied this herself twice, unprompted: "I'm not going to assume what it means because I'm not going to give him credit. It's his job to define what subcontract means," and on the Mercury passage, "as much as I like astronomy, I don't know what the hell a perihelion is, so I'm not going to assume anything."

## Teaching moves

- **Insist on the intuitive version after the textbook version.** She defined sufficient as "the doorway that leads to something that must happen." Joshua: "you're not wrong. I just want to prod that a bit more because you said the answer like it's a very scholastic answer, which is good, but I want to make sure we understand it on an intuitive level." He then asks for the plain-English synonym, and she produces "enough."
- **Drop the LSAT frame when a definition stalls.** "Forget the LSAT, just forget about the LSAT for now. What's another way of saying sufficient?" Same move used in the March session; it works both times.
- **Argue for intuitive over mechanical understanding by naming the failure mode.** "If you don't understand it on an intuitive level, you'll second guess yourself... and then when the pressure hits, when the section comes into doing it timed and your back is against the wall, you're just going to default to what you feel more comfortable with, which could be bad."
- **The bilingual analogy for translation.** Built on her actual languages: hearing French when you speak Spanish, you get pieces; you want it in Spanish. "That native tongue, that language we speak more fluently, is if-then."
- **Extract the example from the student.** "Tell me one of your hobbies." She said gardening, then reading, then apologized: "I have grandma hobbies." He built the whole one-way/only-way lesson on gardening → inner peace.
- **Restate the abstract link in blunt operational language.** On the subcontracting question: "when you put the two rules in the same room and watch them kind of fight... the first rule said that farming out work forces you to lose some control. But the second rule said the specific guys we're going to hire are required to keep 100% control. So the only thing they're mathematically forbidden from doing is taking the work and sending it out." Then he audits his own explanation: "that was kind of a longer more fluffy way of saying it."
- **Handle the trap answer by asking what the author actually thinks, in the crudest terms.** For the moral-action question he abandons technical vocabulary: "He's saying screw morality entirely... let's look at the numbers. We don't need to worry about whether people are trying to do good by us or bad by us. What was the actual tangible result?" Then the diagnosis of E: "E makes consequences the mandatory requirement for judging morality. But he's just saying we don't care about morality... The author isn't trying to use consequences as a tool to help us judge morality. E kind of ignores that starting fact and just blindly mashes together morality and consequences, and because they're together it looks attractive."
- **Break the assumption with a concrete moral case.** The charity example: donate $100,000 to an African village purely because someone is watching. "Am I a bad person? Was that a moral action?" Thaniuska: "in the end it becomes a good action because it led to a good consequence." Joshua immediately converts her own answer into the argument's flaw: "you just said it's still a moral action even if I only did it because you're watching. That's the big assumption here — they're assuming that intentions are important to knowing morality, but they didn't do their job to establish that."
- **Withhold the answer after the diagnosis.** "One of the answers addresses those two ideas — intentions and morality — and connects them. I'm not going to say which one. I'll give you another chance to look at it."
- **Correct a category error mid-answer.** She called the conclusion the assumption. Joshua: "that's not the assumption. That's the conclusion. The assumption is the difference between the evidence and the conclusion."
- **Refuse a false self-diagnosis.** When she blamed "indispensable" on English being her second language: "I actually don't think that's fair. You're being way too hard on yourself. Even as I read it, it's very awkward." And when she asked whether she should read more: "I don't think that's your issue. And if it was, I'm very brutally honest. Your English comprehension ability is not going to be the component... that's not your problem. I wouldn't even put that on you."
- **Anchor praise to question difficulty.** "This is a level five, hardest question. You'll never get a question harder than this... you went four for four, all conditional-heavy, all different types of questions to poke at different components. You played a trick on me today. You had me thinking you're going to come in rough, but you came in swinging."
- **Note speed as evidence, not just accuracy.** "You got to D very fast, which is the other thing that's impressing me — you're getting there in an efficient fashion. You're not lollygagging."

## Worked examples

- **Subcontracting (necessary assumption, level 4)** — Company president: "Whenever you subcontract the manufacturing of a product, you lose some control over the quality of that product," but "we subcontract only with companies that maintain complete control over the quality of the products they supply." Conditional indicators she spotted: "whenever" (= if) and "only with." Answer A. The missing link: the subcontractors must not themselves subcontract the same work. She got it quickly and with confidence, and credited the method: "thinking of it as something's missing and playing the detective, figuring out what can connect these two that they're not directly saying." Joshua: "A puzzle. Exactly."
- **Dairy inspectors (sufficient assumption)** — Chain: if the budget does not allow more dairy inspectors, most large Central Valley dairies will not meet federal waste-disposal standards, which lets waste seep into streams and groundwater. The new budget does not allow more inspectors. Conclusion: most of the district's drinking water is likely to become polluted. She built the chain in writing as she read: "I was jotting it down and then as I was writing it down I realized it connects." Answer D, supplying the missing link — if dairies fail the standards, then the drinking water becomes polluted. She reported second-guessing herself during review despite being right.
- **Mercury's perihelion (principle / justify, level 4)** — The advance of Mercury's perihelion is cited as evidence for general relativity because no other theory predicts it as simply and accurately; but the phenomenon was already known when Einstein developed the theory, and he probably adjusted his equations to generate the right numbers; therefore accounting for it should not count as evidence for the theory. She needed three reads because of the vocabulary but refused to guess at "perihelion." Answer D: if a theory is adjusted specifically to account for a particular phenomenon, a match between the theory and the phenomenon should not count as evidence in its favor.
- **Moral action / ulterior motives (principle / justify, level 5)** — "One can never tell whether another person is acting from an ulterior motive. Therefore it is impossible to tell whether someone's action is moral. So one should evaluate the consequences of an action rather than its morality." Two conclusions: subconclusion (can't tell if an action is moral) and main conclusion (evaluate consequences instead). Two gaps, and the load-bearing one sits in the first: the author assumes you must know motives to judge morality. She chose E by elimination — "one cannot know whether someone acted morally unless one knows what consequences that person's actions had" — which inverts the argument by making consequences a tool for judging morality rather than a replacement for it. Correct answer A: "the intention of an action is indispensable for any evaluation of its morality." She had been between A and E and rejected A only because she did not know the word "indispensable." She eliminated B ("assigning praise and blame is what is most important" — not established), C ("one can sometimes know one's own motives" — contradicts the premise), and D ("there can be good actions not performed by a good person" — no mention of motive; Joshua: "I don't see any motive in D").
