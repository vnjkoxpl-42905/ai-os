# Dillen Sampson, 2025-06-01

## LSAT concepts

- **Conditional logic vs causation**: the session's opening distinction, prompted by Dillen having missed several questions by reading conditionals as causal. "Causality by definition means explanation. Conditionals are more like, if you know one thing to be true." Joshua explicitly warns against the intuitive mapping of sufficient onto cause and necessary onto effect: "People will fall into the trap of thinking the sufficient is the cause and the effect is necessary. That's not the case. It could be the other way around."
- **Sufficient and necessary conditions, diagrammed**: left of the arrow is always sufficient, right is always necessary, and the arrow is read left to right only. "Think of it as a train. If I jump on this left side, whatever's happening on this side, it'll take me to this outcome. We never read right to left."
- **Contrapositive**: the only valid inference from a conditional. Two steps in order, "step one flip, step two negate." The original and its contrapositive are logically equivalent and interchangeable.
- **Negation**: not the logical opposite, but anything crossing out of the original category. "The negation of hot is not necessarily cold. That could be room temperature." Applied to the example, not studying hard does not mean not studying at all.
- **The closed-world rule for conditionals**: only the statement and its contrapositive are known. "If it's not literally one of these two situations, we do not know it, no matter how close it is."
- **Conditional indicator words**: sufficient-side triggers include if, when, only, each, any. Joshua treats them as fixed groups with fixed rules, "like crime families, they all have their own rules."
- **Universal claims as implicit conditionals**: an unqualified group claim is a universal. "If I said apples are green, they're saying if it's an apple, then it's green."
- **Certainty and requirement language as conditional signals**: "Whenever they speak in total certainty, or they mention actual requirements, always keep in mind that's going to be referring to conditionals."
- **Probabilistic conditionals**: "likely" and "probably" are interchangeable, but a likely consequent does not license a guaranteed inference. This was the entire flaw in the stimulus.
- **Parallel reasoning**: match by structure, so both the direction of the arrow and the polarity (negated vs affirmative) of each half must correspond.

## Teaching moves

- **Justifying the notation before teaching it.** "English is not an effective way to communicate logical relationships. It's too wordy." The point of diagramming is offloading: "We don't want to map it out so that we have to keep it in our heads. We can keep logical relationships on paper and see it visually."
- **Rejecting letter variables, a deliberate break with conventional instruction.** "If you learn conditionals from traditional teachers, they'll tell you how to do it with notational forms, LS or L. I find that very tricky for students, because once you look back and read it, it can be very jarring." Prescribes shortened real words instead, and pre-empts the objection about speed: "It takes like an extra second, which makes no difference, and it's usually the difference between a question right or wrong."
- **Turning off judgment on purpose.** "It'll be very important for us to almost turn your brain off, to force yourself to live and die by certain principles that give you the ability to never veer off from the right path."
- **Naming the trap before the student meets it.** He flags that answer choices often present the contrapositive rather than the original form, so the student does not panic when the expected wording is absent. Dillen finished the thought himself: "So sometimes the answer choices will have the contrapositive and that's where people get tripped up." Joshua's term for it: "a little smoke screen."
- **Abstracting the stimulus to letters for the parallel match.** Once diagrammed, he converts it: if A then B is likely, and if B is not likely then A is not. Then the task is finding the answer whose content plugs into that same skeleton.
- **Handing the work back rather than finishing it.** "I'll let you take a shot at it, it's much better for you to do it." He repeatedly asks Dillen to produce the diagram and its contrapositive before revealing anything.
- **Letting the student beat him to the answer.** When Dillen worked out that answer B fails because the stimulus's second half is negated and B's is affirmative, Joshua's response was "You beat me to the punch. Exactly. That's the problem. Bang on."
- **Correcting a near-miss precisely rather than accepting it.** Dillen said answer A fails because the two statements "aren't going the same direction." Joshua: "Somewhat, not exactly though." Then he isolated the real defect, that Ben is never established as an artisan, versus the stimulus where Jackie is unambiguously inside the group. "Who's Ben? I have no idea who Ben is."
- **Blocking an assumption in real time.** Dillen: "I'm going to assume an artisan." Joshua: "Well, we can't assume, but correct."
- **Warning against the tool becoming the obstacle**, the session's closing note. "Don't lose the forest for the trees. Sometimes conditionals can be in the way of understanding. If it's easier to understand it intuitively, don't let yourself think you have to do diagramming and get lost in that."
- **Acknowledging the compression openly.** "This is very condensed, this usually is taught over a span of time, but you'll be fine, it's more for you to get familiar with it."

## Worked examples

- **Teaching example**: "If you get into law school, then you studied hard." Sufficient is getting into law school, necessary is having studied hard. Contrapositive: if you did not study hard, you did not get into law school. Used specifically because it inverts the naive cause/effect mapping, since the cause (studying) sits in the necessary slot.
- **Parallel reasoning question, births before due date.** Stimulus: if a mother's first child is born before its due date, it is likely that her second child will be also. Jackie's second child was not born before its due date, so it is likely Jackie's first child was not either. Diagrammed as first child early, arrow, second child likely early, with the contrapositive being second child not likely early, arrow, first child not early. The flaw: the argument treats a "likely" consequent as if it were guaranteed, so the failure of the second child to arrive early does not deliver the contrapositive. Joshua: "Likely doesn't mean guaranteed. They missed the likely part."
  - A (artisans and the craft fair, Ben will not finish so he probably will not go): rejected, but not for the reason Dillen first offered. The internal reasoning is consistent, the defect is that Ben is never placed inside the artisan group, whereas Jackie is unambiguously a mother in the stimulus.
  - B (all responsible pet owners are likely good with children, so anyone good with children is probably a responsible pet owner): diagrammed correctly, then rejected on polarity. The stimulus's second statement runs through negated terms, B's runs through affirmative ones, so the structures do not match. "It started off good, but then it didn't end off properly. It should have said if someone is not good with children, then you'd expect them to not be a responsible pet owner."
  - C (if a movie is a box office hit, its sequel likely will be too; Hawkman 2 was not a box office hit, so Hawkman 1 probably was not either): correct. Same arrow direction, same "likely" hedge, same negated second statement.
