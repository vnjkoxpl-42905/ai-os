# Moses Bryant, 2025-11-16

## LSAT concepts
- **Sufficient assumption** — the goal is to make the conclusion a guarantee: "it should be impossible to falsify the conclusion." Joshua's stem tell: the word "if" alongside "assume," or "which one would allow the conclusion to be properly inferred," since "inferred means must be true."
- **The new-concept rule (Joshua's "cheat code")** — any concept appearing in the conclusion but not in the evidence must appear in the correct sufficient assumption answer. Reason given: if the conclusion must be guaranteed by the evidence alone, a new concept means the author assumed something about it. Applies 100% of the time to sufficient assumption, roughly 80 to 90% to necessary assumption.
- **Must be true** — never arguments, only sets of facts. "They're just saying what can you say based off these facts." Joshua calls must-be-true and sufficient assumption "cousins": sufficient assumption asks what would make the conclusion valid, must be true asks you to produce a valid conclusion.
- **Comparative versus absolute claims** — a conclusion that says "more frequent than other countries" is a relative claim. An answer establishing that crises are merely "common" in one group does not establish the comparison.
- **No outside knowledge** — you may not import even common sense. If the argument does not define arson, you cannot supply the definition.
- **Necessary assumption, strengthen, weaken** — positioned relative to sufficient assumption: sufficient assumption is "like a super strengthener," a strengthener only has to make a problem less likely, and a weakener attacks the same crack.
- **Prephrasing** — build the missing link before reading answers, framed as an if-then bridge from where the evidence stops to where the conclusion begins.

## Teaching moves
- **Prosecutor framing for sufficient assumption**: "our goal is to be as though we're like trying to be prosecutors... we need to prove that it's guilty." Later flips it to defense: once you have attacked your own argument, "now I just want to play defense mode. I have to make sure they don't come up with that."
- **"Building blocks" / "finish line"** as the diagnostic on evidence: the premises build one idea, "but do they take us to the finish line?" The finish line is the conclusion.
- **Bridge construction procedure, stated as a recipe**: take the exact language of where the evidence stops, make it the "if," take where the conclusion starts, make it the "then." Do this before reading any answer choice.
- **Everyday analogy to expose the comparative gap**: "if I said you're more likely to become a millionaire than your best friend, does that mean I'm saying it's likely you're going to become a multi-millionaire? No, I'm saying comparatively." Then a second pass with France versus Canada, so the point lands twice with different content.
- **Refuses credit for implication.** When Moses said the small elite "would have political power" and admitted "this is the thing the author is saying without directly saying," Joshua drew the hard line: "if the author doesn't say what they're directly saying, we cannot give them credit for it."
- **The Alex/arson hypothetical**, built live on a whiteboard, as the vehicle for the whole cheat code. He deliberately blocks Moses from importing the definition of arson, saying it "fights common sense," and drives the rule home with absurd extensions: "if I don't tell you water is wet, you cannot assume that it's wet. If I don't tell you the sun shines..."
- **Ties precision to the profession**: "the letter of the law is very precise... the law is a precise endeavor." And later, "lawyers are not reasonable. Lawyers will say technically the word of the law says... My job is not to be fair. My job is to win."
- **Constructs the rule inductively rather than announcing it.** He runs the arson argument in two directions (missing the arson definition, then missing Alex) and asks Moses to find the sufficient assumption under a constraint each time. Moses discovers that the missing term is always the one appearing in the conclusion but not the evidence, and only then does Joshua name it: "this is what gives you the cheat code."
- **Immediately gamifies the rule.** They rescan the answer choices for the new concept alone and cut A, C, D, and E without reading them. "You're already down to a 50% choice without even reading the answer choices."
- **Attack-your-own-argument as the way to find the assumption**: "given that the evidence is true, how can I prove the conclusion is false?" Articulate a specific objection, then find the answer that rules out that world. He insists the objection be concrete, not vague.
- **Boxer metaphor** for playing devil's advocate: "you're almost thinking of it as a fear of boxer, you're projecting where the punch can go so you can deflect it before it comes and hits you."
- **Dam metaphor** for the argument's weak point: the new concept is "a leak in the dam." As sufficient assumption you patch it, as a weakener "you want to use that dam to break the dam open." One image covers three question types.
- **Pop-culture illustration for the art criticism attack**: Kendrick Lamar's good kid, m.A.A.d city losing to Macklemore, used to make concrete the objection that critics may simply be bad indicators of greatness.
- **Callback to session one**: "the conclusion is almost a baby of the evidence... we're full-time validity checkers. The land of validity lives upon the intersection of the premises. If it's not mentioned in the premises, it's being taken from somewhere else and we can't accept that."
- **Explains why he taught by hypothetical rather than by rule**, after Moses' question about question stems: "I want you to understand the hacks... so when you apply them they're not just blindly applying them."

## Worked examples
- **Sufficient assumption, controlled information and economic crises.** Evidence: in some countries there is a free flow of information about infrastructure, agriculture, and industry, whereas in others this information is controlled by a small elite; in the latter, the vast majority of the population is denied vital information about factors determining their welfare. Conclusion: thus these countries are likely to experience more frequent economic crises than other countries do.
  Moses' initial pick was C. Path: prephrase the if-then bridge from denied welfare information to more frequent crises. C fails because "economic crises are common" in elite-controlled nations is absolute, not comparative, and leaves open that crises are even more common in free-flow countries. A fails because "political power" never appears in the stimulus. D (population participation improving decisions) and E (elite manipulating information for its own benefit) both import unsupported content. B is correct: economic crises become more frequent as the amount of information available to the population about factors determining its welfare decreases, which correlates the evidence directly to the conclusion. They then re-ran the whole question with the new-concept rule, checking only for "more frequent economic crises," and reached B by elimination without reading the answers.
- **The arson hypothetical (Joshua's construction, not a real LSAT item).** Version one: Alex burned a building down, therefore Alex is guilty of arson. Missing sufficient assumption: burning a building down means guilty of arson. Version two: anyone who burns a building down is guilty of arson, therefore Alex is guilty of arson. Missing piece: Alex burned a building down. Constraint games (find the assumption without using "Alex," find it without using "guilty of arson") prove that the term new to the conclusion is unavoidable.
- **Sufficient assumption, art criticism.** Many artists claim critics find it easier to write about art they dislike than art they like. Whether or not this hypothesis is correct, most art criticism is devoted to artworks that fail to satisfy the critic. Hence most art criticism is devoted to works other than the greatest works of art.
  New concept in the conclusion: "greatest works of art." A, B, and C do not contain it and were cut unread; D and E remain. The attack: what if critics' satisfaction has nothing to do with whether an artwork is great, what if they simply have bad taste (the Kendrick Lamar example)? The answer must rule out that world. E is correct: the greatest works of art satisfy all critics, since the assumption linking evidence to conclusion is that anything failing to satisfy all critics cannot be the greatest work of art.
