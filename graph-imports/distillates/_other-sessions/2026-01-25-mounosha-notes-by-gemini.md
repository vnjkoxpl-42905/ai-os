# Mounusha Nunna, 2026-01-25

A 55-minute working session on flaw questions: how conditional chains get abused (sufficient/necessary), and the higher-level skill of reading the *verb* that opens each flaw answer choice ("does not consider" vs "assumes" vs "describes") to decide which test to run on it. Closed with an extended consult on LSAT accommodations for her ADHD diagnosis.

## LSAT concepts

- **Conditional chain: head and tail**: linked conditionals form a chain, and the test never rewards knowing the middle. "They'll never ever test your ability to know understand the neighboring idea... they want to reward your ability to see the inference. So they want you to see the head and the tail of the kind of like the dragon here."
- **Only two things are knowable from a chain**: "There's only two things you ever know about any chain: whatever the chain stated as it is, or its contrapositive. Any other iteration, all of those are disqualified instantaneously."
- **Sufficient versus necessary flaw**: negating the sufficient tells you nothing. "If A then B and if B then C — I can't say if not A what happens. Instantly you should say I don't know anything." Joshua's habit-level instruction: any time an *if* clause is not stated exactly as in the chain, or is negated or altered, "you cannot engage with it no matter what they give you."
- **"Should" introduces an opinion, and opinions need criteria**: "When someone says should, or someone says better, you need a criteria. You need a definition for what does better mean? When do you know you should or should not do something?" Then a second step: "after they give us criteria, you have to use that criteria and show how that facts fall into that criteria." He named the formal version as "epistemic justification... but we don't have any standard here."
- **The arguer's burden**: "Their job is to make sure it's impossible for the conclusion to be wrong in any context, so long as the evidence is true. If they leave even one potential opening for say 'but what if maybe' then automatically they lose."
- **The three flaw answer-choice types.** This is the session's central taxonomy. (1) *Objection*: "does not consider," "fails to consider," "ignores a possibility," "the author overlooks a possibility" — all the same thing. (2) *Assumption*: "assumes," "takes for granted," "fails to establish," "presumes" — "interchangeable terms." (3) *Descriptive*: "describes," "rejects" — functions like a method or role question.
- **Objection answers are weakening questions**: "If that part in pink is accepted to be true in our hypothetical, what would that do to our argument? If it would not do anything at all, then we know that it doesn't matter whether you consider it or not."
- **Assumption answers are necessary-assumption questions, and point the opposite way**: "They're kind of almost polar opposites in a sense. If this orange part is true, the argument should be stronger. If the pink part is true, the argument should be weaker."
- **Negation test**: two-step. "First: does the author — if this was true, would it benefit the argument? Oh, it would benefit the argument. Okay, second step: let's negate it and let's see what would happen if we took away that answer choice from the argument."
- **Descriptive flaw answers need two things, not one**: accuracy *and* a problem. "It has to actually say something that's like a problem, right? Not just saying what's happening." His check: "look for the adverbs — is he actually doing the action they're describing? If there's a noun, is a noun actually found in the actual argument?"
- **Necessary assumption reframed as must-be-true**: "You can just say, okay, does the author have to agree with this — versus negating it and seeing if the author's argument would collapse. They're two of the same things."

## Teaching moves

- **The Jenga image for negation**: "Let's see what would happen if we took away that answer choice from the argument, to see as though it's like Jenga, the game of Jenga. You take away that block, you see if the whole thing collapses. If it doesn't collapse, then we don't know for a fact if the author assumed it. It might benefit him, but it doesn't necessarily mean he had to assume it."
- **The "dragon" for a conditional chain** — head and tail, never the neighbouring idea.
- **The "highway" for chaining**: whatever attaches to the repeating idea "will let you basically jump on that highway to take you to the end point of that train."
- **Sports-center replay analogy for descriptive answers**: "Think of it as like a sports center where it's like they tell you the replays of the game before. It has to be accurately reflecting what happened the game the day before. But that's not enough."
- **Teach the criteria concept through a lived example, not the stimulus.** He stopped the surgical-trials question cold and asked her: "If I told you that hey, you should move to Canada instead of living in the United States... let's say Canadian food is better than American food. For me to make that claim, what do I need to be able to establish?" He deliberately withheld the word: "I'm not going to say it out loud because I want you to come to it." She landed on "better's kind of open-ended," and he closed it: "You're right. We need a criteria."
- **Conclusion precision over evidence precision**: "With the evidence, the justification, to be a little bit fluffy with it is okay. The conclusion though, we want to be razor sharp with what precisely is the conclusion trying to push for... The conclusion is the part where you want to have tattooed on you."
- **Teach off her mistake rather than correcting it.** On the first question he asked "give me your thought process in regards to E" before saying anything about whether it was right, then used her (correct) reasoning as the launch pad. On the second, when she named C, he did not mark it wrong — he walked all five choices by verb type and let the taxonomy do the work.
- **Seize the teachable moment when the student surfaces it.** She noticed the question stem read both like an objection and like a necessary assumption; he said "you're flirting with a certain idea that I was going to go into with you eventually... I wasn't planning on doing it with you today, but we can, we might as well at this point," and pulled up a random question on the spot to build the taxonomy. The entire second half of the session was unplanned.
- **Do not over-tool.** He warned against reflexively deploying technique: "You don't have to apply [negation] to all of the assumption questions. That could be more complicated than it's worth. Only when you get down to two options and you're wrestling with it, then you can pull out these higher level tricks." Same for diagramming: "If you see a conditional, it doesn't mean you have to diagram it. It can be useful — when it's useful, use it. When it's not, put it away and put it on the shelf."
- **Deflate absurd answer choices with humour** rather than analysis. On (E), rejecting a good-faith proposal: "It's like he was really trying his best. It's like, thanks. That's not what we're talking about though. It's like, it's cute, you know... He must have been in his feelings for this. Yeah, good faith, pat on the back. But it doesn't give us what we want here."
- **The ankle-weights frame for accommodations.** On whether extra time makes you lazy: "Why don't you do that but then just get 70 minutes? You just study as though you don't have 70 minutes... think of it as like you're putting on ankle weights and you're running, and then when you go on the test day... now you can be comfortable to say there will be zero chances of you not getting enough time." And: "You're never going to say, oh man, I wish I had less time to do this section."
- **Advocate hard on accommodations, with volume as the argument.** "Easily over 50 students... I've only seen one rejection ever in my time." He was careful to hedge before criticising her clinician ("I don't want to speak badly about your doctor") but did not soften the substance: the reading-aloud denial for an ADHD diagnosis he called "almost tyrannical," backed by "I did my thesis on ADHD."

## Worked examples

- **Press agent / reporters, flaw.** Chain: if the press agent told every reporter everything about the accident, then no reporter knows any more about it than any other reporter; if no reporter knows more than any other, then no reporter can scoop all the other reporters. The author concludes: the press agent did *not* tell every reporter everything, so some reporters can scoop. Mounusha correctly identified head ("if the press agent told every reporter everything") and tail ("no reporter can scoop"), and diagnosed the sufficient/necessary flaw herself. Joshua supplied the objection in concrete form: "maybe the press agent told half the story, but they told the same half to all the other reporters." He noted the contrapositive is "if there is a reporter that can scoop, then the press agent told every reporter everything." She got it right; she had picked (E) by reasoning that the answer would be one of the components of the chain she had built.
- **Clinical trials for surgical procedures, flaw.** Conclusion: the proposal to extend to new surgical procedures the clinical trials routinely used as systematic tests of pharmaceutical innovations should not be implemented. Evidence: surgical procedures differ from medicinal drugs — a correctly prescribed drug's effectiveness depends only on the drug's composition, whereas even the most appropriate surgical procedure's effectiveness is transparently related to the surgeon's skill. Gap: no criteria for *should*. Mounusha generated her own objection unprompted: "the drug's composition is also something that people make... it could also be related to the skill of the researcher who created the drug." Joshua added his own: if new surgical procedures are more likely to be dangerous, the rigorous testing is *more* warranted, not less.
  - (A) *does not consider* new surgical procedures might be intrinsically more harmful than the best treatment previously available — correct; treated as a weakener, and it devastates the argument. Mounusha: "that would say that oh, we should actually extend the clinical trials instead."
  - (B) *ignores the possibility* the challenged proposal is deliberately crude to elicit criticism used in refining it — true or not, no impact.
  - (C) *assumes* a surgeon's skills remain unchanged throughout the surgeon's professional life — her original pick. Negated ("a surgeon's skills do change"), nothing happens to the conclusion, so it was never assumed. She recognised it retroactively: "after reading it again, I was like, oh, this is a bad answer choice, what was I thinking?"
  - (D) *describes* a dissimilarity without citing any scientific evidence for its existence — accurate-ish but not a flaw. Mounusha caught this herself: "it wouldn't constitute as a flaw that they didn't do that."
  - (E) *rejects* a proposal presumably advanced in good faith without acknowledging any such good faith — not a problem at all.
