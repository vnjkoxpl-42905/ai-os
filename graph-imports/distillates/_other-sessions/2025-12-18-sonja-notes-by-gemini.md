# Sonja Wilbert, 2025-12-18

## LSAT concepts

- **Conditional logic, intuition over diagramming**: "internalizing it is always far superior than trying to diagram it, because it becomes very abstract in your brain." Diagramming is for when a problem "gets way out of hand." Joshua explicitly contrasted this with Seven Sage's "diagram, diagram, diagram" approach.
- **Contrapositive**: after every conditional, state the only other thing you know. If it is not evoking intense feelings, it is not a work of art.
- **Necessary condition as focal point**: the necessary condition carries more information. If it is not met you know the sufficient is not met; denying the sufficient tells you nothing. "If the necessary condition is not met, you know for sure the sufficient is not met. But based off the sufficient condition, you don't know whether the necessary is met or not."
- **Denying the sufficient**: "denying the sufficient does not mean the necessary condition can't happen. Sufficient is a guaranteed way to get there, but it's not the only way." The conditional simply becomes irrelevant.
- **"Or" vs "and"**: "and" requires both. "Or" on the LSAT is inclusive, it can mean both, which Joshua flagged as counterintuitive. "Or does mean both. It can mean both. It's not at the expense of the other thing."
- **"Some are not" equals "not all"**: equivalent statements.
- **"All" as a conditional trigger**: "all historians are able to spot trends" is a conditional. Reading "anyone" as "all" is a deliberate translation trick to force explicitness.
- **Categorical error / subset treated as set**: the core flaw in the second question. "The historians are a subset and they treated the historians as not a subset. They treat the historians as the set."
- **Parallel reasoning**: match on striking structural features, then on the type and strength of each claim.
- **Parallel flaw**: distinct from ordinary parallel. At the hardest level the structural skeleton will not match one for one, and you must match the *flaw* instead. "They're not testing your ability to see the structure. They're more so seeing if you solve the problem of the argument."
- **Correlation vs causation**: A and B occurring together does not tell you which causes which, or whether a third factor causes both.

## Teaching moves

- **Interrogated the habit, not the answer.** Sonja got the question right, and Joshua's first question was still "is there a particular reason why you went to diagram right away?" He kept pressing when she answered at the wrong level: "the question I asked was slightly actually even more fundamental than that. Why diagram at all? What was the intention behind that?"
- **Reframed her self-diagnosis.** She said she diagrams because she has "a really bad memory." He rejected the premise: "in my impression of you, I don't think you actually... I think your active memory is fairly strong." He renamed the behavior as "a reflexive feeling of intimidation" when she sees "if," and reframed the goal: "the goal is not to memorize it. It's to synthesize it, internalize it."
- **Reframed stalling as the cheaper option.** This is the session's central move. "If you looked at yourself from a third person point of view, you'd realize you're actually wasting more time by not stalling. You end up reading it three times versus if I read it once and it took a longer time to read and understand." He returned to it repeatedly: "not letting the pressure of time force your hand to move forward."
- **The no-rereading rule.** "You should not allow yourself to read it more than once. Make it a hard rule... I'm not going to let my eyes go left. It can only go right." The mechanism he named is removing the crutch: "when you know that you can go back, that crutch is there. You will use it because you know that you can use it. If I say I'm not allowed to go back, that means I will read very slowly and very meticulously the first time."
- **Solve as you read, not at the end.** "I'm solving the puzzle as I read it, not trying to solve the puzzle at the end. Try to assess the cracks as you're putting pieces together."
- **Anticipate the next line before reading it.** "Don't look at the next line and try to force yourself to engage with it prior to seeing what they say versus what you understood so far... once you see it, it'll influence you to make it be true versus what you know to be true." He then walked the stimulus line by line, pausing at each and asking her what must follow, and explicitly said "do not read the next claim" before the conclusion.
- **Named "but" as a pivot signal.** "When we say but, we're like, ah, we already know the pivot word. They're going to do something that contradicts what they said prior."
- **Intuitive substitutions instead of letters.** LA and California is his standing conditional example: "if you're in LA then you're in California... you can intuitively get it." He also offered swapping in content the student finds natural: "you could use a parallel of a different kind of content that might make more sense to you intuitively. Finding that for yourself is kind of a personal process."
- **The striking-feature scan.** For parallel questions, find one distinctive feature, usually in the conclusion, and use it to bulk-eliminate. "Something you can say, I have something in my hand to hold on to that can give me almost like a guiding light. One thing, just one thing." They then scanned only the five conclusions for "or" and killed B (has "and") and D (flat statement) in seconds. "We already got rid of two out of five without really doing much work."
- **Elimination as the default posture.** "We don't look for the answer choice to find out if it's correct. We're trying to find ways to dismiss the answer as fast as possible, because four of the answers are wrong... almost every answer you read, you should think it's almost certain this answer choice is wrong."
- **Speed is downstream of skill.** When Sonja asked whether she could replicate his approach: "the practice is not trying to go faster, the practice is getting that skill down. Then the speed will come very quickly. They go together." Also: "why go fast if you get the answer wrong? If you spend 10 seconds versus 10 minutes, it's the same utility if you get the answer wrong."
- **Endorsed her color-coding.** "Even though you might think I'm not going to do this on test day, it just reinforces the patterns of recognition, and when you take off the training wheels you'll just do it faster."
- **Built the template with her, live, on her request.** Step 1: read to internalize into simple English, gated hard, "if I cannot rephrase the statement I just read, I do not go forward." Step 2: notice language cues for statement type (conditional, causal, correlation). Step 3: test the bounds, articulate what you know and what you do not. He insisted on the priority order: "I don't care if it's a conditional, if it's a causal or correlation statement, if I don't understand what it's saying. That is secondary." And: "all of it is just English. At the end of the day it's just English. It's not actually a calculation test."
- **"Playing with your food."** His phrase for testing the bounds of a claim: "taking it and trying to see the bounds of it."
- **Accepted her analogy and adopted it on the spot.** Sonja compared bounds-testing to logic games: "you're creating worlds and you're answering questions before you get into the answer choices." Joshua: "Phenomenal. Beautifully said... it's actually blowing my mind how I never thought of that connection." He then extended it himself: solve the world up front, and comprehension jumps, "when they bring in the information, you're like, I already solved that world up front, I already know that can't be true." He called it "a cheat code of cheat codes" and the resulting experience "this downhill kind of ride versus running uphill."
- **Personal analogies for set relations.** Every Californian is American but not every American is Californian. Then a shared-identity one: "you're black, I'm black," on Africans, immigration, and African-American identity, to show a subset is not the set. Later, all Cubans dance, all dancers are kind, therefore this kind person is Ethiopian.
- **Admitted confusion in real time on the hardest answer.** Working through A: "Oh, no, no. I'm mixing them up entirely. Whoa. I got kind of tripped out as I read that too. I'm completely off the ball here. This is what got me too." He then restarted in "dumbed down language."
- **Honest about answer quality.** On A: "this answer is super confusing because it's actually not a good answer. It's the least bad. It's the least worst answer." On E: "this is absolutely garbage. From the top... they got fisty with it." On B, he separated his professional advice from his real instinct: "I'm being very professional here, but if I was by myself I'd probably be a degenerate, I'd just get rid of it."
- **Named difficulty explicitly.** "This is like a level five question by the way." He used the tier to set expectations about when clean structural matching stops working: skeleton matching "is usually going to work up to level three, even up to level four."
- **Prescribed an iterative template, not a fixed one.** "You might make a template at first and be like, that was an unnecessary step, and refine the template... throw a bunch of vomit on the paper and say okay, this is the process, and then iterate."

## Worked examples

- **Parallel, works of art (correct: C).** Stimulus: if all works of art evoke intense feelings, and this sculpture is a work of art, then it follows that the sculpture evokes intense feelings; but this sculpture does not evoke intense feelings at all; so either this sculpture is not a work of art, or not all works of art evoke intense feelings. Joshua flagged the unusual structure: the opening "if" makes the whole conditional hypothetical, putting the rule itself in question, and the conclusion is therefore valid. Structure required: a general hypothetical rule, a particular case, denial of the *necessary* condition, and an "or" conclusion with two negations. B eliminated on "and." D eliminated for lacking "or." E eliminated as structurally incoherent (compound "and" inside the conditional, mismatched pieces). A eliminated because "the biology lab is not a class" denies the *sufficient*, not the necessary. Sonja had eliminated A for a different reason ("some classes are not cancelled"); Joshua noted "some" and "not all" are equivalent so that reasoning failed, but "it worked though, it got you there. We'll take our W's when we can get them." C matches exactly: if all vitamins are safe in large doses, beta carotene is a vitamin, therefore beta carotene is safe in large doses; but beta carotene is not safe in large doses; so not all vitamins are safe in large doses, or beta carotene is not a vitamin.
- **Parallel flaw, historians (correct: A, a level-five question).** Stimulus: all historians are able to spot trends; anyone able to spot trends is able to distinguish the significant from the insignificant; thus anyone who can distinguish the significant from the insignificant is a historian. The flaw is a categorical error, treating the subset (historians) as the entire set. They pre-derived the required answer shape before reading choices. Eliminations: C on "or" plus a quantified subset; D for lacking "all" ("no one without a deep desire to communicate can be a blues musician"); E on "and" plus "at least in part," neither present in the original. B (political systems, factionalism, civil disorder) fails because it imports a brand new idea, autocrats, into the conclusion, where the original stays within its own terms and only misappropriates the groups. A (all expressions used for emotional impact are expressions used by poets; all figures of speech are expressions used for emotional impact; so any expression used by poets is a figure of speech) is not a clean structural parallel and Joshua said so, but it commits the same group-mixing error, and at level five the flaw governs over the skeleton.
