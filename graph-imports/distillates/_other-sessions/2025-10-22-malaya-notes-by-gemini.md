# Malaya Jules, 2025-10-22

Joint session. Glenn Gonzales joined partway through with Malaya's consent, and much of the second half is Joshua teaching Glenn.

## LSAT concepts

- **Necessary assumption**: the answer must be something the author had to be thinking, not whatever would help most. "You're not looking for the answer that would be 100% beneficial for the argument... it's something the author must have been thinking." An answer that links the argument perfectly is sufficient assumption territory.
- **Negation test**: negating the right answer should make the conclusion impossible. With conditionals he steers away from it: "we don't want to use negation as much for necessary assumption conditionals. It's going to come in a positive form reaffirming what is the missing link."
- **New concept in the conclusion**: a concept in the conclusion but absent from the evidence must appear in the answer. Guaranteed on sufficient assumption, roughly 80% on necessary assumption. Conceptual, not verbatim.
- **Conclusion as a conditional**: split it into subject and predicate, treat subject as sufficient and predicate as necessary, then require the answer to put the conclusion's idea in the necessary slot.
- **Must be true and conditional logic**: chain the diagrams, then read the repeating concept as the bridge. "Whatever is on the other side of each of the repeating concept is going to be usually what's changed." The inference joins the two outer ideas.
- **Trigger and outcome**: his plain-English names for sufficient and necessary. A rule breaks when "the trigger gets pulled and the outcome doesn't happen."
- **Or is inclusive**: "if they don't go out of their way to say not both, always assume it's both."
- **Strong qualifiers as red flags**: "solely," "must," "primarily," "most," "every," especially paired with "takes for granted," should almost always eliminate an answer on necessary assumption.
- **Valid conclusion equals must-be-true conclusion**: "for a conclusion to be valid it has to be essentially a must be true statement based off the evidence." This is why he treats must be true and assumption questions as relatives.
- **Two kinds of gap**: either the evidence fails to reach the conclusion, or the premises fail to link to each other internally. Identify which before hunting an answer; if the premise-to-conclusion link is intact, the defect sits upstream.

## Teaching moves

- **Timed silent solve, then recap by interrogation.** Share the question, 90 seconds to two minutes, then ask conclusion first, evidence second, answer choices last. Repeated all session.
- **Ask for the process, not the answer.** "When you're reading for the first time, what was the method of how did you get to diagramming that? What was the process?"
- **Reverse engineer from the conclusion.** "If this conclusion was valid, what would this conclusion need to be valid? And where would it go wrong?" Prephrase before answers, explanations last: "Don't use explanation videos recklessly because they can get in the way of your learning."
- **The basketball coach analogy**, used with Glenn, an ex-D1 player, to surface a missing standard: telling a kid he spends insufficient time on his jump shot invites "how much time should I be spending?" Without a criterion the argument is incomplete. He then extends it to test overreach: if the kid later claimed the coach meant every hour yields 10% improvement, "would you say it's a fair thing for him to assume that you thought?" No. The only fair takeaway is that what he does now is not enough.
- **The LSAT as a pampered child.** "It's almost like you're dealing with a child that just won't listen. And if you didn't technically say that, so I could do that." His younger sibling and the stove handle illustrate why implicit human understanding earns no credit.
- **Human reading over machine reading.** "We're not like LLMs. We're not AIs... we can just read it and understand it from a perspective of nuance understanding with a human mind." Used to explain why the unstated "vote against" kills answer A.
- **Diagramming as scaffold, not substitute.** "Do not take away the humanity element of it... if you just diagram without knowing what you're diagramming it becomes infinitely more difficult." He tells her to convert conditionals into normal English while writing, and reframes her habit as an asset: "the harder skill to develop often is actually the diagramming part, so you have the harder part handled."
- **Praise the instinct, then upgrade it.** "You're going to get the answer right. I already knew you get the answer right." The stated goal is not accuracy but "making it a well-oiled machine," saving mental energy and building confidence.
- **Concede when the student is right.** On her reading of A: "You might be right about that. Actually, the thing is, and you're correct." He then drops the detour.
- **Drop the clock in practice.** "Don't worry about the time, force yourself to see the chains because you want to develop that skill in practice."
- **Recontextualize a score instead of consoling.** 164 on PT 153, one of the two hardest tests, is worth about two more points on a normal test, "like a 170 on most tests."

## Worked examples

- **Must be true, Rosa Luxemburg (level 4).** Every early-20th-century political philosopher who was a socialist or communist was influenced by Rosa Luxemburg; no one influenced by her advocated a totalitarian state. Malaya diagrammed the chain but took it to the answers without prephrasing. Joshua's fix: the repeating concept (influenced by Rosa) is the bridge, so the inference joins the outer ideas, socialist or communist and did not advocate a totalitarian state. She got it right anyway; the lesson was speed and certainty. He also cleared up the embedded "or": one set, and inclusive unless stated otherwise.
- **Must be true, state-owned entity sale (level 5).** Two requirements on a government sale: highest price the open market commands, and citizens holding majority ownership for at least a year after. Which situation must violate one? Both students chose E: heavy purchaser restrictions to preserve majority ownership necessarily depress the price. C was the bait, and Malaya pushed back that being unable to determine majority ownership violates "must ensure." Joshua's resolution: the requirement attaches to ownership after the sale, not to determinability at the sale. Glenn caught C's second trap, "made one of the highest" rather than "the highest."
- **Necessary assumption, party policy inconsistency.** Conclusion: the party's policy is clearly inconsistent. Evidence: they called increased education spending a worthy goal, then said spending should not increase. Malaya eliminated B, C, E immediately and chose D. A was the trap because it introduces "vote against," never stated. "We have to be very careful not to overstep our grounds."
- **Necessary assumption, preventive medicine.** Conclusion: if the goal is more cost-effective medicine, medical schools spend insufficient time teaching preventive medicine. Glenn flagged "cost effective" as the new concept. Joshua built out what the evidence must supply: a standard for sufficient time, plus the current amount. E works because the author must at minimum hold that the current one-tenth is not enough. B ("every hour devoted to preventive medicine reduces medical costs by 10% or more") is too strong and would be sufficient, not necessary. The coach analogy carried the whole discrimination.
- **Necessary assumption, high blood pressure.** Conclusion: some cases of high blood pressure must not be caused by stress. Evidence: some cases are treated effectively with medicine, and any stress-caused illness is treatable only by reducing stress. The assumption is that treatment by medicine and treatment by stress reduction are mutually exclusive, which is never stated. D fails because "some" is not what the conclusion needs; the right answer establishes that the medicine does not itself reduce stress. Joshua's parallel: the only route to distinction in the military is courage, therefore a 20-year top-of-class servicemember is not distinguished. His example of the second gap type, a missing link between premises.
