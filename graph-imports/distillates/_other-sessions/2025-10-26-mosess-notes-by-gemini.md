# Moses Bryant, 2025-10-26

## LSAT concepts
- **Main conclusion** — a conclusion must be an opinion that is explicitly supported by another statement in the stimulus. A bare fact is not a conclusion, and neither is an unsupported opinion.
- **Argument strength / certainty language** — Joshua's "titan terms": *must*, *cannot*, *not necessarily*, *could*. He treats these as the infrastructure of an argument, the words that decide whether a claim is definitive or merely possible.
- **Weaken** — the goal is never to prove the opposite of the conclusion. It is to show the conclusion is not guaranteed. Casting "some shadow of a doubt" is enough against a definitive claim.
- **Flaw** — worked the flaw of taking a figure of speech literally. Joshua deliberately deferred flaw-question practice at this stage in favor of certainty language.
- **Inference / universal reading** — on the LSAT a statement carries universal meaning without exception, unlike everyday conversation. Statements are meant to hold in every circumstance.

## Teaching moves
- Frames the four certainty words as **"titan terms"** and as the **"infrastructure"** of an argument, giving abstract logical operators a physical, load-bearing image.
- Splits the terms into two families with a family metaphor: *must* and *cannot* are the **"parents"**, demanding absolute support (100% and 0% respectively); *could* and *not necessarily* are the **"flexible"** or **"malleable"** ones, indicating possibility.
- Quantifies each term as a percentage range so the student can test claims numerically: *must* = 100%, *cannot* = 0%, *could* = 1 to 100%, *not necessarily* = 0 to 99%. *Not necessarily* is taught explicitly as the inverse of *must*.
- Analogizes the burden on a definitive claim to a legal claim requiring a high level of certainty, which lands with a pre-law student.
- Teaches the **default rule**: absent an explicit possibility indicator, English defaults to certainty. Demonstrates with a mundane example ("Mary and Jane plan to go to the park") to show it is read as a 100% claim.
- Names the payoff of the default rule directly: it is what makes many LSAT conclusions seem **"ridiculous"** and **"absurd"** once read in the extreme sense, and recognizing that absurdity is the key to attacking them.
- Sequencing decision made aloud: certainty versus possibility language comes before flaw questions. "Understanding the language of certainty versus possibility is paramount for approaching arguments."
