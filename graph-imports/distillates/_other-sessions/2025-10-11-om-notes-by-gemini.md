# Om Desai, 2025-10-11

A metacognition session built around one deliverable: Om's personal LSAT "guide," a living template of triggers and checks that he updates every time a question exposes a gap. Joshua worked three LR questions (flaw, principle-except with two speakers, necessary assumption) but the questions were vehicles; the repeated instruction was "add that to your guide."

## LSAT concepts

- **Study means causation.** "When you see a study, what do we expect it to say?" Om answered "hypothesis," which Joshua seized on: "on the LSAT it means a potential cause, a causation. So when we hear that, causation instantly, causation brain should go on your flag." He had Om write the trigger before seeing the question: "I haven't even seen this question yet."
- **"More likely" is the LSAT's favorite causal bait.** "The LSAT's favorite way of baiting you into thinking of causation... something being more likely doesn't necessarily mean it's because of that reason." His example: "If I told you people who study for the LSAT are more likely to do better, your brain will naturally autofill that's just — no, it's because of the study. We don't know that. We have no idea." The distinction he draws: *more likely* is correlational; *makes more likely* is causal.
- **The causal framework.** Once a correlation feeds a causal conclusion, four moves are available: reverse the cause and effect, introduce a third factor, find the cause present without the effect, or the effect present without the cause.
- **Flaw vs weaken.** "Flaw questions don't really require you to find an alternate explanation so much as it's asking you to descriptively describe what happened... you're almost doing an internal critique versus trying to think if I can bring in new things. Weakening questions will ask you to actually come up with them." Flaw answers sound like "this person mistook this XYZ thing for this XYZ thing"; weaken answers illustrate the third cause.
- **The two-part test for a flaw answer choice.** (1) Descriptively accurate: "is that a proper retelling of the events?" (2) Points to a genuine problem: "You can't just say 'the author did a survey.' That's true, we did do a survey, but that's not a problem."
- **Name the fallacy in every answer choice, right or wrong.** "Can I accurately say what fallacy this is even if it's the wrong answer? Because if you know what the fallacy is in the argument, you want to find the corresponding fallacy in the answer choices."
- **Circular reasoning, defined by demonstration.** "If I told you I'm assuming I'm right without trying to prove it. I'm just saying I'm right because I'm right. So, yeah, I'm right. Why? Because I'm right." Formally: assuming the truth of the conclusion in the evidence.
- **The assumption vocabulary is one thing.** "Takes for granted," "assumes," "fails to establish" all "just mean that the author assumed it."
- **An assumption is always about the conclusion, never the evidence.** "The evidence is always true on the LSAT. We don't dispute that. So the issue will never be with assuming the evidence is true. Whenever they're talking about assuming, whatever follows should be a conclusion" — and there must be no evidence given for it.
- **"Without warrant" means without evidence.** Combined with the above: "assumes without warrant that X" requires X to be a conclusion *and* to be unsupported.
- **Two-part answer-choice mapping.** Answer choices usually name a conclusion piece and an evidence piece; check each separately for accurate characterization. "Purports to establish" = the conclusion. "On the basis of" = the evidence.
- **Out of scope is not a valid elimination on flaw questions.** "Does it have to be something that the author spoke about for an answer choice to be correct? No... he didn't talk about it because he didn't think of it. The author's problem is that he should have thought about that."
- **Wrong answers usually fail twice.** "There's multiple problems with every choice that's wrong, often."
- **"Except" means "the least."** "Always replace that with 'the least.' Which one of these does the whatever-you-said-beforehand the least?" On an agree/principle-except, the credited answer "has to violate at least one of those principles."
- **Two-speaker method.** Fully outline speaker one — conclusion, premise, assumption — then use speaker two as a checker: "go line for line, statement by statement. Does he believe the first statement's conclusion is true according to this line? Is he even addressing it? Is he addressing the evidence? Does he say the evidence is true or false, or does he just add his own input?"
- **Necessary assumption runs the other direction.** Om said a choice was wrong because "it doesn't support the conclusion." Joshua corrected the arrow: "Is a necessary assumption supposed to support the conclusion? You want to think about does the conclusion support this idea, not does the idea support the conclusion. If the conclusion is true, this answer choice should also be true."
- **The negation test as Jenga.** "Take away the not and read it straight through." Then: "You take away that wooden piece — would the whole thing fall? If it falls, we did need that piece to be there. If it just buckled a bit but didn't actually fall, that means it wasn't needed. It definitely helped, but it didn't benefit the argument to the extent where without it, it would be destroyed."
- **Rephrase after every big thought.** "Force yourself to rephrase it. It's something students find annoying to do, but... if you rephrase it you'll understand where you made a mistake, especially when you review." Otherwise the review is useless: "I get why I got it wrong, but I kind of don't get why I got it wrong, because you don't have a clear idea of what you thought it was."

## Teaching moves

- **The guide is the product of the session.** Joshua interrupted roughly ten times with a variant of "that's something you want to add to your guide," turning question-level corrections into permanent triggers. He wants it living: "it should be almost in real time being modified as you see certain things that you might fall susceptible to... using the data you see when you get questions wrong to tell you, okay, next time in my guide I should add this component to my toolkit."
- **Preload the check before the content.** The point of the guide is to remove thinking at test time: "you want to have almost all the work pre-done. So you take away the thinking, and then whenever your mind is hitting a bump you can say, okay, maybe I'm missing something here — where do I find an issue — and go through the steps."
- **Demonstrate rather than lecture.** "There's a couple things I want to say, but I want to just show you in practice what I mean. That's more how you like to [learn]. I want to just jump into this versus trying to explain beforehand."
- **Stop the read mid-stimulus.** He halted Om after the first sentence — "approximately 10,000 randomly selected individuals were telephoned" — to extract the study/causation trigger before any content was known.
- **Withhold the term and make him produce it.** "I won't say the phrase, but can you guess what it is based on what we just read?" Om guessed wrong twice, then landed on "more likely." Same pattern for circular reasoning: Om guessed necessary/sufficient, Joshua said "Close," gave the I'm-right-because-I'm-right paraphrase, and Om produced the name. "Yes. Bingo."
- **Ask what he was looking for, not what he chose.** After Om picked an answer: "If you were to take a pause and say, what was I looking for in the answer choices, and what was the thing I found tricky?" The target is the process, not the pick.
- **Refuse to accept process-of-elimination as understanding.** Om admitted "I couldn't really figure out why it was right, but all the other ones didn't make sense." Joshua then walked every wrong choice individually rather than moving on.
- **Disagree openly and then show, not tell.** When Om explained answer A with a plausible-sounding but wrong rationale (a one-year sampling fluke), Joshua said "I actually would disagree with you there. I don't think there's meaning there. I mean, you'll see what I mean here. Tell me if you agree," then rebuilt it from the conclusion.
- **Force randomness to break question-type autopilot.** He had Om open LSAT Demon's smart drilling feature mid-session precisely so a question would arrive unlabeled: "you'll see exactly like, wait, what do I do with this question — versus when we know the question coming is flaw, we kind of have a premade answer." Second reason: "sometimes there's an overlapping issue with flaw and other question types."
- **Name the outlier so it does not distort the template.** On the two-speaker principle question: "This is a bit of a peculiar question. Principle is not often the case for two speakers. It's very rare. I don't know if I've seen a question, principle with two speakers." He still worked it, framed as stamina for randomness.
- **Personify the author to make the critique feel natural.** "Our goal is to critique him. So we want to say, yeah, you should have thought of that, buddy." And on unstated assumptions: "Say it, buddy. If you want to use it, then say it, but don't just think it and not tell us."
- **Route feedback through Google Classroom, not screenshots.** "You send me a screenshot and I have to type a text out to you, and then what am I referring to in the text? It gets way more confusing. Classroom makes it very clear what we're referring to and it's just very polished."
- **Reduce the stakes on the group-class invite.** Offered three times with explicit outs: "It's completely your call... there's no pressure... you can leave and join as you wish."
- **The closing thesis.** "The most important part of your prep is really analyzing the way you think, your thought process, and seeing exactly where the mechanics of your thought process can be improved. That's what will lead you to becoming a master of this LR section, especially, because it's small things."

## Worked examples

- **Flaw: the telephone survey (age and willingness to disclose finances).** ~10,000 randomly selected individuals telephoned about income and savings; the older the person, the more likely they refused to answer; conclusion: this "clearly demonstrates that in general people are more willing when they are younger than when they are older to reveal personal and financial information to strangers over the telephone." Correlation to causation via "more likely."

  Om chose D by elimination. Joshua killed each distractor by named fallacy plus descriptive accuracy: **D** (assumes from the outset what it purports to establish) is circular reasoning, and the author did appeal to evidence, so the description is false. **E** (provides no reason to believe what is true of a group in general is true of all individuals) is part-to-whole *and* factually wrong — "he gave us a reason, it's just maybe a bad one." **C** (assumes without warrant that age is the main determinant of personal income and savings levels) fails twice: the argument is about willingness to answer, not income levels, and "the main" means number one, which was never claimed. **B** (fails to specify the exact number of people telephoned) "is lying to us" — 10,000 was stated — and would not be a flaw anyway.

  Credited answer **A**: no evidence the individuals queried would have responded differently had they been asked the same questions in years prior. Om had dismissed it as out of scope. Joshua's reconstruction is the cause-without-effect move: "if it's the age, then when they were younger they would have given the information. Take away the age factor, we should have the same results. If that's not true, then it's not really the age." He supplied alternate causes: a cohort-wide privacy disposition, geography ("maybe they're calling different countries").

- **Principle / agree-except, two speakers (Vanessa and Joe, pair programming).** Vanessa: all computer code must be written by a pair of programmers at a single workstation, to prevent idiosyncratic code understandable only by the original programmer. Joe: most projects are kept afloat by the best programmers, typically at least a hundred times more productive than the worst; since they generally work best alone, the most productive programmers must be allowed to work by themselves.

  Joshua's prephrase: the credited answer must violate one principle — either it lets someone write code alone (against Vanessa) or it forces one of the best programmers onto a team (against Joe). Om independently produced the second violation before seeing the choices. Credited answer **D**: Yolanda, the most productive programmer on the team, assigned with Mike, also very productive, at the same workstation. Joshua: "this essentially involves putting the best programmer in a team, and what are they thinking? Yolanda needs her space to do her thing."

- **Necessary assumption: street lights.** Nighttime crime rates have risen in several neighborhoods; research shows increased visibility reduces the likelihood of criminal activity; therefore the city can reduce crime by installing more street lights there. Joshua flagged the double causal claim (causation in the evidence *and* in the conclusion) and drove Om to the gap: street lights increase visibility, never stated. "Obviously common sense will tell you that. But did they say it though?"

  Credited answer **A**, given in negated form: it is *not* the case that the neighborhoods are already lit to a point where visibility would not be increased by additional lighting at any hour. Om read it un-negated, restated it as "the neighborhoods already have enough light so there's no point adding any more," and Joshua showed that this is exactly the destruction the test looks for. Om then named his own misconception: "I kept thinking when you remove the not, it has to match the answer choice. That's what I was confused [about]."

## Student state

- Has a working baseline LR process: "logging the stimulus... looking for the conclusion, the evidence, and what the author is trying to do."
- **Eliminates well, understands poorly.** The recurring pattern is arriving at the right or near-right answer by elimination without being able to state why: "it was kind of like a process of elimination... I couldn't really figure out why it was right, but all the other ones didn't make sense." This is the specific weakness the session targeted.
- Strong when reading answer-choice language closely: he correctly rejected the "assumes without warrant... main determinant of personal income and savings levels" choice on precise wording grounds, and Joshua called it: "You're dead on, you're right on the ball there."
- **Dismisses answers as out of scope too readily.** He rejected the credited flaw answer because the years-prior comparison "wasn't talked about." Joshua treated this as a category error to correct permanently, not a one-off.
- **Confused the negation test's mechanics**, believing the un-negated statement had to match the choice rather than testing whether removal collapses the argument.
- **Confused the direction of support in necessary assumption** (idea supports conclusion, rather than conclusion entails idea).
- Recognizes two-speaker structure and its purpose unprompted: "usually these questions are trying to figure out what they agreed with or what they didn't agree with... they're going to agree on one thing but attack something else."
- Uses LSAT Demon; the smart drilling feature was new to the session's workflow.
- Sitting the November test (Joshua's group class was framed as "a group of people that are taking the test as well in November").
