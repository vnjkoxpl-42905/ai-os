# Mounusha Nunna, 2026-01-08

## LSAT concepts

- **Method of reasoning**: the highest-leverage fundamental because its skills bleed into flaw and parallel. The task is to describe how the argument travels from evidence to conclusion.
- **Argument parts**: opposing viewpoint, concession, context/background, premise, intermediate or subconclusion, main conclusion. Distinct from the *nature* of a claim (opinion, generalization, fact, principle).
- **Premise indicators**: "for," "after all," "because," "since." Whatever follows is guaranteed to be a premise, and a premise guarantees an attached conclusion that precedes it.
- **Pivot words**: "but" marks the shift into the author's position.
- **Validity**: "valid means it has to have zero assumptions. It has to be literally impossible for the conclusion to be wrong if the evidence is true." One minor assumption disqualifies it.
- **"Explanation" means causation on the LSAT**, always, 100 percent of the time. Not elaboration, not detail, not "why" in the ordinary sense.
- **"Principle" means a normative rule that applies across multiple scenarios**, a rule of thumb. A rule governing a single situation is just a rule, not a principle.
- **"Defend" means advocating that something is right**, taking a side. Stating what the motivation is does not defend it.
- **"From" and "by" introduce evidence** in answer choices; whatever the phrase attaches to is the conclusion.
- **Causal flaw attacks**: reverse causation (B causes A), a third C factor causing one or both, or pure coincidence.
- **Nuance over black and white**: "not the only thing" is not the same as "not important." Joshua called reading nuance one of the LSAT's most fundamental tested skills.
- **Literal reading**: "they mean things what they say very literally... they're very brutal, extreme with the claims they're making."
- **Sampling / extrapolation**: a general trend claim drawn from a narrow data range.
- **Controlled conditions cancel out confounders**: a variable only threatens a comparison if it affects one group disproportionately.

## Teaching moves

- **Observe first, silently.** "Take on this question as though I'm not here... pretend I'm just a fly on the wall." He watched her full unaided attempt before saying anything, and later told her he had been "tracking you as you're doing that."
- **The travel-destination analogy for method questions.** He asked where she lives (Jersey City), then: getting from Jersey to Canada by driving, flying, or running are different methods, and "the way you get there will give you a very strong inclination as to where those things could go wrong." A plane does not have a fender bender. The point: "you know by the way that the argument is not going to take you to the destination. It's not going to be valid. If it did, then you'd be good, but they won't." So identifying the mode of transport tells you where to look for the break.
- **The building-materials analogy for claim substance.** If he hands you a piece of wood and asks what part of a house it is, "you're probably not going to say it's your pillow." Floors, walls, a bed frame, yes. "The nature of the substance of the claim can give you an indication of what role it plays in regards to the argument parts... we can't really know the function of the claim until we know the substance." He reused it later with feathers.
- **"Think of the author as a narcissist."** His heuristic for opposing viewpoints: whenever the author cites biologists, anthropologists, scientists, or "people," expect hostility. "He's never going to agree with the other side he's bringing up... he'll never make a claim about general groups of people without him just coming and donkey on them, because that's just the author never existing." He then honestly qualified it: "I don't technically know if it's an opposing viewpoint because that has to be him actually posing against it."
- **Pre-read scan for indicator words.** "A very quick 10-second, 5-second scan before you even read the argument, almost like you're doing a preview to a book, like chapters. If I see 'but' and I see 'for,' no matter what, I would have known instantly that this is going to be a premise."
- **De-abstractify the answer choices.** His central claim about difficulty: "the reason why they're so hard, a lot of it is not even the stimulus, it's mostly the answer choices. They're very abstract... turning very vague language and making it very concrete." He calls the two component skills **answer choice extraction** and **keyword mapping**.
- **The two-part answer check.** For each choice: which portion refers to the premise, which to the conclusion; then, is each portion descriptively accurate of that specific part of the argument. He enforced it literally: "if I just looked at the part that's pink in the answer choice and what's pink in the actual argument, it has to be descriptively accurate."
- **Least wrong, not most right.** "It's not enough for the answer choice to just give you the right feel. You have to be able to empirically map it onto the argument... our number one priority is an answer choice that's accurate, but the thing that supersedes that is it can't have any part that's inaccurate. So the least wrong answer, not necessarily the most right answer."
- **The falsification rule.** His stated standing rule for all students: "if you can't explicitly tell me why it's wrong, you're not allowed to disqualify it." He applied it against her own aesthetic objection to "historical fact": "would I say that's a good characterization? Maybe not. But I can't falsify it based off the evidence." He was candid that this is painful: "this is where I've lost probably half my hair," and called D "just a garbage answer choice to be honest."
- **Disqualify, do not qualify.** "You're looking to say why are these answer choices wrong until you get to an answer choice that you can't actually say why it's explicitly wrong. Not implicitly, but explicitly... I would spend 10 or 20 minutes on a question. If I can't say explicitly what's wrong, I'm literally allowed to remove it."
- **Reverse engineering.** For the second question he flipped the order entirely: read all five answer choices *before* the stimulus, and for each ask "what would be needed for this stimulus to make this answer choice correct." This front-loaded the causal requirement in B, the extrapolation structure in C, the presupposition in D, and the causal reading of "explanation" in E.
- **Held her to a term she had just learned.** When she re-defined explanation as "elaboration" on choice E: "I'm going to call you out. Do we remember what does explanation mean?" Then: "it's very tricky because what you're saying is so intuitively true when we use that word." He set the expectation that unlearning takes time: "it will take practice to rewire that."
- **Pushed her to the literal extreme of a claim.** "The cooler the temperature the longer it stays fresh" plus "how about if it was negative a thousand?" He named the social reflex that hides the flaw: "you've been socially conditioned to be a normal person, so you hear that and yeah, I can see why you would get there. But the LSAT means it literally, to the point where until literally the ice age would be the ideal temperature. That's ludicrous."
- **Played devil's advocate against her correct instinct.** She dismissed B because humidity and sunlight are not in the argument. He pushed back: other factors manipulating the outcome would make the results unreliable. She then found the real reason herself, "similar conditions," and he confirmed and generalized it: "whenever there's something that can change the outcome, it's only a problem if it disproportionately affected one of the groups more than the other. If it applies equally to all groups, it cancels out." He flagged this as a recurring trap: "whenever they're making comparable groups, almost always one of the answer choices is going to point towards this."
- **Distinguished descriptive accuracy from relevance.** On D: even if the thermometer assumption were descriptively present, "it has to also pass, this is actually a problem for the argument." An unreliable thermometer would shift all three readings, leaving the trend intact.
- **Named her real bottleneck, then reframed her stated one.** She raised timing. He told her to drop it entirely: "the best way to get faster is just to get better at it... don't focus on getting faster." The bike analogy: thinking "I need to move my feet faster" is worse than grooving the motion until it is reflex, "you won't have to think about, oh, does 'from' actually introduce evidence." Bottom line: "efficiency comes after complete accuracy. Accuracy will lead to efficiency," plus reassurance: "I'm certain time won't be an issue by the time you get to the test day. I'm yet to see it not happen."
- **Named where the score gains live.** "Advanced test takers are not only good at understanding the stimulus. They understand the implications of what the answer choice has to have for it to be correct." He located this as his own breakthrough "between my first and second test," and as the transition "from good to great."
- **Anti-vibes framing.** "Vibes are the enemy for perfection. It gets you to a good point, but then you need to turn that knob up to get precise, repeatable results."

## Worked examples

- **Method of reasoning, realism and photography (correct: D).** Stimulus: people may praise the talent of a painter capable of realistically portraying a scene and dismiss as artistically worthless the efforts of abstract expressionists; but obviously an exact replica of the scene depicted is not the only thing people appreciate in a painting, for otherwise photography would have entirely displaced painting as an art form. Sentence one is an opposing viewpoint/generalization; "but" pivots to the main conclusion; "for" marks the final line as the premise. Invalid, since it assumes photography is inherently realist and that the displacement would already have happened by now. A fails because the premise is not a claim about what most people appreciate (that content sits in the opposing viewpoint, not in the supporting claim). B fails because the premise is not an aesthetic principle, it is a statement of what the world is like, not a normative rule spanning scenarios. C fails on reversal plus the causal reading of "explaining," it makes the historical fact the thing explained rather than the support. D is correct: appealing to a historical fact to support a claim about people's artistic preferences. E fails on "defend," the author states what the motivation is rather than advocating that people are justified.
- **Flaw, fruit storage temperatures (correct: C).** Stimulus: a science class stored one selection of various fruits at 30°C, a similar selection in similar conditions at 20°C, and another at 10°C; because the fruits at 20° stayed fresh longer than those at 30°, and those at 10° stayed fresh longest, the class concluded that the cooler the temperature at which these varieties of fruits are stored, the longer they will stay fresh. Answer choices were analyzed before the stimulus. E eliminated, the argument does offer a temperature-based account. A eliminated, the conclusion is scoped to "these varieties of fruits," so untested fruits are irrelevant (Joshua: "a lot of people would have fallen for that one, you have a very sharp eye"). D eliminated, thermometer unreliability would shift all three readings without disturbing the trend. B eliminated because the fruits were kept in "similar conditions," so humidity and sunlight affect all groups equally and cancel out. C is correct: extrapolating too readily from a narrow range of temperatures (three points) to the entire range, which the conclusion's unbounded comparative form commits it to.
