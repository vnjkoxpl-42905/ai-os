# Fiorella Meyer, 2025-12-22

## LSAT concepts

- **Conditional logic**: after every conditional, state what you know and its contrapositive. "We only know what's being said and the contrapositive, and we all live and die by that." Denying the sufficient tells you nothing.
- **Chaining conditionals**: when an idea repeats, join the links and read off the head-to-tail inference. Morally wrong then offense against humanity, offense against humanity then equally bad, so all morally wrong actions are equally bad.
- **Head and tail get tested, not neighbors**: "they're going to almost never test you on the ideas that are neighboring... that's too obvious. They want to reward your ability to make the inference." The tested link is the start of the chain against the end of it.
- **Trigger discipline**: only the sufficient condition being satisfied yields knowledge. Any answer choice that never pulls the trigger cannot be proven and is dismissed without reading it fully.
- **Must be true vs. could be true**: "could be true is irrelevant to us. What do we know has to be." Conditionals exist on the LSAT to test what can be guaranteed.
- **Flaw**: the most common LR question type. Joshua's framing, it is easier than application: "all you're doing is basically being a referee. You're saying oh, you can't do that... it's way easier to criticize than to actually affirm a position."
- **Part to whole / whole to part**: a trait of a member does not transfer to the collective, or vice versa.
- **Unproven vs. proven false**: "a lack of evidence is not evidence of it lacking." Also, the truth of a claim is independent of the quality of the argument for it.
- **Ad hominem**: attacking the source rather than the claim. Usually a wrong-answer distractor, rarely the credited flaw.
- **Sampling bias / overgeneralization**: extrapolating from a sample to a universal claim. Distinct from part-to-whole.
- **Inappropriate appeal**: to authority or to emotion/fear.
- **Straw man**: attacking an argument the author did not make, usually one that sounds adjacent.
- **Causation**: named as co-backbone of LR with conditionals, deliberately deferred to a dedicated future session.

## Teaching moves

- **Build the house before reading further.** His opening frame: "with every stimulus you really want to take your time in the beginning and build like a house... make the illustration of what I'm reading very tangible in the beginning parts to set the framework for what I'm going to read later." Payoff phrasing he reuses: "that will often lead to running downhill versus running uphill."
- **Read one line, stop, extract.** He interrupted after every sentence to ask what is now known, and warned her he was doing it: "I know I'm stopping you quite a bit here." At each stop: what do we know, what is the contrapositive, is anything repeated.
- **Solve up front to avoid being primed.** "We do not want to wait till the end, because when it's a bunch of information, trying to sort that out is a nightmare, and you're also biased toward what you read... they'll prime you to seeing certain things that are not there."
- **Logic games as the mental model.** "Back to when there used to be this thing, the land before time with the dinosaurs, logic games... you want to upfront solve for the conceivable possible worlds."
- **The gun-trigger metaphor for conditionals.** "If the gun trigger is pulled, we know the outcome, the bullet will execute. But if the trigger never gets pulled, we don't know what happens." He used it to strip three answer choices in seconds: risking one's life, accidentally causing death, a situation involving one person's life, none pull the trigger, none can be engaged with.
- **Also "landmines."** "If that conditional is met, the rest of the chain has to follow, like landmines. They all fall over."
- **Do not read the second half of an answer choice first.** A named discipline he repeated twice: "if you read the second half without prephrasing the first half, you'll look and find the answer. You'll almost justify the answer... we don't want to let them take us on a ride here." Instead, stop at the trigger clause, derive what must follow, then read the rest as a check.
- **Demanded examples, refused definitions.** On the fallacies, he blocked her from looking them up: "I want you to avoid looking at it. Tell me based off your own words." And on why: "it's not about the words, it's more the conceptual theory behind it. That will make it so you'll really stick."
- **Hockey team for part/whole.** One player is a superstar, therefore the team is amazing (part to whole). The Maple Leafs are a phenomenal team, therefore each player is phenomenal (whole to part). He pushed the second further: "there may not even be any individual player that's really good, but they might just be a good team which then they can execute."
- **Made her the subject of the fallacy.** "Can you prove to me right now that you're in the USA?" She said not right now. "Okay, so you're lying to me. I knew it." Then the VPN extension: even a failed proof (an IP address masked by a VPN) does not make the claim false. Then the strongest version: "if I'm a religious person that believes in God, if I can't prove to you that God exists, doesn't mean God doesn't exist. It just means I can't prove it to you, which is a very different claim." Summary line: "the truth or falsity of a claim is independent of what I argue."
- **Politically neutral both ways.** He deliberately built the ad-hominem and unproven examples so either side could be the target: a politician advocating higher taxes who never paid taxes, and "you might say Donald Trump is the best or Donald Trump is the worst... I could argue he's terrible because of his hairstyle."
- **Pizza role play for the sampling/part-whole distinction.** This slice is triangular therefore all pizza slices are triangular (overgeneralization, a claim about other slices). This slice is triangular therefore the pizza it came from is a big triangular pizza (part to whole, a claim about the source). The pizza is circular therefore the slices are circular (whole to part). His test for telling them apart: does the conclusion talk about the source, or about other members of the same class.
- **Ranked the fallacy list rather than teaching all of it.** Conditional and causal are "the biggest chunk of the pie," then unproven-vs-proven-false and part/whole, then sampling. The rest are "scapegoats. They're there to distract you." Bottom line he gave her: "there's only really about four to six ones you really need to know," and the rest matter as recognizable wrong answers, "because ad hominem is kind of too obvious for most LSAT students."
- **Mine the wrong answers.** His stated highest-leverage practice habit: "using the wrong answers to get you to learn the language of the flaw... labeling them is as useful as finding the correct answer. It's one of the most underrated performance boosters." He then ran C, D, and E purely as naming drills.
- **Held the line on descriptive accuracy.** On A: the choice sounded right ("very close in the vibes") but "the answer choice that's correct has to be descriptively accurate." Her argument uses the absence of evidence for the *opposing* view, not the absence of evidence against her own.
- **Named the double jump in the shoe factory argument.** "They can't prove their side doesn't mean their side's wrong. But also, because their side is wrong doesn't mean my side by default is right. That's two problems there."
- **Encouraged open-book practice.** "Don't be scared of using the actual list of flaws and having them side by side. You'll be very quick at finding the flaws once you're able to do that."
- **Light, personal register.** When she agreed that lying is as bad as murder: "Well, I'm scared for your husband. That's scary." On someone who thinks pizza slices are circular: "cut that person off."

## Worked examples

- **Principle/application, morally wrong actions (correct: A, rated level four or five).** Stimulus: to perform an act that is morally wrong is to offend against humanity; all offenses against humanity are equally bad; because murder is morally wrong, it is just as bad to have murdered one person by setting off a bomb as to have murdered a hundred people by setting off that bomb. The chain yields: all morally wrong actions are equally bad. Answer choices about risking one's life to save a hundred people, accidentally causing a death, and a situation involving one person's life were all dismissed without full reading because none engages "morally wrong." A (if lying is morally wrong, telling a lie is as bad as murdering someone) follows exactly. C (if stealing is morally wrong, it is equally important to society to prevent people from...) fails because the stimulus says nothing about importance to society and issues no prescription about what anyone should do.
- **Flaw, shoe factory shelter (correct: B).** Stimulus: a council member recommends the abandoned shoe factory as a municipal emergency shelter; some council members assert the courthouse would be better but have provided no evidence of this; thus the shoe factory would be a better shelter site. First and last lines restate each other, and the sole evidence is the opposition's lack of proof. B correctly describes it: accepting a claim because advocates of an opposing claim have not adequately defended their view. A fails on direction, it describes a lack of evidence *against* the speaker's own view, which is not what she offers. C is ad hominem. D is inappropriate appeal to fear/emotion. E is straw man.
