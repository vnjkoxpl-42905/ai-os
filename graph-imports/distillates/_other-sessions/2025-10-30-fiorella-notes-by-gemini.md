# Fiorella Meyer, 2025-10-30

## LSAT concepts
- **Conditional logic** — the core question is always "what do I know must be true?" given the rules as stated, read as straight as possible. Sufficient is the "if" part, necessary is the "then" part.
- **Sufficient condition** — "one guaranteed way" to reach an outcome, never the only way. If the sufficient condition is absent, the conditional is simply not activated and you know nothing.
- **Necessary condition** — what must be true whenever the trigger fires. Joshua defines it flatly: "necessary just means must."
- **Contrapositive** — flip and negate. The only other statement you can ever deduce from a conditional. Every combination outside the original and its contrapositive is invalid by default.
- **Negation** — denying an idea by adding "not", not taking the logical opposite. "Not hot" is not "cold"; it can be room temperature or anything else outside hot.
- **Inference / chaining** — previewed only: A → B plus B → C gives A → C. Deferred to the next lesson.
- **Flaw** — previewed the sufficient/necessary confusion flaw: treating a requirement as a trigger. "You taking something that's required and assuming that's sufficient."
- **Must be true, necessary assumption, sufficient assumption, parallel reasoning, principle** — named as the question types conditional logic runs through. Joshua's framing: it is easier to say which question types do NOT involve it.

## Teaching moves
- **Door metaphor for the pair**: "the if is the open door. The necessary condition should think of as the closed door." Extends it live: when the door opens, somebody enters and closes it. Then uses the metaphor to break the reversal error, "does that mean that if a door is open that somebody will enter? No."
- **Crime-family metaphor for indicator words**: "they're almost like crime families. You can think of them as having their own codes like kind of like the mafia style." Each group has its own members to memorize.
- **Tone-coding the two word groups**: sufficient indicators (if, when, all, every, any, whoever, each) are "light-hearted", "friendly", "open" words. Necessary indicators (must, necessary, required, depends, needs to, essential, precondition) are "ironclad" words. He is teaching an intuitive feel, not just a list.
- Second framing on the same pair: sufficient conditions are **triggers**, necessary conditions are **requirements**.
- **Train metaphor for the arrow**: "think of it as a train. So that if you jump on this train reading it from left to right, you know what outcome is going to happen." Names it the **"must arrow"** and drills "you only read left to right. You don't read right to left ever."
- **Diagramming justified by a language analogy**: English is "very ineffective to communicate logic" and is "fluffier"; conditional notation is a different language with a different strong suit, like calling French better suited to romance. He deliberately wrote out "United States of America" in full so the contrast with `I → USA` would land visually.
- **"Get dumber"** as an explicit instruction: "it's almost like you want to make get yourself to be dumber... Sometimes I have to tell students to get dumber like do not try to think outside of the box. Try to live within the frameworks of being said." Justified by the trap: answers that "sound right that are so close but not."
- **Rapid-fire drill loop** as the checking mechanism: cycles all four permutations ("If you're in the US, what do you know?" / "If you're not in the US?" / "If you're in Iowa?" / "If you're not in Iowa?") until Fiorella answers correctly without hesitation.
- **Deliberate baiting.** He sets the trap himself and lets her fall in twice on "if you're not in Iowa," then names it warmly rather than correcting coldly: "Oh, that's the Iowa pride right there." Diagnoses it as intuition, not ignorance: "sufficient necessary is very much it plays with your intuition. It tricks you into believing things that look like they're there but not exactly there."
- **Multiple domains for the same structure**, rotated so the form is visible apart from the content: air/breathing, Iowa/USA, law school/LSAT, speeding at 90 mph, hot/cold, rich/poor, money/spending power/happiness. The speeding example is chosen for a pre-law student and does real work: 90 mph guarantees speeding but does not exclude 70 mph also being speeding.
- **Predicts the test's behavior** before she meets it, so wrong answers are pre-labeled: "they're going to bait you in to say answer choice A will say, oh, there was air... so Alex must have been breathing. That's not true."
- **Sets expectations honestly about the homework being dull**: "I'm going to give you a lot of work... It's going to be repetitive. Like it's going to be boring. I'll give you a heads up. It'll be really boring."
- **Automaticity as the standard**, with a times-tables analogy: "you're just doing it so many times where you can't forget it... you do not want to be thinking through it while you're reading a question." Reason given: hesitation causes second-guessing, and "they do it masterfully."
- **Stakes framing to earn the drilling**: "this is like a big big moment for your LSAT journey... it's the part that students struggle with the most. So then it'll pay you in dividends later."
- **Scope discipline.** Repeatedly names what he is deliberately not covering yet (chaining, the two extra word groups) rather than letting the lesson sprawl, and shows a parallel-reasoning question purely as a preview: "we're not going to work through this question, but I'll show you what it would look like."

## Worked examples
- **Air and breathing.** Breathing → air. She diagrammed it herself and produced the contrapositive (no air → not breathing). Joshua then quizzed all four permutations.
- **Iowa and the USA.** The spine of the lesson. In Iowa → in the USA; contrapositive not in the USA → not in Iowa. Used to establish that "in the US" tells you nothing, and that Iowa is a subset of the US.
- **Law school and the LSAT.** In law school → has taken the LSAT. Fiorella correctly identified taking the LSAT as necessary. Asked what follows from having taken the LSAT, she answered "I have nothing," which Joshua confirmed as the point.
- **Speeding.** Driving over 90 mph → speeding. Used to show the rule does not exclude 70 mph also being speeding: "90 mph is one guaranteed way. I'm not saying it's the only way."
- **Embedded sufficient indicator.** "You must be in the US if you are in New York." Taught that the indicator keeps its role regardless of sentence position, so this diagrams identically to "If you're in New York, then you are in the US."
- **A parallel reasoning question** shown as a preview: large amount of money in bank → great spending power → happy. Fiorella produced the contrapositive of the first link, then Joshua chained to money → happy to preview inference building.
- **Flaw preview.** "If you're good at math, then you are good in school. Max was very good in school, so he must therefore be good at math." Named as reversing necessary for sufficient.
- Flash-card drill on notation: representing "if A then B" as A → B, what the arrow signifies, which side is sufficient, which is necessary, and how to form the contrapositive.
