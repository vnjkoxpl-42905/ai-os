# Isela Chavez, 2026-03-30

## LSAT concepts

- **Main conclusion, two-step test.** It must be a statement of the author's opinion, and it must receive explicit support from at least one other statement in the argument. "If one of those two things are missing, it cannot be the main conclusion."
- **A fact can never be a conclusion.** "If it's something that's empirically measurable or verifiable, then it cannot be a conclusion. It has to be a statement of opinion where I'm taking facts and asserting something I believe to be true." Joshua flags this as widely missed: "Something many people for some reason don't understand."
- **"But" is a pivot, not a conclusion marker.** "The word *but* is likely to introduce a conclusion, but not always. It is a good indicator that there's a pivot happening. But a pivot does not always introduce a conclusion."
- **Referential phrasing** as a deliberate obfuscation device: "this," "that," "they" are "secret words that hold onto ideas spoken about earlier, but they can use those words to keep things very muddled." The fix is substitution, in place, as you read: replace "this purpose" with "the purpose of influencing popular opinion." "When you keep it razor sharp, it becomes extremely clear."
- **"Why should I believe this?"** applied after every statement. "If you cannot answer that question with another statement, then you know that's not the main conclusion."
- **Timing is downstream of comprehension.** "It's almost synonymous. It's not a timing issue, it's actually an understanding, a comprehension of what you're doing... Timing will come. The last thing you should worry about is timing. It strictly should be accuracy: if there was zero time on the clock, would I get this question right?"

## Teaching moves

- **The magic number question** as the goal-setting device: "If I could take a piece of paper out and put a magical score where this is the score that you would be able to say I could take this and never do the LSAT again, what would that number on the paper be?"
- **Watch the process, ignore the answer.** "I don't really care if the question's right or wrong. It doesn't really make a difference for me. It's more so for me to watch you run through the process." He does not ask why she chose B before walking the stimulus.
- **Left side of the screen.** "Almost all of the answers are going to be toward the left side of your screen... People like to run to the right side of the screen where the answers are, because they think that's where they're going to find them, but it's never going to be there."
- **A deliberately chosen trap question, then the reveal.** After she falls for the "but," he names the setup: "I don't want to sound evil, but I deliberately chose this question because 7Sage students typically fall for that exact thing. They get trained on these indicator words so heavily that they rely on them, and it'll catch you." He does this to expose the dependency on indicator words rather than to catch her out.
- **Read-and-retell at every statement**, warned in advance: "I'm going to interrupt you quite a bit, so forgive me in advance." Prompt each time: "If I were to ask you to teach me that first statement in a story-like fashion, like you're telling a friend, what did it tell you?"
- **Force the distinction between two near-identical phrasings.** She keeps reading the conclusion as "to influence popular opinion." He will not let it go: "Is there any difference between those two statements?" until she says "they're testing out the potential." Then the payoff: "Testing the potential is not the same thing as doing it. He's essentially dipping his feet in the water. He wants to see what he could do if he wanted to."
- **Quantify the abstract to expose it as fact.** On "sent to too few households": "There's a number, a threshold that has to be met to serve the purpose effectively, whatever that number is. It could be 50, could be 100, could be a thousand. Epsen did not reach that number. So that means it's an empirical measurement." Then he hands her the inference: "What does that tell us about our second line? What can it not be?"
- **The shopping mall analogy** for prephrasing before answer choices: "If you go to the shopping mall without a shopping list and a salesman gets his hands on you, he's going to take you left and right. But if you go into the shopping mall knowing what you want, you can say, sure, I know what I want, it's right in that lane. You don't waste money, you don't waste time." Then: "We did our homework. All of our work is up front."
- **The bicycle analogy** for the timing question: "If somebody's trying to bike fast and they don't know how to bike properly, they'll scratch their legs, because they don't know how to apply the pressure on the pedals at the right timing and momentum. But once you get into a flow and understand what you're doing, it's almost autopilot."

That's going to be a very strong thing for your prep, because a lot of students don't have a good background in reading, and it's hard to teach that on my side, because reading is a lifelong skill." ## Worked examples

- **Epsen campaign ads (main conclusion).** "Normally political candidates send out campaign material in order to influence popular opinion. But the recent ads for Epsen's campaign were sent to too few households to serve this purpose effectively. The ads were evidently sent out to test their potential to influence popular opinion. They covered a wide variety of topics, and Epsen's campaign has been spending heavily on follow-up to gauge their effect on recipients." Path taken: sentence 1 is context, standard politician behavior. Sentence 2, after substituting the referent for "this purpose," reduces to a claim that a numerical threshold was not met, which makes it an empirical fact and therefore disqualified as the conclusion, despite the "but." Sentence 3 is the conclusion: the ads were sent to *test potential*, which is distinct from actually influencing opinion. Sentence 4 supports it: wide topic variety plus heavy follow-up spending is what a trial run looks like. Isela originally answered B; with the conclusion prephrased, she found the correct choice (C) immediately. Joshua called it "obviously a harder question than most."
