# Azaria Lavender, 2025-10-17

## LSAT concepts

- **Argument structure (the six functions)**: every statement in a stimulus is one of premise, main conclusion, intermediate conclusion / subconclusion, background information, opposing viewpoint, or concession. "Every single statement presented to you on the LSAT can fall into one of these six."
- **Argument core**: premise + intermediate conclusion + main conclusion. Background, opposing viewpoint, and concession are "peripheral information," deliberately added to distract.
- **Definition of an argument**: an opinion supported by at least one other statement. "An opinion is just an assertion. For an argument to be an argument, it has to be an opinion that is supported by another statement."
- **Main conclusion as North Star**: "You want to be very very razor sharp with knowing what the conclusion is saying at every level. The nuances, every single comma, every little word."
- **Opposing viewpoint**: another person's view, always introduced to be denied. "The author will never ever bring up somebody else without trying to deny him." Signaled by pivot words: but, yet, however.
- **Concession**: granting the other side points. Requires an opposing viewpoint to exist. Always followed by the author's own argument. Order after the pivot is not fixed: premise then conclusion, or conclusion then premise.
- **Fact vs opinion as a structural filter**: a fact can never be a main conclusion, an intermediate conclusion, or an opposing viewpoint (all of those are conclusions). It can be a premise, background, or rarely a concession. An author's opinion is most likely the conclusion, possibly a premise, never background.
- **Validity / inference**: the evidence must guarantee the conclusion. "They're not saying it's sensible. They're saying it's impossible for the conclusion to be false based solely on the evidence." Test by generating a "what if" that keeps the evidence true and the conclusion false.
- **Role (argument part) questions**: the answer is the relationship between the isolated statement and the main conclusion. Answer choices have a two-part check, one part characterizing the evidence, one characterizing the conclusion, and both must be descriptively accurate.
- **Direct vs indirect support**: direct support means nothing sits between the statement and the conclusion. "Indirect support means there has to be an intermediate conclusion in between." Corollary rule: an intermediate conclusion is always direct support to the main conclusion; a premise can be either.
- **Keyword: "by"**: "By can only ever introduce evidence, either a premise or an intermediate conclusion. It never introduces a conclusion." What precedes *by* is the conclusion; what follows is the evidence.
- **Keyword: illustration = example.** Interchangeable on the LSAT.
- **Referential language**: harder stimuli hide the conclusion behind "this," "they," "that" ("this is mistaken," "they are mistaken"). Rearticulate the full idea being referred to.

## Teaching moves

- **The five-variable model of difficulty** framed the whole session. Joshua told her the LSAC has five levers it uses to make a question harder or easier, structure being the first and ranking the last, and that mastery of the five covers any question. He used this to explain why they would drill only structure today.
- **Gut plus guardrail**: he validated her instinct rather than replacing it. "You're using your gut instinctual feeling to guide you towards the right answer, and that's very good. The problem is the gut has to be reliable. You also need a guardrail," a checklist to fall back on when the answer does not come.
- **Dinosaur fossil analogy** for the argument core: "You want to get past all the dirt, brush off the bones, and say what piece of this fits into the T-Rex. This is his arm, this is his torso." He returned to it repeatedly ("we're on a manhunt," "we're trying to get through the dirt").
- **Body-parts analogy** for core vs peripheral: you cannot live without lungs, you can live without a pinky. Background, opposing viewpoints, and concessions are the pinky.
- **LeBron vs Jordan** to teach concession: "You give him his flowers, then you come back and push back." He used it to show both orderings after the pivot word.
- **"Coloring"**, his own named technique: read, rephrase, then color the line in with a concrete personal illustration. He modeled it on the drunk driving premise by inventing Sally leaving a bar. "Turn it into the language of Azaria versus the LSAT language." Diagnostic value attached: "If you can't do it, that means there's a disconnect."
- **Praised abstraction over recall.** When she summarized the evidence as "the definition of being morally responsible and also the example about driving," he stopped to name it: "You see how you did that perfectly? You didn't tell me oh the drunk driving, what was the word after that. People do that often. You generalized the content into what it is."
- **Mechanize the eye movement**: underline the isolated statement in the stimulus, highlight the conclusion, "let your eyes dart back and forth." Rationale given in cognitive-load terms: "Think of it like tabs on a browser. You don't want to activate those, it's taking mental energy."
- **Least wrong, not most right.** The session's sharpest reframe: "The LSAT is the opposite. You want to look for the answer that gives you legal deniability. It's not about looking for the answer that's correct, it's the answer that's least wrong, least vulnerable. It's a different framework." Applied by attacking each choice for a single falsifiable word rather than ranking them by appeal.
- **One wrong word kills the choice.** On seeing "solely" absent from the stimulus: "So instantly dismiss it. It cannot have anything wrong with it." He had her verify word by word out loud, choice by choice.
- **The bait is the attractive answer.** He predicted that "is offered as an illustration" would pull her and used C and B to show two near-misses that mischaracterized the conclusion.
- **Teach with hard questions first**: "I like to use the hardest questions, because the only difference between them and the easy ones is that they rank up the difficulty of one of those five components. Easier questions come way easier after that point."
- **Diagnosed her existing process by asking for it.** She said she goes to the answer choices and picks what "sounds like best will go with the argument." He did not correct it on the spot: "Which is fair, and we'll adjust that for sure, but just to get an idea."
- **Let her win the argument.** On the indirect-support choice she objected "but the underlined statement is the intermediate conclusion, so wouldn't that be wrong?" He conceded immediately: "I agree. I think you're smart. Exactly."
- **Review discipline over volume.** "The problem is people think when they get a question wrong, oh I almost got it. You didn't almost get it. You saw the wrong answer and you chose it was right." He set an explicit accountability hook: "If I ever see you get a question that says direct versus indirect support and you don't tell me what it is, I'm going to be mad." And: "It's not just doing sections and practice tests. That's great, but it's not going to give you that insight."

## Worked examples

- **Moral vs legal responsibility, role question.** Stimulus: penalties for drunk driving are more severe when the driver injures someone; moral responsibility depends solely on intentions and not results; therefore legal responsibility, depending in at least some cases on factors other than intentions, is different from moral responsibility. Isolated statement: the drunk driving premise. Joshua walked all five choices. (A) "based solely upon features of the action" contains *solely*, not in the conclusion, dismissed. (B) "criteria of legal responsibility include but are not the same as those for moral responsibility" mischaracterizes, the argument never says legal criteria include moral ones. (C) the attractive trap, "people may be held morally responsible for an action for which they're not legally responsible" is not the stated conclusion. (D) correct, "offered in support of the claim that legal responsibility depends in at least some cases on factors other than the agent's intentions." (E) again imports *solely*. Azaria initially chose C.
- **Cell phones on airplanes, role question.** Main conclusion: recent proposals to allow cell phone use on airplanes are ill advised. Isolated statement: cell phone use would be far more upsetting on airplanes than on buses and trains. Azaria first called it a premise, then self-corrected to a subconclusion; Joshua confirmed, noting the following two sentences (planes are packed, passengers cannot move away) support it. Distractors covered: one framing it as an opposing viewpoint, and one framing it as a premise giving *indirect* support to the main conclusion, which fails because the statement is itself the intermediate conclusion. Correct answer: a conclusion for which support is provided, used in turn to directly support the main conclusion. Azaria was stuck between C and D and chose D, correctly. Joshua: "This is actually a very tricky question, harder than even the last one."
