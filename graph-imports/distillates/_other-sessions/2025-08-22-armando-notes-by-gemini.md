# Armando Vidaña, 2025-08-22

## LSAT concepts

- **Role (argument part)**: the session's sole question type, worked at the highest difficulty tier. Joshua's case for over-training on it: "You're bringing a bazooka to a gunfight. You'll be super overprepared for other questions."
- **Main conclusion vs premise vs subsidiary conclusion**: the recurring discrimination Armando is drilling.
- **"And" as a premise-conjunction signal**: two ideas joined by "and" are normally two independent premises supporting the conclusion, not one supporting the other. Joshua treats it as near-automatic, then this session found the exception.
- **The "therefore" test**: place one line, then "therefore," then the other, and check whether it reads. Useful, but explicitly not decisive here.
- **Concession**: an opening acknowledgement of the other side before the author's own claim ("Although allowing coal mining would create new jobs...").
- **Opposing viewpoint**: "Every time we see an opposing viewpoint, we know automatically the author is opposing it."
- **Falsifiability as an answer-selection framework**: on brutal questions, ask which answer cannot be pointed at and proven wrong, rather than which is best described. "You can't say it's wrong. You can just say it's a terrible answer."
- **"Direct support"**: a load-bearing qualifier. If a premise reaches the conclusion only through another premise, or only in conjunction with one, "direct" fails.
- **Refuting the outcome vs refuting the motivation**: disputing that a tactic achieves its stated aim is not disputing that the aim is genuinely held.
- **Justification**: the reason an actor offers for a practice, not necessarily a premise in the author's own argument.
- **Example vs generalization**: an example is a particular instance illustrating a general statement, and "an example will never be a conclusion." A generalization summarizes many instances. "The example is like a seedling of the generalization."
- **Explain equals cause, phenomenon equals effect**: a hard substitution rule Joshua gave for parsing answer choices. "Replace the word explain with cause. This strictly means cause."

## Teaching moves

- **Word substitution rules delivered as hard equivalences.** The "explain = cause, phenomenon = effect" translation was the session's highest-value single item, and Armando immediately wrote it down. Joshua then made him apply it: an answer describing the sentence as an example of a phenomenon the argument seeks to explain requires the argument to be proposing a cause for an agreed effect, which this argument is not doing, since it disputes the effect itself.
- **Reconstructing the world in which a wrong answer would be right.** Asked how answer A could ever have been correct, Joshua built the counterfactual stimulus: philosophers and the author would both have to agree emotions are nonrational and disagree only about the cause. He then named the technique as a study method: "That's a great question to ask. You have to reconstruct the evidence and the conclusion, so that next time you see it, your built-in pattern recognition kicks in. That forces you to get in the mind of the LSAT writer."
- **De-obfuscating LSAC's phrasing before analyzing it.** On "emotion is not nonrational": "Let's reword that, because they wrote it ridiculously. They're obfuscating here." Armando converts it to "emotions are rational" and the argument becomes tractable.
- **Refusing to defend an indefensible answer key.** On the coal mining question, Joshua agreed with Armando that the credited answer is bad, worked visibly at rescuing it, and stopped: "I really don't like this answer. I don't know how they justify it. It's just not correct. Even as I say this, I'm kind of bullshitting you." He then constructed the only charitable reading available, that many local businesses depending on natural beauty supports the closure premise, which in turn supports the conclusion, and labeled it as forcing.
- **Updating his own rule in front of the student.** "It's almost like I turn my brain off because I saw the 'and.' This is probably the first time I've ever seen it not be that way. I probably have to correct that now, because I know there is an exception I've never seen before."
- **Permission to lose specific questions.** "If you get this question wrong on a test, you have to be okay with that. You can still get a 180 technically if you get a question like this wrong." Paired with the constraint: the goal is one mistake per section, two maximum, which lands in the high 170s. "There are certain questions where you can forgive, but you can't forgive the questions you have to get."
- **Reframing a miss as non-diagnostic.** "This is not a misunderstanding, not a knowledge gap. This is just when the LSAT gets quirky. One in every three tests will have a weird question." Armando's relief was explicit: "I'm glad I wasn't gaslit."
- **Withholding the answer and forcing comparison instead.** On the journalists question: "Don't try to force yourself to see if it's correct or not. We're comparing them against each other. Let's go to A. Do we see anything problematic with A?"
- **Escalating analogies to isolate motivation from outcome.** Starting mild (using MCAT books to study for the LSAT, which fails without implying you were not really trying), then a parent raising kids in a cave, then deliberately uncomfortable ones, Trump's stated motives versus his diagnosis, and Hitler, each flagged as dicey but used to force the split. "The motivation is not the problem. It's the outcome they're expecting where you disagree." He reached for "the road to hell is paved with good intentions" and could not retrieve it, saying so.
- **Naming the bait.** "A is really for the person trying to rush through it. The bait is right in front of you, and it's the first option too, so it's the first one that will stick."
- **Praising the question rather than the student.** Repeatedly: "This is a good question because it's forcing you to pick hairs of specifically what they're addressing. When I see this, I see a high level of engagement."
- **Contrasting role questions with everything else** to justify the training emphasis: "When you're doing a weaken question, you're just looking for an answer that points in that direction. This asks you to articulate precisely what the author is thinking."
- **The barbell curl analogy for study quality over volume.** "If you do barbell curls with terrible form you'll do better than someone who doesn't at all. But the person who focuses on the form, the full range of motion, concentric and eccentric, gets the maximum value. Doing one good set is better than three half-assed sets. Doing less is sometimes doing more."
- **The ankle weights image for the payoff**: after finishing level five drills, timed sections will feel "like taking ankle weights off after running with them on."
- **Skepticism about the platform's difficulty ratings.** "7Sage is really ruthless with their difficulty ranking. I'll see level three questions that feel like level five." He reclassified both hard questions in this session as level five.

## Worked examples

- **PT53, S3, Q20, coal mining** (role). Concession, allowing coal mining would create new jobs. Main conclusion, if permitted, total jobs in the region will decrease. Two premises joined by "and": many local businesses depend on the region's natural beauty, and heavy industrial coal mining activity would force most of them to close. Claim in question is the first premise. Armando chose B (direct support / direct evidence for the conclusion); credited answer is A, treating the closure premise as an intermediate conclusion which the natural-beauty claim supports. Both agreed A is a poor characterization but is unfalsifiable, while B is defeated by the word "direct." Armando's own proposed correct phrasing, which Joshua endorsed as what he would have expected: a piece of evidence working in conjunction with another piece of evidence to support the conclusion.
- **PT56, S4, Q9, journalists and private lives** (role). Claim in question: journalists often claim their investigation of political leaders' private lives is an effort to improve society by forcing the powerful to conform to the same standards as the less powerful. Main conclusion: in reality the tactic is detrimental to society. Premises: it makes public figures more concerned with appearances, and makes everyone else cynical about their leaders. Correct answer B, it mentions a justification sometimes offered for a practice the argument concludes has undesirable consequences. A rejected on close reading, the author does not attempt to refute the claim, because the claim is about the journalists' motivation and the author disputes only whether the tactic produces the intended outcome. Armando got there himself: "He's attacking the tactic, not necessarily the claim." C rejected because "cited as evidence" fits the second half of the underlined sentence, not the claim in question. D and E eliminated quickly, E on "assumed by everyone." Joshua unpacked "justification" concretely: the practice is the investigating, the justification is the stated aim of helping everybody by making sure no one lives a double life.
- **PT57, S3, Q22, philosopher on emotions** (role). Rewritten conclusion: emotions are rational. Premises: they only seem nonrational because language lacks the ability to convey adequate conceptions of emotion, and the words we use for emotions name only very general kinds of inner experience. Claim in question, "there is no language for describing just how one joy differs from another," introduced by "thus, for example." Armando initially called it a subsidiary conclusion, which Joshua tested by asking what supports it. Resolution: the "thus" is a decoy, "for example" makes it an illustration of the preceding premise, and an example cannot be a conclusion. Armando chose A (example of a phenomenon the argument seeks to explain), which fails because the argument disputes the phenomenon rather than proposing a cause for an agreed one. B rejected as a concession, which it is not.
- **Armando's edge-case question**, taken seriously: can an example ever be a subsidiary conclusion if it follows from two premises jointly? Joshua's answer: not the example itself, but a characterization of an example can be. "If I said this example is wrong, that could be a subsidiary conclusion. The example itself isn't. It's the characterization of it being wrong or right."
