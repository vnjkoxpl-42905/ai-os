# Andrew Randolph, 2026-04-05

Second session, heavy on theory by design. Joshua's fullest statement of the validity frame and the four buckets.

## LSAT concepts

- **Every LSAT argument is invalid.** The premise of the whole test. "Forgive me in advance, but none of the arguments are valid. Not even one." The stimulus always claims its conclusion follows with certainty, and it never does.
- **Validity is binary.** "99.99 percent valid means 0 percent. It's one or the other." You are checking structure, not truth: "Think of it less from the perspective of a social science student analyzing whether the information is reliable or true. You're looking to be more like a structural engineer. Does the structure of the statements guarantee one truth from another?"
- **The winning move is one counterexample.** "If I can point to one potential conceivable situation where the evidence could be true but the conclusion has a chance of being wrong, then my conclusion is destroyed. That's the name of the game." Joshua puts the value at "almost 90 percent of your questions."
- **A valid conclusion must be a restatement of the evidence.** "The land of validity lives upon the intersection of the premises. The moment I add one piece of information that's not verbatim, that's where you want to inspect every word and take it hostage."
- **Every word counts, especially in the conclusion.** Demonstrated with "from" versus "in": Andrew is from California, California is in the USA, therefore Andrew is in the USA. Invalid, because being from a place does not guarantee being in it.
- **Two-step conclusion test.** It must be the author's opinion, not a statement of fact, and it must be explicitly supported by at least one other statement.
- **Fact versus opinion, precisely.** A fact is descriptive, empirical, measurable: "Russia is the largest country by land mass in the world." Not a fact: "better," "should." "Should indicates some sort of subjective interpretation." Ordinary speech misuses "fact" ("Trump is the best president we've ever had, and that's a fact"), but the LSAT uses the technical sense.
- **Facts can only ever be evidence, but not all evidence is fact.** "If I have a factual statement, it can only be admitted as evidence. I can't use it as a conclusion." An opinion can support another opinion.
- **The is/ought gap** as a named fallacy the LSAT exploits: "They're going to go from saying bigger to better. We can't take a fact, which is something describing the world as it is, and make a statement about how the world ought to be. Those are two different claims."
- **"Why should I believe this?"** applied to every statement as you read, not at the end. The statement that gets an answer is the conclusion; the answer is the evidence.
- **Main conclusion versus conclusion.** Every conclusion needs opinion plus support; the *main* conclusion is additionally "the statement that is not supporting any other statement. It's the end of the tunnel."
- **Intermediate / secondary / subsidiary conclusion**: receives support from a premise and gives support to the main conclusion. "It's the middle of the road." Test which of two conclusions is main by running "why should I believe this?" both directions and hearing which sounds coherent.
- **Hard questions add volume, not new parts.** "They're just adding more of it. The easiest questions and the hardest questions are the same." Two intermediate conclusions is possible and "ruthless," but no new components exist at level five.
- **LR and RC are the same skill.** "Fundamentally the same. It's just more information, so it seems like it's hiding the conclusion better at times." What you are looking for never changes: evidence and conclusion.
- **The four buckets**: main conclusion, support (premises and intermediate conclusions), opposing (rebuttal or concession), neutral (background). "If I can't identify which of these four it is, I don't understand something."
- **Every statement serves the conclusion.** "The main conclusion is the north star. Every statement in the argument is there to serve one purpose, even if indirectly, which is to deliver the main conclusion. They're not independent statements. Even if they're proposing another side, even that side is ultimately being used to support their side."
- **Concession**: acknowledging the other side has a point, done for political-correctness or framing. It is a subtype of opposing and "a more nuanced, sneakier way they can do this," because it can trick you into thinking the author agrees.
- **Attributed views are always attacked.** "Every single time they bring up another point of view, without exception, they're going to disagree with it. Mr. LSAT is always there to disagree. That's the only reason he brings up anybody else."
- **Neutral / background**: defines terms, gives context, explains why the topic is relevant. Must be separated out so it does not get mistaken for premise or conclusion.
- **Premise indicators, FABS**: for, after all, because, since. Learned from a former student. Whatever follows is a premise, guaranteed, "a quirk of the English language." They double as conclusion indicators because a premise must have a conclusion attached, usually immediately before.
- **Conclusion indicators**: therefore, hence, so. Weaker than FABS: "a little more flimsy," and may flag an intermediate conclusion rather than the main one.
- **Concession indicators**: although, while, despite, granted.
- **"But" and "however"** signal the pivot to the author's side: "where there's smoke there's fire. Whatever follows is going to be the conclusion, or the premise that leads to the conclusion right after."
- **"And" joining two factual statements means two separate premises**, conjoined, serving one function. "*And* can't mean your conclusion."
- **Conclusion placement for main conclusion and role questions**: never expect it last. "They know that everybody thinks it's going to be the last sentence." Expect the first sentence, or the second in a rebuttal structure. "90 percent chance it won't be the last sentence."

## Teaching moves

- **Logical hostility as a reading stance.** "We're reading every argument with hostility, because you know they're trying to pull something fast on you." And: "You want to read it from the first word. Why is this wrong? Why is this wrong? You're not reading just to understand."
- **Absence of a flaw means you misread.** "If you don't see the problem, that means you didn't read something. There's something you didn't read that, if you had read it and understood it completely, would have solved the problem."
- **Don't let the LSAT drive.** "If you let him take the driver's seat and you take the passenger seat, you're going to end up in a place you don't want to be."
- **Say the counterexample search out loud, as a mantra.** "Given that Andrew is from California, how can I argue that he is not in the USA? When you're hearing it out loud, sometimes seeing it on paper might not be as obvious as hearing it."
- **The California example built from the student's own location**, with a social caveat that lands the point better than the logic alone: "Don't be a weirdo that says you didn't say *in*, you said *from*. Don't do that in real life. But in the context of the LSAT, you're that kind of guy."
- **Arguments written out as P1, P2, conclusion under a line.** "Arguments are literally almost a verbal expression of math."
- **The LeBron and Kyrie analogy** for intermediate conclusions: the supporting cast supports Kyrie, and Kyrie supports LeBron. Same DNA, one on top of the tower. He checks first whether Andrew follows sports, gets no, and uses it anyway with hedging.
- **The dirty dishes analogy** for concession versus rebuttal, drawn from his own past relationship. Aggressive: "I told you for the last time, don't do this, or I'm leaving." Conceding: "Listen babe, I know you work super hard, you're the man of the house, X, Y, Z, but you really need to put the dishes away." Stoke the ego a little, then push. "One is just saying it straight up, one is giving them their flowers then going forward."
- **The narcissist framing for the LSAT author**: "Think of him as a narcissist. You only bring somebody else up to show why they're wrong, not to actually assess whether what they're saying is true. He's made up his mind."
- **Water, ice, and gas** for LR versus RC: "They're all the same substance, just in different forms. H2O is H2O. They might look different at the superficial level, but at the end of the day it's H2O." Andrew supplies his own framing first, algebra problem versus word problem, and Joshua takes it: "Very well put actually. It's exactly what it is."
- **The lawyer-to-client translation standard** for rephrasing: "You don't tell the client the legal technical background. He's not here to hear about the history of precedent case law. Your job is to synthesize the legalese for the person you're talking to, who might be a janitor. He's going to ask, am I going to jail?"
- **Warn against synonym hunting.** "Sometimes people turn into synonym hunters. They try to translate every word into a synonym. That's not your goal. Your goal is not to find the equivalent words, it's to understand the essence of every statement." Sometimes your wording should be better than theirs.
- **Adopt and amplify the student's own device.** Andrew mentions he tries to explain things as if to his daughter. Joshua immediately promotes it to a standard: "That's a phenomenal framework. If you can't do that, then there's a problem. It means there's something that's not as obvious to you as it should be."
- **Have the student narrate their method, not just their answer.** After Andrew answers, Joshua asks him to walk through the exact methodology he used before confirming anything, and withholds the fact that Andrew was correct until the end of the walkthrough.
- **Indicator scan before reading.** "It's often useful to do a quick scan of the stimulus before you read it. You basically see the chapters to a book prior to reading the book."
- **The three-step role-question procedure**, given as a standing process: (1) find and highlight the conclusion, expecting it first or second, never last; (2) underline the target statement in the question stem, then find and underline the same words in the stimulus; (3) ask what the relationship is between the underlined part and the conclusion, or whether it is the conclusion itself. Then run the four buckets as yes/no questions.
- **Elimination by bucket to save reading.** "When we're looking through the answers, we don't have to waste our time reading everything. If it's not a supporting statement, then the answer that says main conclusion, I don't have to read that."
- **Isolation over holistic judgment.** "When you're trying to understand the role of a statement in isolation, it becomes much muddier. Versus when it's categorized and you can surgically figure it out."
- **Names the format up front and apologizes for it**: "Quite a bit of lecturing today. Usually it won't be as much, it'll be more engaging with the actual questions. But for the first session I like to get these fundamental things clarified, so when I say them in the future you know what I mean."
- **Frames the timeline honestly**: "We don't have time to mess around, especially because we have high ambitions. It requires us to be locked in, but we're in a good amount of time."

## Worked examples

- **Silk Road / migration (argument part, role).** Stimulus: it would not be surprising to discover the trade routes between China and the West were open many centuries, even millennia, earlier than 200 BC; what made the Great Silk Road attractive was level terrain, easily traversible mountain passes and desert oases; after all, migration from Africa and the Middle East to China occurred at least one million years ago. Structure walked: sentence 1 is the main conclusion; "after all" flags the migration claim as a premise; "and" joining the terrain features marks two conjoined premises; "contrary to what is currently believed" flags an opposing view. Question asked about the migration statement. Applying the three-step process: highlight the conclusion, underline the migration statement in stem and stimulus, ask the relationship. Andrew: "That one is supporting the original sentence." Correct answer C, "offered as evidence in support of the claim that the trade routes between China and the West could have been established much earlier."
- **California (illustrative, not a real LSAT item).** P1: Andrew is from California. P2: California is in the USA. Conclusion: Andrew is in the USA. Andrew initially judged it valid. The gap is "from" versus "in." Used to establish that every conclusion smuggles in something not stated.
- **Canada (illustrative).** "Canada is far superior to the US" plus "therefore you should move to Canada" used to demonstrate testing which of two conclusions is main. Run "why should I believe this?" in both directions; only one ordering sounds coherent.
