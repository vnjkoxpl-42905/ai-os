# Sabrina Egal, 2026-02-21

## LSAT concepts
- **Flaw**: both questions worked, and the session's declared focus area alongside necessary assumption.
- **Sufficient vs. necessary**: the flaw in Q1. Two directions of the error: asserting the necessary happened therefore the sufficient happened, or the sufficient did not happen therefore the necessary did not. Joshua calls it the most popular flaw on the LSAT, with "at least one" per section.
- **Conditional reasoning**: "only" and "if" as indicators; also chained conditionals.
- **Causal reasoning**: a claim can be both conditional and causal. "As long as one thing leads to another thing, you know that is by definition causal."
- **Correlation vs. causation**: "a correlation is a descriptive claim saying what is happening. A causation is telling you why it's happening."
- **Anti-causation as its own flaw**: claiming something will NOT cause an effect is as unsupported as claiming it will.
- **Term shift / equivocation**: government to politicians, attitude to conviction, important problems to most important problems.
- **Strength of language**: "entirely" in a conclusion means literally 100%, which requires 100%-strength evidence.
- **Necessary assumption**: signaled by "takes for granted"; correct answers use weak, unrestrictive language.
- **Answer choice styles for flaw**: objection ("overlooks the possibility," "fails to address the possibility," "fails to consider"), assumption ("takes for granted"), and descriptive (which usually names a fallacy).
- **Unproven vs. proven fallacy**: treating evidence that fails to establish a claim as if it established the opposite.

## Teaching moves
- **The two-step reflex**: step one identify the question type, step two "reflexively, you want to almost make it a habit" think about what traits to expect. For flaw: expect a named fallacy (at least 50% of the time), otherwise separate evidence from conclusion.
- **The highlight-and-bounce technique**: highlight or underline the conclusion, highlight the premise, "have your eyes bounce back and forth and say what words are different. That can often get you to the right answer."
- **Underline every foreign concept in the conclusion.** The core drill of the session: treat evidence and conclusion as "two separate, completely separate entities" and mark every word or phrase that appears in the conclusion but not the evidence. "If there's anything different, that by definition is going to be a flaw."
- **Pause after partial thoughts, not full sentences**: "even if it's like the first three or four words, force yourself to check what you just read... You would think it's going to hold you back, but it's only two or three seconds. Once you're in the zone to understand the context, the rest of the stimulus will be way faster. But when you try to process all of it at once, your brain will just go, what did I just read? Let me read it again."
- **Withholds the label to make her find it**: "I'm trying to avoid saying it, trying to say it without saying it, but there's something else there." Lets her arrive at "large number of people," then at causation.
- **Read conditionals backward from the "if"**: "we want to read from the 'if' and then read it backwards, because the conditional is telling you if this happens then the other thing will happen as said beforehand."
- **The study-hard analogy for sufficient/necessary**, used twice. "If you work hard, you'll get a high LSAT score. So if you don't work hard, what do I know?" When she answers "we don't know," he praises the resistance: "Stick to your guns. I'm trying to beat you again... you'll naturally think, oh that means you won't do well. We don't fall for that." Then the memorable one-liner: "one guaranteed way to get to an outcome doesn't mean it's the only way. You can get a high score by cheating."
- **The framework answer to feeling overwhelmed.** She says flaws are hard because "there's a lot of possibilities and it's hard to think about all of them." He: "you want to give yourself a framework, because if it's a bunch of information you're trying to sort through, it can feel overwhelming... because we know there's a conditional, instantly you say, I see a conditional indicator, what's sufficient, what's necessary, be on the lookout for the switch around. It cuts out a bunch of worlds that can potentially be true and makes it more narrow."
- **Causation-word intuition through physics**: "think of causation as active words, words that have force, words that have movement to them. If I hear 'make,' there's a component of movement. If I say 'are,' that's correlation, that just sounds flat, this is happening, we don't know why."
- **"Because" contains "cause"**: "think about the word cause is in the word because. Because means why it's happening."
- **Names the human default as the trap**: "every human's natural intuition is to say one thing is leading to the other. Everything happens for a reason." And on "more likely": "it's one of their favorite tricks, it gets so many students."
- **Argue the other side.** Standing method he adds to her approach: "when you go to the conclusion, say okay, what's the other side, and let's see if the evidence could ever show to that other side. Then you start attacking it." Practiced live on the bike-lane question until she produced alternative explanations herself.
- **Two parallel arguments for the bike-lane structure**: "you'd only install speed bumps on a street if that street already had a problem with people speeding." And "stats reveal that more healthcare dollars are spent on people who suffer from health conditions, so spending money on healthcare is unlikely to reduce your suffering." His name for the error: "they're putting the cart before the horse."
- **Answer-choice phrase decoding as a mechanical procedure**: on seeing "overlooks the possibility," mentally blur out the phrase, then ask only "if whatever follows that is true, what would that mean for our conclusion? Would that be an objection?" Notes "fails to address the possibility" is interchangeable.
- **Descriptive answer choices always have two parts**, one describing the evidence and one describing the conclusion. Each half must map onto the actual argument, verified as "a mapping exercise."
- **Explains the goal as removing the intermediate phase**: "you shouldn't be thinking about what is it even saying in the first place. You want to get rid of that intermediate phase where you're going, what does it even really mean, while trying to see if it's right or wrong. It's going to cost you a lot of mental energy and a lot of questions."
- **Reframes forgetting as normal and cheap to recover.** She says she loses old material as she learns new material. He: "the LSAT trip is never going to be linear. You fall a little bit, then get back up, then fall again. But every time you fall back it's 10 times easier. It's almost like one, two, two, one, three, one, three, five." And: "you're not relearning it. It's pattern recognition. You're just re-waking those machines up."
- Diagnoses from her records rather than from feel: notes that necessary assumption "was your bag, low key" but the last two sections "killed you, it was kind of out of your character," and that her wrong answers are "largely due to causation/correlation."

## Worked examples
- **Q1, voter turnout (flaw).** People become unenthusiastic about voting if they believe that important problems can be addressed only by large numbers of people drastically changing their attitudes, and that such attitudinal changes generally do not result from government action. The decreasing voter turnout is thus entirely due to a growing conviction that politicians cannot solve the most important problems. Flaws catalogued: government swapped for politicians; attitude swapped for conviction; important problems narrowed to most important problems; "entirely" claiming 100% causation; and the core error, treating the necessary condition (decreased turnout) occurring as proof the sufficient condition (that belief) occurred. Correct diagnosis: sufficient vs. necessary.
- **Q2, bicycle lanes (flaw).** Statistics reveal that more collisions between bicycles and motor vehicles occur on roads having specially marked bicycle lanes than on roads without them. Hence adding such lanes to existing roads is unlikely to enhance the safety of bicyclists. Path taken: "statistics" flags causal reasoning; "more" in the evidence is correlation; "enhance" in the conclusion is causal; and the conclusion is an anti-causal claim, which is equally unsupported. Alternative explanations generated: bike lanes are installed on busy roads where collisions were already more likely, so the correlation reflects where lanes get placed, not their effect. Answer choices worked: A (overlooks the possibility, injuries as serious on average) rejected because seriousness is not the issue; B correct, more bicyclists ride on roads with lanes than without, which supplies the alternative explanation; C rejected as a necessary assumption answer whose content was too strong because of "any," meaning all; E rejected as the unproven-vs-proven fallacy, since the author is not using the failed evidence to prove the opposite.
