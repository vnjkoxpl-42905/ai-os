# Giselle Montes Perez, 2026-02-12

## LSAT concepts
- **Main conclusion**: the whole demo question. Defined by a two-step test, not by summary.
- **The two-step conclusion test**: (1) it must be a statement of the author's opinion, (2) it must receive explicit support from another statement in the argument. Missing either disqualifies it.
- **Fact vs. opinion**: "Facts can't be conclusions. They can only be opinion statements." Descriptive claims, even ones dressed in evaluative-sounding words like "effectively," are facts.
- **Referential phrases**: "this," "they," "their" pointing back at earlier ideas. The LSAT layers them until you lose track of the referent.
- **Active reading, defined operationally**: hypothesize where the stimulus is going, then confirm or falsify with the next piece of information.
- **"Normally" as a setup word**: signals the status quo, and predicts a departure from it is coming.

## Teaching moves
- **Operationalizes a piece of advice most students only hear as a slogan.** "People say actively read. What does that mean? What that really means is to hypothesize where the stimulus is going to go and confirm that with new information. So, hypothesize-validation framework."
- **Makes being wrong safe and useful**: "It's good if it's wrong. You only know what's wrong if you know what's actually right."
- **Reads the first word of the stimulus as data.** Stops at "Normally" and asks what she expects to follow, drawing the prediction out of her before continuing.
- **Substitution drill for referential phrases.** Makes her identify what "this purpose" refers to, then reads the sentence back with the referent spliced in: "the recent ads for Epson's campaign were sent to too few households to serve the purpose of influencing popular opinion effectively." Explains why: "they layer that on, layer that on, and then at the end of it you get the idea but it's kind of vague. Now we're keeping it very concrete."
- **Corrects the "conclusion equals summary" misconception directly**: "it's not a summary of the argument. That's the mistake a lot of students make. They think it's like a summary like in English class."
- **The intentions test for fact vs. opinion**: "with intentions, it's really hard to know somebody's intentions because we're not them... do we truly have access to his thoughts?"
- **Plain-language paraphrase of the stimulus content**: "politicians do what politicians do," and for Epson, "he's just dipping his feet in the water, he's almost doing like a trial run. He wants to see, do I have what it takes to be the big man in office?"
- **The shopping mall analogy for pre-phrasing**, the session's signature image: "if you go to a shopping mall without a shopping list and the salesman gets his hands on you, he's going to take you left, right, he's going to sell you everything. You're going to leave empty and you're going to leave broke. We want to say, sorry sir, we're good, we know what we want, it's the second aisle."
- **The left-side rule**: "the answers to our questions are not on the right side of the screen. They're on the left side... The right answers are always on the left side, which is where the stimulus is."
- **Chose the question as a deliberate trap for her platform's habits.** He knew 7Sage students react to the word "but" as a conclusion indicator. "I'm not an evil guy, but there's a very dogmatic approach 7Sage takes that exposes you to certain weaknesses... we want to engage with it not on a mechanical level but on an intuitive level. We don't want to be like AI."
- **Withholds the answer and walks her to it by asking what the last line builds on**, so that she names the main conclusion herself. When she says "Is that the main conclusion?" he replies "You got it. You just got it."
- **The tutoring pitch, delivered as a contrast rather than a claim**: prep companies "can only give you a system that's factory style"; his job is to install a system and then "refine that system with you to be able to make it so it fits towards you, because it's not a one-size-fits-all approach," using her session data to adjust the approach.
- **Uses his own history as credibility rather than credential-flashing**: "it's actually pretty embarrassing. I was just desperate, it didn't come to me easily. I started off fairly low... I went through Demon, LSAT Lab, 7Sage, Loophole, PowerScore, Manhattan Prep, all of it, because I was neurotic." What changed things was "understanding what the test is testing for," with help from a tutor.
- **Closes by locating the constraint in effort, not ability**: "it's not going to be an issue of whether you can do it. 155 is very conservative, I don't see how you couldn't get there. It's going to be a matter of whether you really want to put down the effort."

## Worked examples
- **Epson campaign ads (main conclusion).** Normally political candidates send out campaign material in order to influence popular opinion. But the recent ads for Epson's campaign were sent to too few households to serve this purpose effectively. The ads were evidently sent out to test their potential to influence popular opinion. They covered a wide variety of topics, and Epson's campaign has been spending heavily on follow-up to gauge their effect on recipients. Path taken: predict from "normally" that a departure is coming; substitute "influencing popular opinion" for "this purpose"; rule out sentence two as a fact (a threshold either was or was not met); recognize sentence three as opinion because it claims an intention no one can observe; see that the last sentence (variety of topics, heavy follow-up spending) exists to support sentence three. Therefore sentence three is the main conclusion. Answer C.
