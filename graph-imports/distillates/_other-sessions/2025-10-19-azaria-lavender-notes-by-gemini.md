# Azaria Lavender, 2025-10-19

## LSAT concepts

- **Structure vs logic are separate analyses.** Structure is premise / subconclusion / conclusion. Logic is the method of reasoning: causation, conditional, comparison. "People think for whatever reason that structure and logic are the same thing." Specifically, a cause is not automatically the premise and the effect is not automatically the conclusion; either can occupy either slot. Same for sufficient and necessary.
- **Method of reasoning**: how the author advances from premise A to conclusion B. The same premise and conclusion can be joined by a conditional, a causal claim, or an analogy, and which one is used changes everything downstream.
- **Reasoning type predicts the flaw.** A conditional argument is susceptible to reversing sufficient and necessary. A causal argument is susceptible to inferring causation from correlation. This lets you pre-eliminate answer choices in flaw questions: "If I see a conditional problem, I shouldn't see something to do with alternative explanation."
- **Keyword: "explanation" means causation.** "Interchangeable words on the LSAT. Explanation does not mean definition, does not mean support. Replace that word with cause every time." Companion: **"phenomenon" means effect.**
- **Keyword: "so"** introduces a conclusion, like *therefore*. Premise indicators: since, because, for, after all.
- **Keyword: "if"** is a sufficient indicator, flagging a conditional. Others: when, every, any.
- **Keyword: "by"** introduces evidence, never a conclusion (carried over from the prior session and applied).
- **Keyword: "on the grounds"** introduces a premise.
- **Logical contradiction** has a technical definition: A is true and not-A is true simultaneously. "If A then B, and also if A then not B." Not merely "you're not making sense" or "you're incorrect."
- **Counter-example**: an example that goes against the principle being discussed. Used here to refute a universal claim.
- **Inductive vs deductive reasoning**: inductive moves from a list of examples to a generalization (wolves, foxes, dogs have morality, therefore all animals do). Deductive moves from a generalization down to an instance.
- **Internal critique vs affirming a position.** If an author says a conclusion cannot be drawn *on these grounds*, he is not stating his own view, he is attacking someone else's inference. "If he's criticizing some sort of belief or system, he's responding. If he's just asserting what he believes to be true, that's his argument independent of somebody else's."
- **Two-part answer check** (reviewed from the prior session): one part characterizes the evidence, one the conclusion, and both must be accurate even if one seems less relevant.
- **Peripheral information** review: opposing viewpoint plus pivot word (but, yet, however), background as context, concession tied to an opposing viewpoint.
- **De-abstraction**: translating LSAT-register language into ordinary terms. "They're trying to get us to be intimidated by the language. We need to get past it, work not around it, work through it."

## Teaching moves

- **The travel analogy carries the entire logic lesson.** Premise is Birmingham, Alabama (where Azaria actually lives), conclusion is Toronto (where Joshua is). "How they get there, think of that as the logic. It's the fuel that gets them from the premise to the conclusion." A subconclusion is a layover in New York.
- **He extended the analogy into flaw prediction, which is its real payoff.** Each mode of transport has its own characteristic danger. He asked her what goes wrong on a plane (she said mechanical failure), then pressed on whether two commercial planes ever collide the way cars do. "That's nearly impossible. So if I'm taking that method of transportation, that's a specific kind of danger." Therefore: identify the reasoning type, and you know in advance which flaw to expect and which answer choices are impossible.
- **Same argument, three engines.** He built one argument (John scores over 170 on practice tests, therefore John is analytical in his approach to life) and swapped the connecting premise three times: conditional ("only people who are highly analytical score that high"), causal ("constant practice on the LSAT causes one to be analytical in life"), analogy ("David, who scores the same percentile, is highly analytical"). "I could have used a train here instead of taking the flight. We're still getting to the same outcome."
- **Anticipate rather than react.** "I'm not waiting till the end of the stimulus. People wait till they look for what they want to find in the answer choices. I want to be halfway through the stimulus and say, okay, I can see where this is going."
- **Keyword scan before reading**, framed as "chapters to a book." Ten to twenty seconds, one or two hits is enough. "You do not want to linger on this too much, but getting at least one or two gives you a huge boost so you know what's coming your way."
- **Read conditionals backwards.** On seeing *if*, reread from the *if* clause first. "We're playing with the ideas as we go. We don't want to just read passively. Literally play with the ideas in your head, it keeps that retention."
- **Blocked her from defining a word with itself.** She defined logical contradiction as "two ideas that contradict one another." Joshua: "I want you to not use the word contradict. You can't cheat." She then produced a workable definition, and he supplied the technical one (New York is in the US / New York is not in the US).
- **Made her locate the claimed flaw in the argument.** Rather than telling her D was wrong, he asked where the contradiction lived, and used the *by* rule to show it had to live in the evidence, not the conclusion. She then answered her own question: "I don't think they can. There were two examples showing the same thing."
- **Mined her own vocabulary for the credited answer.** She kept saying "examples." He stopped: "You keep saying one word that's getting me excited. What's the word?" Then walked her from *example* to *counter-example*, which she defined correctly, which is the credited characterization.
- **Precision on what the conclusion actually claims.** He checked twice that she saw the author is not arguing animals should have rights, only that this particular ground fails. "He's making a specific claim. On this particular piece of evidence, it is not sufficient."
- **A real-world parallel for internal critique**, using the pro-choice framing: arguing "it would be wrong to deny reproductive rights on the grounds that it's murdering babies" implicitly opposes another position, whereas "women should have autonomy over their bodies" is a free-standing claim.
- **Teach with the hardest questions**, restated: "It's way better to do the hardest questions to see all of the potential ways they can pull tricks on us. Then when you go to easy questions, it becomes way faster."
- **Never choose an answer whose key term you cannot define technically.** "We do not want to choose an answer choice unless we have a technical definition."
- **Diagnostic read on wrong answers goes past the wrong answer.** Reviewing her Demon section, he explained he does not just look at what she got wrong: "That's very surface level. There's timing and confidence, which aren't available on Demon." He asked her to flag confidence 1 to 5 per question, because "two people could choose the same answer, but one got it because they knew."
- **Removed choice architecture as a tactic.** The new platform will carry pre-made drill sets and a schedule so she never decides what to do. "You don't have to think. You can just sit down. Kind of makes it so it's a highway for you."
- **The dishes analogy for concession**, using a partner who has left the dishes for the tenth day. One route is "your bags are at the door," the other is "I get it, you work hard, you're the man of the house, but you need to put the dishes away." Both push back; the concession just makes it land better. "Taking three steps back to go four steps forward."

## Worked examples

- **Animal rights, method of reasoning.** Stimulus: wolves do not tolerate an attack by one wolf on another if the latter demonstrates submission by baring his throat; the same is true of foxes and domesticated dogs; so it would be erroneous to deny that animals have rights on the grounds that only human beings are capable of obeying moral rules. Keyword scan yields *if* (conditional, line one) and *so* (conclusion, line three); line two is a comparison. Azaria chose D: "establishes a claim by showing that the denial of that claim entails a logical contradiction." Joshua broke D word by word: *by* means the characterization must fit the evidence, and the evidence contains no A / not-A pair, only two examples pointing the same way. Correct characterization: the author offers counter-examples to refute the claim that only humans obey moral rules. Another distractor claimed the argument establishes inductively that all animals possess morality, which overgeneralizes what the stimulus concludes. The unstated assumption they surfaced together: that declining to attack a submitting animal *is* obeying a moral rule, and that this entails having rights. Azaria also generated the gap independently: "Could be other animals that behave in different ways."
