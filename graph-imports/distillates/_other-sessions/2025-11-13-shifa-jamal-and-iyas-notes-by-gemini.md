## LSAT concepts
- **Main conclusion** — the operational two-step test Joshua teaches: the statement must be an opinion, and it must receive explicit support from at least one other statement. A conclusion can never be a fact.
- **Fact versus opinion** — a fact is descriptive and verifiable, mappable onto something real, true or false against the world. An opinion says how things ought to be or what the author believes. "Whenever there's a measurement that could be objectively put in, like there's a black and white line," it is a fact.
- **Pivot words** — but, yet, however. They always pivot away from some other situation, and there is usually a point of tension that transitions into the author's argument. Necessary but not sufficient to mark a conclusion.
- **Referential language** — replace "this," "they," "their" with the antecedent explicitly. "That keeps it very sharp in your head... they will not be able to take you on a ride if you explicitly spell it out."
- **Active reading** — pausing mid-stimulus to form a hypothesis about what comes next, rather than reading straight through and reconstructing after.

## Teaching moves
- **Opens with a full history intake before touching a question**: journey, diagnostic, PT range, test dates, target schools, undergrad GPA shape, sections of strength and weakness, resources used. Only then, "is it okay if we run through a question quickly?"
- **Builds rapport with concrete shared ground**, not filler: both born in Ottawa, he researched at University of Toronto, he knows Queen's weights the best two years and Osgoode is holistic. Signals he understands the Canadian process without her having to explain it.
- **Discloses his own path honestly**, including not going to law school: "I was more interested in the academic element versus more so like the attorney route which was not as glamorous as I initially thought it to be." Positions himself as a fellow traveler rather than an authority.
- **Explicitly decouples the diagnostic from correctness**: "I don't really care if you get the question right or wrong... it's more about the approach is what I'm more interested in seeing." Repeats this three times to defuse the pressure, and names the pressure out loud, "I'm putting you on the spot here," "I'm throwing you in at the eye of the beast."
- **Reads the nerves and says so**: "I can see already the nerves building up as I asked you that question."
- **Diagnoses her prep company by her behavior, not her answer.** He chose the question deliberately to expose the 7Sage signature. His framing of the field: 7Sage is "hard logic," "very formulaic," "almost like religious," and can leave students "too mechanical." LSAT Demon is the opposite, "very flowy, very fluid, but there's not enough hard lines, no guardrails, no structure that's like able to be repeatable." His position: take both, "be able to do what the question asks of us."
- **Stops her on the very first word of the stimulus** ("Normally") and makes her predict what must follow. She gets there herself: normally sets the status quo, so expect a departure from it. This is the whole active-reading lesson delivered in one move.
- **Plane metaphor for reading pace**: "like a plane, we want a plane gets traction, takes off, it flies off. It's shaky the first, then once you take it off it takes off." Start slow, accelerate.
- **The "triple R" framework**: **read, rephrase, react.** Rephrase instantly as you go, not at the end. The diagnostic value is the point: if you cannot restate a line in dumbed-down English, that flags the difficulty, because "the content is pretty simple usually. It's the way that they word things. They word things very badly, very poorly, intentionally."
- **Models the rephrase himself** at a deliberately low register: "normally politicians do what politicians do, right? They send out flyers to get people to like them."
- **Pushes past a correct-sounding answer to demand the operationalized version.** When Shifa said she looks for "where the author's argument is," he refused it: "what's the actual actionable... let's say I've never heard of what an argument is. How would we find that part of the argument?" The move forces a procedure instead of an intuition.
- **Lets her be wrong on the fact/opinion call and works it out slowly.** She classified "sent to too few households" as opinion; he showed it implies a threshold number, hence a verifiable metric, hence a fact. When she said "I'm not really sure what you mean by opinion," he stopped and defined the category from scratch rather than pressing on, using Canada's landmass and a 3.9 GPA cutoff as fact examples.
- **"Evidently" as the tell for opinion**: the root is evidence, so the author is inferring intent. Then the check question, "is there a way to objectively know someone's intentions?" No, therefore opinion. This is the move that isolated the real conclusion.
- **Praise is specific and immediate when earned**: "You got it. Bang on. You sniped it."
- **Names her strengths as a strategic asset, and explains why it matters for the plan**: high reading ability is hard to build from scratch and "usually a lifetime of skill," so having it already unlocked means her gap is systematic approach, not raw capacity. "Knowing the difference between what to do, what not to do, and applying your energy towards the right places is the biggest difference to make."
- **Answers the "will you re-explain things four times" question by inverting the worry**: his failure mode is going too slow, not too fast. "I don't like to leave tabs opened without them being closed." Justification is foundational: an unrefined micro-skill "will follow you into the later parts of your LSAT journey" and surfaces later on hard questions. Adds that disengagement, not repeated questions, is what actually frustrates him: "voicing up is very important... I'll never be frustrated by asking questions."
- **Grounds his credibility on struggle rather than talent**: "the LSAT didn't come to be easy. It took me a very long time... I expected it to come much quicker but didn't."
- **Describes the session model concretely** when asked: theory, then application to questions selected from her practice-test and 7Sage analytics, then recap note and homework, then check the homework against real section results. Data-driven targeting is the pitch, "I do things off the data."

## Worked examples
- **A main conclusion question (Epson campaign ads).** Stimulus: normally political candidates send out campaign material in order to influence popular opinion, but the recent ads for Epson's campaign were sent to too few households to serve this purpose effectively. The ads were evidently sent out to test their potential to influence popular opinion. They covered a wide variety of topics and Epson's campaign has been spending heavily on follow-up to gauge their effect on recipients.
  Shifa's first pass answered B, choosing the "but" sentence because "but" introduces the author's opinion. Joshua walked it line by line: "normally" sets a status quo and predicts a departure; the "but" sentence contains a verifiable threshold (a number of households) and is therefore a **fact**, not the conclusion; "evidently" signals the author inferring intent, which cannot be objectively verified, so the third sentence is the opinion; the last sentence (wide variety of topics, heavy follow-up spending) exists to support that inference. Shifa reached the conclusion herself and corrected to **C**.
  Also drilled referential language twice inside this stimulus: "this purpose" resolves to influencing popular opinion, "they" resolves to the ads.
