# Kristine Ale, 2025-10-04

## LSAT concepts

- **Fallacies as the human default, not as arcana.** "These are wrong ways of thinking that typically occur with people in general. It's almost like a human condition, certain types of faulty thinking that we naturally are inclined to fall into because it's against our initial intuition." Sourced to informal logic, where philosophers catalogued "the common tropes humans naturally fall susceptible to."
- **Conditional reasoning fallacy**: affirming the necessary condition. If it is a Granny Smith then it is green does not license green therefore Granny Smith.
- **Correlation for causation**: "People who eat green apples are more likely to live longer. Naturally we'll hear a causal relationship. It's only saying those two things happen together a lot. There's a frequent overlap in those groups." Also flagged the third-factor explanation, that people who live on farms may both eat those apples and live longer.
- **Whole to part and part to whole**: two sides of one coin. A trait of the entity does not transfer to every component, and a trait of a component does not transfer to the entity.
- **Unproven vs proven false**: "Because I don't have evidence to prove something doesn't mean that by default it's wrong." Flagged as especially common in stimuli that cite studies.
- **Sampling bias**: the sample must be representative of the population the study claims to measure. Distinguished from small sample size in practice, though the credited answer here used the "too small a sample" phrasing.
- **Appeal to popularity**: a claim inferred to be false merely because a majority believes it false.
- **Equivocation**: the same word used with two different meanings inside one argument. Characterized as "the most comedic of all fallacies."
- **Sufficient vs necessary**: named as the single most common flaw on the test. "That's the most common fallacy. Number one on the test."
- **Premise indicator "for"**: signals that what follows is evidence, so the conclusion sits just before it.
- **Referential language in conclusions**: a small word like "this" can carry an entire preceding concept. "They're going to use one small word that's fully loaded."
- **Fallacy tiering**: some fallacies are frequent correct answers (conditional, causal, whole/part, unproven vs proven false, sampling), while others appear mainly as wrong answers because they are too obvious. Ad hominem was named as "too predictable" for the LSAT to use as the credited answer.

## Teaching moves

- **Homework designed for exposure, not mastery.** Kristine had only skimmed the fallacy list. Joshua treated that as the plan: "That was the goal. It was only to get you exposed to it, so when we come into it today you'll have at least an exposure, because when you see it for the first time, seeing it for the second time is way easier. Now you're just putting the pieces together."
- **Analogies built from the student's own biography.** Her undergrad campus, University of Maryland, carried the whole-to-part explanation (a beautiful campus does not mean the law library washroom is beautiful, and a beautiful library does not mean a beautiful campus). Her hoped-for law school carried the sufficient/necessary explanation. Her stated 26 years of age got used to calibrate a movie reference.
- **Making the student generate the mirror case.** After she volunteered a whole-to-part example (Bowie, Maryland is a good community, therefore everyone there has a nice house), he immediately asked for the inverse: "Now let's go from part to whole." She produced it.
- **Testing recall by asking rather than restating.** "Whole to part, right? Do you remember that one? Do you recognize that one in the way it sounds?" When she did not, he taught it rather than assuming.
- **Working a question he had never seen, deliberately.** "I haven't seen this one, but I want to do one I haven't seen with you." Signals that the method, not memorized answers, is what is being transmitted.
- **Escalating equivocation examples until one lands.** First "arms" (body parts vs weapons, with "where did they get the licensing for it" as the reveal), which did not connect at first, so he rebuilt it around a hypothetical friend Jerry. Then "rich" (a rich movie vs how much it made), then Kristine supplied "rich chocolate" herself.
- **Redirecting to indicator words when the student gropes.** She guessed at unnamed additional factors on the Joan question. "Not exactly. Let's pay attention to the indicator words. In the first line, we see if. What does if refer to?"
- **Making her build the contrapositive aloud and correcting the shortcut.** Her first attempt collapsed the necessary condition ("if she doesn't get an A, she can't pass the course"). He held her to the full negation, that not passing the course without the presentation implies no A on the term paper.
- **The ski mask analogy for one sufficient path among many.** "If you run into a store with a ski mask and a gun, you'll be convicted of a crime. Just because you didn't have a ski mask doesn't mean you were not convicted. That's just one way. You could do fraud."
- **The 180 analogy, personalized.** "If you get a 180 on the LSAT, you'll get into Maryland Law School. Kristine got a 179, so therefore she won't get in." She produced the diagnosis herself: "Because it's not necessary."
- **Crediting the student's own first instinct after she abandons it.** She noted the conclusion was about the presentation, not about passing, then drifted toward E. Joshua pulled her back: "No, you're right though. What you said at first was right. Repeat that. It's exactly the problem."
- **Treating wrong answer choices as free training reps**, the session's closing thesis. "Let the wrong answers help you get better too. That's the cheat code to flaw questions. People that just look for the right answers are wasting four other answer choices that could help build pattern recognition, which is all fallacies are." Demonstrated live when he asked her to name the fallacy in an answer choice that was not the credited one.
- **Quantifying the payoff to motivate memorization.** "It gives you basically one to two questions per section you'll get. You'll recognize it and the answer choice will be exactly as you expect. You get at least three points in a section just by knowing these in and out." And: "Once it becomes second nature, you get these questions done in like 20 seconds."
- **Raising the bar past recognition.** "It's not enough to recognize them. You need to know all the fallacies by name and what they actually pertain to."
- **Casual, joking register.** Ribbing her for not knowing Coach Carter ("Are you even American? Come on"), and describing the students taunting the speaker as having "smoke for him" and "popped off here."

## Worked examples

- **Guest speaker at the political ideologies class** (flaw). A carefully researched, theoretical talk drew vicious taunts from two dissenting students, and several others applauded the attempt to humiliate the speaker. Conclusion: universities these days do not foster fair-minded and tolerant intellectual debate. Flaw: sampling bias, generalizing from one politically charged classroom to all universities. Joshua's sharpening: "Do you think the biochem nerds are going to be acting like this? If a guest speaker came from math, I don't think people are going to attack him over certain equations. A class called political ideologies, you can assume they're very opinionated people." Credited answer, draws a general conclusion based on too small a sample. Rejected: an answer about the professor's own opinion (no professor opinion appears), an answer about inconsistently advocating tolerance (nothing is advocated), an answer about emotional appeal, and an answer about focusing on behavior rather than its reasons.
- **Political commentators and country X** (flaw). Commentators see recent government policies toward country X as appeasement pure and simple. Conclusion: that view is fundamentally mistaken. Evidence, marked by "for": polls show most people disagree with the commentators' assessment. Correct answer C, a claim is inferred to be false merely because a majority believes it to be false, that is, appeal to popularity. Distractors named by fallacy type as a drill: the ambiguity answer points at equivocation, and E points at part to whole (what is true of persons individually is true of a country as a whole), rejected because the argument concerns policies toward the country, never the people as a whole.
- **Joan's term paper** (flaw). Joan got A's on all her homework, so if she had gotten an A on her term paper she would pass the course even without the class presentation. She did not get an A on the term paper, so she must do the presentation to pass. Only two things are known: the conditional and its contrapositive. Not getting an A triggers neither. An A minus or a B plus might equally have cleared the presentation requirement. Correct answer B, presupposes without justification that not getting an A prevents her from passing without the presentation. A rejected on the word "must." E rejected because it addresses whether she passes the course at all, while the conclusion is only about whether she has to do the presentation.
