# Mosess Bryant, 2025-10-19

## LSAT concepts

- **Argument (LSAT definition)**: the relationship between a premise and a conclusion. Not a disagreement between two parties. "Everything else is baggage. There's a sweet dance between the premise and conclusion, and your goal is to assess if they're in sync."
- **Conclusion (LSAT definition)**: "a statement that is supported by at least one other statement." Emphatically not a summary. "If I ask you what the main conclusion of the stimulus is, you're looking to understand the general idea of the stimulus. That's not the main conclusion for the LSAT. It's actually one explicit statement."
- **Every statement serves the main conclusion.** Even an opposing viewpoint is introduced only to be discredited, which supports the author indirectly. "Indirect attacks and direct attacks."
- **The golden rule**: "You need to have a ridiculously strong and very clear understanding of the conclusion." Step one on every argument question type. Must-be-true and most-strongly-supported are the exceptions since they have no conclusion.
- **Conclusion is an opinion, and an opinion alone is not an argument.** "An opinion is required in an argument, but an opinion alone is not an argument. It's not sufficient."
- **Two-step conclusion check**: (1) is the statement an opinion, something debatable rather than measurable? (2) is at least one other statement *explicitly* supporting it? "Your goal is not to read between the lines."
- **The why / because test**: insert "why" or "because" between the candidate conclusion and a candidate premise. If it reads coherently, the conclusion is right. Run it in reverse to eliminate. "It doesn't have to be a good argument, but it has to be coherent."
- **Validity**: the conclusion must be guaranteed by the evidence. "There should be no conceivable possible way where the conclusion could be false if the evidence is true." Not high probability, not credible, not reasonable.
- **Evidence is never disputed.** Every stimulus is its own universe with its own laws; you accept them on entry.
- **Validity is binary.** "It's either valid or invalid. It's not a range." A 99% argument fails exactly as hard as an absurd one.
- **The what-if / objection**: generate a scenario where the evidence holds and the conclusion still fails. "That what-if you've designed is the most versatile asset on the test," and it works identically for strengthen (rule out the objection), weaken (expose it), and sufficient assumption (plug the gap to 100%).
- **Every argument on the LSAT is invalid**, with rare exceptions in parallel questions. Therefore always take the opposing side of the conclusion.
- **Validity lives at the intersection of the premises.** Look for the overlapping term; the conclusion bridges whatever is attached to each side of it.
- **Three kinds of overlap**, in ascending difficulty: **overlapping term** (hydrogen appears in both premises), **categorical overlap** (6'2" falls under "over six foot" with no repeated wording), **conceptual overlap** (a requirement relates to an outcome, which is really sufficient vs necessary).
- **Requirement is not guarantee (sufficient vs necessary, previewed).** Meeting a requirement does not produce the outcome. "Because he always eats pizza doesn't mean he'll get admitted to the hospital. But if he got into the hospital, he damn sure always eats pizza."
- **No claims about the opposite group.** "Prejudice toward those over six foot is never justified" says nothing about people at exactly six foot or under. "A very popular trick on the LSAT, making a claim about the opposite group."
- **A lack of X is not the opposite of X.** "A lack of prejudice isn't being kind. It's just not being prejudice."
- **Premise indicator words (FABS)**: for, after all, because, since. Whatever follows must be a premise, "a quirk of the English language." Corollary: whatever precedes it is indirectly the conclusion.
- **Conclusion indicators** (therefore, thus, hence) are looser because they can also introduce an intermediate conclusion.
- **Intermediate / subconclusion**: supported by one statement while explicitly supporting another. Deferred to a later session.

## Teaching moves

- **The wedding cake** carries the definition of conclusion. The cake is the conclusion, the table is the premise, together they are the argument. Then the physical version: he mimed handing Mosess a huge wedding cake with nothing underneath. "What would happen? It'd be a glorious mess. It wouldn't be a cake anymore, it'd just be splattered. In the same capacity, a conclusion needs at least one other statement supporting it, or the cake is useless."
- **Correcting everyday definitions without deflating the student.** Twice Mosess gave the common-sense answer (argument as disagreement, conclusion as summary). Joshua's move both times: "You're giving me an intelligent common-sense answer. The problem is the LSAT uses specific words we use in everyday language, but in a completely different context."
- **The Quebec courts analogy** for accepting the evidence. If Mosess were representing him in Quebec, he would have to follow Quebec law. "You can't say, oh, but in New York we do it like this. You can't bring that here." The alternative he offered, deadpan, was to overthrow the LSAC.
- **Alien with amnesia.** "Every argument you read, think of it as though you came to this planet as an alien. You don't know the laws of physics. We don't know water is wet." Followed by the key rule: "What's left unsaid on the LSAT is arguably more important than what's actually said."
- **"Basically the same is the enemy of this test."** Repeated as a refrain, and enforced ruthlessly: over six foot is not six foot, containing hydrogen is not "similar," containing hydrogen is not "natural" because hydrogen could be made in a lab.
- **Deliberate sarcasm as a confidence test.** After Mosess correctly rejected "both contain something natural," Joshua feigned disagreement ("But hydrogen"). Mosess held: "Yes, it's valid" then doubled down on his original. Joshua then disclosed the tactic: "I will be sarcastic a lot. I do that intentionally because I want you to be a thousand percent sure that when you tell me something, you believe it."
- **The Swedish parents analogy**, same as used with other students: the conclusion is the offspring of the premises and may resemble one parent entirely, but if it comes out looking like Joshua, "the neighbors, we got to ask questions." Anything imported from outside makes it invalid, because outside material means interpretation, and interpretation can always be read the wrong way.
- **The lawyer frame, twice.** For the burden: you do not have to show your client is a saint volunteering at dog shelters, only that guilt is not guaranteed. "If the glove don't fit, you must acquit. I bring it from 100% to 99%, I win, they lose." For posture: "As a lawyer your goal isn't to have opinions. You're like a mercenary. You're formless. You take the side you're hired." He added the discomfort honestly: "There's almost a sociopathic element to being a lawyer," and that judgment is the judge's and jury's job.
- **The cookie jar defense**, reused: crumbs on your beard, caught red-handed, and the patch is that your grandmother owns a cookie factory. Used to prove why you must find the hole before you can strengthen.
- **The arguments are stupid once undressed.** "The arguments on the LSAT are very dumb. If you heard it written in a normal way, talking to a friend, you'd say, bro, that's a stupid argument. But they hide it behind convoluted syntax, behind very poorly written stimulus. That's all they're doing."
- **Named the psychological difficulty rather than pretending it away.** "It's very difficult for people to live within the premises. It's so easy to introduce our beliefs into the evidence. It's a reflexive habit you've been doing your whole life." And on the same content in conversation: "You'd say, man, what are you on drugs? But on the LSAT you have to bite your tongue."
- **Asked the student to justify the method before supplying the reason.** "Why would it make sense to do that for every question type?" Mosess answered eliminate answer choices; Joshua added retention.
- **Politically neutral by design.** He used Mamdani and Trump as examples and repeatedly disclaimed a stance ("I'm Canadian so I can't have an opinion on this"), noting he has seen students taken aback.
- **LeBron and Kyrie for subconclusions.** LeBron is the main conclusion, Kyrie the intermediate conclusion, the rest of the Cavs roster the premises. Kyrie supports LeBron but is himself supported. "Doesn't mean Kyrie's a bum." Then a cheaper mnemonic: intermediate as in difficulty level, middle of the road.
- **Read, rephrase, react.** "Read the first complete thought, then rephrase into natural language and try not to look at it as you do that. Dumb it down. Turn it into a story. Then react, does that make sense, do I have an opinion on it?"
- **Attack even when the question does not ask you to.** On a main conclusion question: "These arguments are invalid. They're good practice even if it's not asking you to point out the problem." Rationale: "You can't not understand an argument if you can make an objection to it. It's a good verification test. If I can't come up with an objection, there's something I'm probably missing."
- **Build a parallel from the student's own interests.** Learning Mosess follows sports, Joshua rebuilt the junk food argument as banning steroids to benefit athletes, then asked what happened when marijuana was outlawed. Mosess supplied "black market." The unintended-consequences gap became his.
- **Refereed the objections honestly.** He rejected three of Mosess's attempts as not respecting the argument (one person needing junk food does not touch a claim about *public* health; denying the contribution disputes the evidence; other health factors are outside the claim). He named the difficulty rather than softening it: "That's tricky, by the way. It takes a lot of practice."
- **Trap-naming before the trap springs.** On the main conclusion answer choices: "D is a very tricky answer choice that most people would fall into. It's counterintuitive because D seems more right. I don't disagree, I'm with you. But we're not looking for the summary. We're looking for that exact line."
- **Set expectations for difficulty as a feature.** "Don't feel intimidated. Expect it to be hard. I promise you it should be at least strenuous. If it's not, great. If it is, it's expected."
- **Conservative on promises.** "I don't want to make any false promises, I don't want to give you false hope, I want to stay conservative. But I have a very good feeling you'll be pleasantly impressed with what we can do before that point."

## Worked examples

- **Whiteboard, overlapping term.** Apples and pears both contain hydrogen; hydrogen is an element. Mosess produced a valid conclusion immediately. Joshua then tested two invalid variants: "both contain something natural" (Mosess rejected it correctly and held under sarcastic pressure), and "apples and pears are similar" (Mosess accepted it at first; Joshua broke it with "what if they only have a tenth of a percent of hydrogen?").
- **Whiteboard, categorical overlap.** Prejudice toward those over six foot is always unjustified; Max is 6'2". Valid: prejudice toward Max would not be justified, though we do not know he will ever face any. Variants tested: Max is exactly 6 foot (invalid, "over" excludes it, "maybe 6.001 would work"); Max is 5'11" and prejudice toward him is justified (invalid, nothing is claimed about the opposite group); "we should be kind to Max" (invalid, a lack of prejudice is not kindness). Mosess got all three right, and explained the last one himself: "his height doesn't correlate with how someone should act towards him."
- **Whiteboard, conceptual overlap.** To be admitted to the hospital you must always eat pizza; Alex eats pizza. Nothing follows, since sometimes is not always. Joshua then flipped it: Alex *always* eats pizza. Mosess concluded admission; Joshua showed that is the sufficient/necessary reversal, using law school ("taking the LSAT is a requirement; does that mean you get in?"). The only valid direction: if Alex was admitted, he must always eat pizza. Joshua flagged setting him up: "I'm kind of evil for that."
- **Junk food tax, main conclusion (official question).** Stimulus: a doctor says it would benefit public health if junk food were taxed; the excessive proportion of junk food in diets contributes to many common and serious health problems in this and other countries; if junk food were much more expensive than healthful food, people would be encouraged to make dietary changes that would reduce these problems. Mosess identified the first sentence as the conclusion unprompted, before Joshua asked. Confirmed with the why/because test forward and backward, the reverse reading being obviously incoherent. Answer choices: Mosess chose D ("taxing junk food would encourage people to reduce the proportion of junk food in their diets"), which restates the *evidence*, not the conclusion. The credited answer restates the first line only. The objection they built together: taxation may push consumption to a black market, so encouraging change does not guarantee a public health benefit, built by analogy to outlawing steroids and marijuana.
