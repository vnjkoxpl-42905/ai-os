# Fiorella Meyer, 2025-10-13

## LSAT concepts

- **Inference (valid conclusion)**: a conclusion is valid only if the evidence *guarantees* it. Joshua drew a hard line between "supported" and "guaranteed": "It's not enough for a conclusion to be just like supported. When I say support, I mean like likely or sounds reasonable. That's not enough." The test: "Is there one conceivable possible scenario where the conclusion could be false even if the evidence is true?"
- **Inference (overlapping concepts)**: to build a valid conclusion, find the point of intersection between the premises. "There has to be a point of overlap to be able to connect them." Apples and pears contain hydrogen + hydrogen is an element, therefore apples and pears contain an element. He called it "almost like verbal mathematics."
- **Inference (applying a premise to a specific case)**: the overlap need not be word for word. "Anyone over 6 foot tall, prejudice is never justified" + "James is 6'2" yields "prejudice toward James is not justified" because James satisfies the criterion. Order of premises does not matter.
- **Conditional logic (negation error, previewed)**: from "anyone over 6 foot" you cannot conclude anything about people under 6 foot. "I need to be very careful not to make a judgment about the opposite group." He tested it by changing James to 6'1" and then 5'11" to see if she would flip the conclusion.
- **New concept in the conclusion = the assumption**: "If the conclusion for it to be valid, it has to essentially be a restatement of the evidence. If they bring any new ideas into the conclusion, by definition it's invalid." Any term appearing in the conclusion but not the evidence is where the argument breaks.
- **Weaken**: identify the gap, then predict an objection that exploits it. Answer choices confirm the gap you already found.
- **Strengthen**: identical process. "You need to know the weakness to be able to strengthen it." The correct answer patches the gap.
- **Assumption (necessary / sufficient)**: flagged as coming later, and tied back to the new-concept rule: "that concept by definition is an assumption."

## Teaching moves

- **The unified framework, stated as a formula**: "Evidence, conclusion, attack." He told her to stop reading the question stem entirely: "I don't want you to read it. I want you to read every argument the same. Then when you figure out the problem, you do what you want with that." Strengthen means deny the problem, weaken means expose it, parallel means find the argument with the same problem.
- **The lawyer frame** is the session's central analogy and he sustained it for twenty minutes. Strengthen = defense attorney: "You're not going there just to advocate for him just blindly. You need to know where the arguer client is weak to be able to defend him." He built a running bit about being caught with cookies in hand, crumbs on the mouth, and on camera, then patched the alibi choice by choice. Weaken = prosecutor: "Now I'm going to show the judge and prove to them that you're guilty."
- **Read as a blank slate**: "If it's not stated explicitly, we don't know it to be true." Pushed to absurdity deliberately: from "James is 6'4", therefore James is tall" he objected "What if James is like a giraffe? I never said James is a human."
- **The Swedish parents analogy** for a conclusion importing a new idea: two blonde, blue-eyed Swedish parents can have a child who looks like the father or the mother, no problem. "But if the child comes out looking like me, we kind of probably have a problem there." A conclusion must inherit from the evidence.
- **Elicit the definition before teaching**: opened by asking "What's a valid conclusion?" She said "supported by its premises," he pushed with "What level of support?" until she said "100%."
- **Build the objection from her own life, not from the text.** On the bookstore question he stopped the analysis and interviewed her: do you read, what did you last read, is it physical, where do you read it, how old are you. She answered 24 and physical book; he replied "that answer was very old people style. Most people, do you think they're reading physical books nowadays?" She produced "a lot of people read on their Kindles." The gap was then hers, not his.
- **Every word counts**: when she paraphrased the stimulus as "a decline of sales from fiction," he refused it. "They did close. That's what they want you to think. Read the first few words." The word she had dropped was *bookstores*.
- **Slow is fast**: "People think that when they run through the stimulus they're getting more time. No, you're wasting more time. You're paying up front." And "when you read the stimulus, the answers are in the stimulus."
- **The prediction will not match verbatim**: "The objection you designed won't be word for word, but it will point towards the hole." She predicted Kindles and audiobooks; the credited answer said mail-order book clubs. He used the mismatch to teach that the gap, not the wording, is what transfers.
- **Characterizing the test as an adversary**: "They're very devious," "if they're snakes, they're snakes," "they want you to fill in the dots for them." The trap is your own reasonableness.
- **The gym analogy**, chosen because she is a bodybuilder: "Six months into the gym, it's still not going to be easy. Three years in, you can do this with your eyes closed. It's a muscle."
- **Named the meta-skill explicitly**: "That muscle I just went through with you is the greatest asset on the LSAT, because with every question type you're doing the same thing."
- **Read the person's disposition off their reading.** When she interpreted the diamond assessor charitably, he named it: "You're a genuine person, you have a good heart. The way you read it, I was like, oh, she read it in a way that was very genuine." He used her charity as the diagnosis of why she missed the conflict of interest.
- **Praised meta-questions as a progress signal**: "It's good that you're starting to ask these questions because it's indicating to me that you're synthesizing."
- **Modeled not knowing**: on "copper alloy kitchen implements" he said "I have no idea. That's way above my pay grade," and later "I completely misread something, I'm off my game right now." Content ignorance is not an obstacle; structure is.

## Worked examples

- **Warmup deductions (whiteboard, Joshua's own)**: apples/pears/hydrogen/element; the 6-foot prejudice and James at 6'2", then 6'1", then 5'11"; "Fiorella is from Peru + Peru is in South America" therefore she is from South America, contrasted with the invalid "therefore she speaks Spanish" and "therefore she is a patriot."
- **Declining fiction sales, weaken.** Stimulus: bookseller statistics show most *bookstores* have had declining fiction revenues over five years despite campaigns to encourage reading fiction; therefore the campaigns were largely unsuccessful. Gap: bookstore sales are not all reading. Fiorella predicted e-readers, Kindles, audiobooks. Credited answer pointed to mail-order book clubs growing in fiction sales. She got it on a first pass and said "I really didn't even have to read all of the answer choices thoroughly."
- **Gem World diamonds, strengthen.** Stimulus: at most jewelry stores the assessor is the seller, so an assessor might overstate quality; but because all diamonds at Gem World are certified in writing, you are assured a fair price. New concept in the conclusion: *fair price*. Gap: written certification does not equal fairness, and the writer could still be the seller. Joshua walked all five choices. (A) other stores also certify in writing, actually cuts against. (B) certifications written by people with years of appraising experience, "yeah, they've probably been snakes for years." (C) higher quality diamonds, Fiorella: "No, they could be more expensive." (D) volatile market, irrelevant. (E) certifications provided by an independent company, correct, because it severs assessor from seller. Note Fiorella first guessed B before the walkthrough.
- **Roman well, strengthen.** Stimulus: copper alloy kitchen implements found in an abandoned Roman-era well, beneath them a cache of coins some dated to 375 AD, therefore the implements were dropped no earlier than 375 AD. Objections generated: Fiorella noted only *some* coins were dated 375, so others could be older, and that someone could have deliberately placed coins underneath. Joshua added that the well's full depth is unknown and something older could lie beneath the coins. Credited answer (B): the coins were in a dense cluster that could not have formed by coins slipping through an accumulation of large objects, which closes the "coins fell through later" hole. Joshua's framing of a strengthener: "We're basically giving them three points. Hey buddy, you forgot, maybe add this to your argument."
