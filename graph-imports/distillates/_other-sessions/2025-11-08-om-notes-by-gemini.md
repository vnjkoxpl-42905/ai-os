# Om Desai, 2025-11-08

## LSAT concepts
- **Parallel reasoning / parallel flaw** — Joshua calls them "flaw questions in disguise" and treats them as the best flaw practice available. The answer must match argument structure, not content or order.
- **Quantity statements ("most")** — a "most" premise has a first half and a second half, and the only real mistake available is reversing the relationship: most A are B does not give most B are A.
- **Flaw** — the specific flaws worked: reversing a "most" relationship, and inferring absolute numbers from proportions.
- **Certainty and conclusion strength** — "probably" versus "must" as a filter. The correct parallel answer's conclusion must match the stimulus conclusion's level of certainty.
- **Statement classification** — conditional, causal, factual, normative/prescriptive, quantitative, principle, comparative. Joshua treats this as an under-developed skill and the entry point to parallel reasoning.
- **Facts versus predictions** — a claim about a future event cannot be a fact "unless this person can technically know the future"; it is a prediction or opinion, which matters because a conclusion cannot be a fact.
- **Conditional logic** — mentioned as the parallel case: when you see a conditional, "they can really only do one thing. Confuse the sufficient for the necessary," or apply the rule incorrectly.
- **Source reliability / evidence quality** — a thorough review of unreliable data versus a partial review of reliable data.

## Teaching moves
- **Attacks the "I was down to two answers" self-report as a measurement bias, not a skill report.** Two-pronged: first, once the right answer is revealed "you're going to say, ah, that makes sense. I knew that was the right answer," which does not prove you would handle a new scenario. Second, and sharper, the base-rate point: "you don't realize that you're down to the last two answer choices for more questions than you realize... the ones that you get right, you won't remember that." The student only counts the misses.
- **Prescribes an epistemic posture rather than a technique**: treat every wrong answer as "100% wrong," not second-best. He is explicit that this is a useful fiction, "it's not that it's 100% always the case, but it's just better for practical advancement," because it forces genuine engagement with the mistake.
- **Bans explanation videos as the first move in review.** "It robs you of your chance to wrestle with it." The prescribed order is: write your own explanation of why you got it wrong, then watch the explanation, then adjust. His diagnosis of the failure mode is precise: the useful question is not "do I understand this now" but "why didn't I see that," and "maybe I knew it a bit, it's the back of my head, but it wasn't actually in the forefront of my mind." Summary line: "explanation videos are like a blessing and a curse... that's not the review. The review is you not looking at explanation and seeing if you can explain it."
- **Adjusts advice to the student's level rather than giving a rule.** On stem-first versus stimulus-first: stimulus-first is for beginners because "you want to force good habits," but Om is past that and should read the stem first to prime anticipation. Explicitly deflates the debate: "people make a slightly bigger deal than it is."
- **Ties efficiency to signal filtering**, connecting it back to something Om had already observed about himself: naming the fallacy before reading answers works because "you're framing the question and seeing what to expect which filters out a lot of the noise... you want to hit the signal versus the noise."
- **Runs the question cold first.** Turns his camera off and has Om work silently, "pretend like I'm not here," then reconstructs the reasoning path by interview afterward. Later flips to think-aloud when he wants to catch the process live.
- **Catches a self-correction mid-sentence and makes it the lesson.** When Om started to say "most engineering majors" then corrected himself, Joshua stopped him: "You caught something there. I'm going to pause you there. We were about to say something and that was the actual issue." The student's own slip was exactly the flaw the argument commits.
- **Uses a deliberately jarring parallel to make the reversal obvious**: most Americans are white does not give most white people live in America, "that's the whole of Europe."
- **"First half / second half" vocabulary for "most" statements** so the student can track which side the conclusion picks up. He drilled it repeatedly when Om did not get it on the first pass, re-asking three different ways rather than explaining again.
- **Sizes the workload to justify the method**: on parallel reasoning, "you're literally reading five, six arguments... it's five questions almost if you think about it." That is why the slow, general approach loses.
- **The "grounding pole" / "north star" technique**: pick one striking structural feature of the stimulus (here "most", later the word "but") and use it as a filter to delete answers without reading them. He named "but" the low-key weapon: "this is a very low-key, very dangerous, very potent thing... the LSAT writers don't think you'll know that."
- **Same-order and same-content answers are red flags, not confirmations**: "if you see the exact same order, probably a trap... expect it to be not in the exact same order."
- **Algebraic recipe**: strip content, replace repeated concepts with variables, and read the pattern (most A are B, but most C are not B, therefore more A than C). Explicitly permissive about notation, "it doesn't have to be A, B, and C. You can call it thing A, group A, different strokes for different folks."
- **Rejects Venn diagrams** when Om proposed them: "you don't want to take time writing circles on the test, it's inefficient." Substitutes underlining the "most" and treating it as a **bridge**: "think of it as a bridge of two ideas... it's a scale carrying both." The goal is reading the **skeleton**.
- **Normalizes the difficulty deliberately after making it hard**: "I put you in the fire because it's a level five question... people that have done this longer have found that tricky."
- **Warns about where these questions live**: parallel flaw clusters in the last five questions, "they love to do that because it's a time killer. It's an energy sink. You're mentally exhausted at that point."
- **Confirms mastery by explanation quality**: "somebody who's able to explain why the argument is wrong in exact precise terms, they probably get the answer right, which you did very well here."

## Worked examples
- **Question 24, parallel flaw (Western Hall).** Lisa will soon attend National University as an engineering major. At National University, most residents of Western Hall are engineering majors. Therefore she will probably live there. Path: classify statement one as a prediction/opinion, not a fact; classify statement two as a quantity statement with "most"; identify the flaw as reversing the most relationship (most Western Hall residents are engineering majors does not give most engineering majors live in Western Hall). Then filter: conclusion must say "probably," which killed D; a premise must contain "most," which killed B and C ("widely agreed" and "generally" are not "most"). Down to A and E. A talked about the second half of the most relationship, close but wrong. E reversed the first half exactly as the stimulus did, and its different ordering was a point in its favor. Answer E.
- **Question 16, flaw (aircraft off course).** Opponents' figure of one in 20,000 dismissed as unreliable because it rests on a partial review of air traffic control tapes, versus a thorough study of pilot flight reports. Om produced the flaw unaided: pilots have an incentive not to report going off course, since that reflects poorly on them and could cost a job. Joshua's formulation: "a thorough review of something that is unreliable is worse than a partial review of something that is reliable," because videotape cannot be disputed and self-written testimony can. Answer B.
- **Parallel flaw (Morris University, level 5, question 25).** Most sociology majors are taking Introduction to Social Psychology, but most psychology majors are not, hence there must be more sociology than psychology majors enrolled. Structure: most A are B, but most C are not B, therefore more A than C. Flaw: proportions do not fix absolute numbers. Om's own counterexample: 1,000 psychology majors against 100 sociology majors breaks the conclusion. Filtering: conclusion must be definitive, which eliminated A (few if any) and flagged A's comparative reasoning; then require "but" in the evidence, which eliminated B and C. Down to D and E without reading the arguments. D (Valley Foods versus Jumbo Supermarket, vegetables and organic produce) matched the recipe exactly, and Joshua used it to make the point that the conclusion need not contain the word "must" to be definitive. Answer D.
