# Kevin Carballo, 2025-11-29

A conditional-logic session built entirely on Kevin's blind review: one necessary assumption question he got wrong, then two principle-sufficient-assumption questions. The through-line Joshua names explicitly near the end is a single repeated error — on linking questions, Kevin does not check whether the *if* side of an answer choice hooks onto the evidence.

## LSAT concepts

- **A conditional answer choice is not automatically wrong.** Kevin distrusted his answer purely because it was conditional. Joshua: "It's not necessarily wrong because it's conditional. It's not really a problem... a lot of the necessary assumption questions will actually come in conditional." The real defect was elsewhere.
- **Mistaking the conditional for its reverse.** The Latin America analogy: "If you're not from Latin America, then you're not from Argentina" is fine. But "if you are from Argentina then you are from Latin America" is a different rule, and if all we know is that you are *not* Argentinian, it never fires. "Does that tell me anything? No. You could be from Mexico. You could be from a million different countries."
- **An untriggered rule is inert.** "It doesn't activate this rule whatsoever. It becomes basically... it disappears. It doesn't get triggered in any way."
- **Do not negate conditional answer choices.** Kevin had negated one correctly and still got confused. Joshua: "For conditional answers, negating a conditional is confusing. It gets very messy. I wouldn't recommend you do that for conditionals." Substitute test: "ask yourself, would this link the evidence to the conclusion? Is it something that has to be true?" Negation stays reserved for choices phrased with *not*.
- **Strong-language screening for necessary assumption, ranked.** *Primary* and *the most important* are "very very bad for necessary assumption" — "that means that of every single thing that ever exists in that category, this is the first one." *Only* is "not ideal" but strictly better, because *only* is a necessary-condition indicator rather than a superlative: "only is just a placeholder term for if-then."
- **The necessary/sufficient ceiling and floor.** "The strongest necessary assumption is a sufficient assumption. The ceiling for a necessary assumption is a sufficient assumption. And the weakest sufficient assumption ever possible is a necessary assumption." Hence "there's an intersection here." The practical consequence: an answer sitting at that boundary is legitimately frightening — "if I were to see E, I'd be scared, because I know it just takes literally a feather of weight to move it too far."
- **Necessary means required, not guaranteeing.** Correcting Kevin's paraphrase: "don't think of it as saying if we had it, we would be effective. It's saying the only way to be effective is by having it. If we don't have it, it's guaranteed we're not going to be effective. But it doesn't mean that if it's there, we will be." The gloss he reached for: "to be Latin American you need to speak Spanish, but because you speak Spanish doesn't necessarily mean you're Latin American."
- **Principle questions ask for a criterion, not a fact.** "We're looking for that criteria. We're not looking for a fact. We're looking for a criteria." The criterion supplies the missing rule: "being part of South America means you have to be from one of these countries. That's what we're looking for."
- **The bridge test.** "The answer has to be a bridge from the evidence to the conclusion, and it has to hook onto both sides. Imagine you're taking this answer choice and putting it in between the premise and the conclusion." Turned into a procedure: restate as *if evidence, then conclusion*; verify the *if* latches onto the evidence; verify the *then* lands on the conclusion. "If there's no place where it can hook on, then we can't get on the train. And does the bridge take us to the outcome we want?"
- **Always restate a conditional as if-then.** "The reason I always make you say if-then is that you want to use if-then for all types of conditionals, so your brain has a consistent way of looking at them, versus 'only if,' 'only when.' Restructure it to your base currency, USD."
- **Check the contrapositive of your final candidate.** "When you get down to your last answer choice, this is a very good practice: have the conditional, have the contrapositive. See both. See if the *if* side of either attaches to any part of the evidence."
- **Contrapositive of an AND is an OR.** "Both means and. The contrapositive of and is or... at least one of these is not harmful." Kevin's original error on the pesticide question was exactly this.
- **Negating a comparative goes to "at least as," not to the reverse.** "If I want to negate the idea that Ashley is smarter than Alex, I don't have to say Alex is smarter than Ashley. The bare minimum requirement for negation is that Alex is at least as smart as her. All you have to prove is that she's not smarter than him. It doesn't mean you're proving he's smarter than her." Restated as a rule: "the negation of one party being more than the other isn't that the second party is now more than the first. That's too far."
- **In sufficient assumption, strong and general is good.** "Don't worry about it being too strong. This is good to be strong." A more general *if* side is actively preferred because it guarantees coverage: "if the stimulus is a subset of the if part, that's perfect, that's amazing. You want that." His test case: evidence says taking the LSAT gets you into law school; an answer saying taking *any test* gets you into law school covers it. "We've covered our grounds and then some. We have leftovers. We want leftovers. We're only scared if they're undercutting us."
- **The specific-versus-general trap.** Kevin's own example: stimulus says drinking a lot of beer causes liver cancer; the answer choices offer *alcohol* and *liquid*. He used to pick alcohol because it felt closer in concept. The correct instinct is the more general term, provided the evidence is a subset of it.
- **The LSAT's standard trick on linking questions is to invert the hook.** "They might go and say 'if you're morally obliged' — we don't care about that. We want the *if* to be the 'likely to assume' part. Don't let them mix that up. Or they might say 'if you're not likely to assume' — we don't care, we don't know that. That's all they're going to do."
- **Abandon an answer choice the moment the *if* fails.** "If it doesn't hook onto the first part, don't even finish the answer choice. This is why you want to be efficient... when you prephrase, it gives you speed, because instantly they'll be disqualified."
- **Prediction over elimination.** "If you just try to find the answer by process of elimination, you don't have to learn the skills required for that question. You're just choosing the answer based off feeling the vibes out. You want to force yourself to have the prediction and say if this then this, then look for the link."

## Teaching moves

- **Diagnose from a pattern across questions, not one miss.** Joshua only names the diagnosis at the very end, after three questions have shown it: "the answer choices for these linking questions, which is where I'm seeing the most repetitive issue here from the beginning, is that the *if* part has to hook on."
- **Analogies drawn from the student's own identity, reused across the hour.** Kevin is Latin American, so the whole conditional apparatus runs on Latin America, Argentina, and Spanish, and the same analogy is recalled twice more later ("back to the analogy, just to rehash this, because now we can work with it"). Kevin supplies "Argentina" himself when Joshua pauses for a country.
- **Praise the correct execution even when the answer is wrong.** "You can do that. That's fine what you just did... you did it well actually, but it's just kind of harder to conceptualize." The negation was performed correctly; the advice is to stop using the tool in that context, not to stop trusting himself.
- **Force him to reconstruct the argument in plain speech before touching answers.** On the home-seller question he refused three successive answers as too vague — "that's very broad," "we need to be more clear here," "get more specific here" — until Kevin produced the concrete version, then reinforced it with a concrete scene: "imagine I come to your house and you're trying to sell me your house and I see a fridge. I have to either tell you the fridge is not included or I have to just not have the fridge there."
- **Build the credited answer before showing the answers.** "So that's what we want. Now you have our answer... you have basically done the job. Now the answer is going to be easy."
- **Warn about the trap before opening the choices.** He named both inversions the test would attempt before Kevin read a single option.
- **Let the student walk into the objection and then flip it.** He asked whether "any belongings" covers "large appliances." Kevin resisted — "any belongings and appliances are two different things... appliances are a subset of any belongings" — and Joshua turned the objection into the answer: "which is funny, because you kind of — exactly. It's perfect. You said something very powerful there."
- **Name the thing Kevin already saw as the hard part.** On the beer/liquid trap: "weirdly, you were trying to say that's why it's not the right answer, but you saw it. It's not like you missed it. That's the hardest part, seeing it. The easy part is knowing what to do when you see it."
- **Mirror the answer back verbatim until the match becomes obvious.** "I honestly, when I said it back to you, I just read the answer twice to you. You were like, yeah, agreed."
- **Concede difficulty rather than pushing through.** On the comparative contrapositive: "it's tricky. So like the negation of one more than the other..." He backed up to the original form when Kevin stalled ("let's go back to the original form. What was the original form?") rather than repeating the hard direction.
- **Hand back the remaining answer choices as homework rather than burning session time.** "I'll let you go through the other options on your own and we'll recap to see if any need attention."

## Worked examples

- **Necessary assumption: the international police force.** The argument runs: to be effective law, there must be an effective enforcement mechanism; there is no international police force; therefore so-called international law is not effective law. The unstated jump treats "international police force" as one-for-one with "effective enforcement mechanism."

  Kevin chose **B** ("if an international police force were established, then international law would become effective") and was uneasy, "because the sufficient doesn't guarantee the necessary." He had even negated it. Joshua's kill: the argument's premise is that there *is no* police force, so a rule keyed on the force existing never fires. "We don't care what happens when there is one. Why would that matter to us?"

  **D** ("the primary purpose is to enforce the laws...") dies on *primary*. Kevin killed it himself: "the most important, we don't know. There could be other purposes."

  Credited answer **E** ("only an international police force could effectively enforce international law"). Kevin had eliminated it on sight because *only* felt too strong — "there could be other effective enforcement mechanisms" — the classic reading of *only* as extremeness rather than as a conditional operator. Joshua rebuilt the argument's logic until Kevin produced the assumption himself, then corrected the direction: not "if we had it, it would be effective," but "it has to be there. It's a requirement." He also pointed out that B contains *only* too, so strength was never the differentiator: "B and E are equally [strong], because only is a placeholder term for if-then. It just doesn't actually activate, doesn't hook onto any part of it."

- **Principle sufficient assumption: pesticides T400 and ENZ.** T400 was thought extremely harmful and was banned; ENZ was thought harmless and was not. Recent studies find ENZ causes greater environmental harm than T400. Conclusion: if the studies are accurate, ENZ should be banned and T400 legalized. Joshua's framing: "we want a principle that argues in favor of consistency. Whatever you do to one, you have to do to the other."

  Kevin chose **C**: "two pesticides should both be illegal only if both are harmful to the environment." Restated: if both illegal, then both harmful. Kevin botched the contrapositive by not converting the AND, and Joshua walked him to it: "if at least one of these is not harmful, then they can't all be illegal." Kevin: "Oh, I see where I got it wrong. I did the CP wrong." Second, independent defect: neither direction hooks onto the evidence. The stimulus tells us all three pesticides are harmful, and C says nothing about what follows from harmfulness — "we don't know anything about when it's harmful. We just know when it's not harmful and when it shouldn't be illegal."

  Credited answer: "one pesticide should be legal and another illegal only if the former is less harmful to the environment than the latter." As if-then: if A is legal and B is illegal, then A must be less harmful than B. Contrapositive: if A is at least as harmful as B, you cannot have A legal and B illegal. That contrapositive hooks straight onto the evidence (ENZ is at least as harmful as T400) and lands on the conclusion.

  Kevin's stated reason for having passed on it is the interesting part: "I felt like it was in a range, meaning it's either harmful or not... this one says it's less harmful, so that means it's still harmful nonetheless." Joshua called this "close" and redirected to the contrapositive mechanics. Kevin then asked the good question — "how would you do the contrapositive of these ranges?" — which produced the Ashley/Alex negation rule. His reaction once it landed: "It's funny, because it's very straightforward once you clear it out."

- **Sufficient assumption: home sellers and appliances.** Sellers are legally entitled to remove anything that is not a permanent fixture; since many prospective buyers are *likely to assume* large appliances are included, sellers who intend to keep them are morally obliged either to remove them before showing the home or to indicate that they are not included.

  Joshua had Kevin build the target bridge cold: *if* buyers are likely to assume large appliances are included with the purchase, *then* the seller must remove them or say they are not included. He flagged *since* as the evidence marker, and stressed that the evidence only says *likely* — a weak trigger the answer must be able to accommodate.

  Credited answer C (as read): if home sellers will be keeping any belongings that prospective buyers might assume would be included with the purchase of the home, they are morally obliged to indicate clearly that those belongings are not included. Two things it does right, both of which Kevin initially read as wrong: *any belongings* is broader than *large appliances* (good, because coverage), and *might assume* is weaker than *likely to assume* (good, because "likely means more than 50%, so that would guarantee coverage"). And on the outcome side, "indicate clearly that those belongings are not included" is what both branches of the conclusion's disjunction are *for* — removing the fridge before the showing is itself a way of making it clear.

  Kevin then killed **E** unaided ("if sellers have indicated in some way that a large appliance is included, then they are morally obliged not to remove it") on exactly the right grounds: the *if* side names an outcome, not the evidence. He connected it to the police force question on his own: "kind of like that international police question... it's the same concept." Joshua: "You're exactly right though."

## Student state

- **In blind review discipline and doing it properly.** He arrives having already reviewed, and can usually say what went wrong before Joshua does: "I did review it earlier. I did understand why I got it wrong. It's because I didn't do the contrapositive of the answer choice."
- **Actively working to unlearn heuristics.** "I'm trying to get rid of the bad habits." Specifically: eliminating any answer containing *only* on sight, and picking the closer-in-concept term over the more general one.
- **His central mechanical error is not checking whether the *if* side hooks the evidence.** Joshua identifies this as "the most repetitive issue here from the beginning," visible across all three questions.
- **Secondary errors, both fixable and both about conditional mechanics:** contrapositive of an AND, and negation of a comparative.
- **He distrusts conditional answer choices as a class**, and distrusts strong language as a class, and both distrusts cost him the right answer at least once each in this session.
- **Comparatives and ranges are a genuine conceptual gap**, not a slip. He asked the question directly and said "crap, this is hard. I wouldn't know, to be honest."
- **He connects across questions when the mechanism repeats**, which is the best sign in the session — he independently mapped the home-seller E trap onto the police-force B trap.
- **Falls back on process of elimination**, which Joshua targeted directly at the close as the thing preventing skill acquisition rather than merely costing points.
- Aware of the necessary/sufficient boundary as a source of anxiety before Joshua explained it: "JY explained that it could be also sufficient and necessary, and that trips me out a little bit."
- Doing timed drills between sessions and tracking accuracy.
- Uses "NA," "SA," and "CP" as working shorthand; comfortable with the vocabulary.
