# Malaya Jules, 2025-10-10

First session after Malaya sat the October LSAT. Joshua opened with the accommodations pitch, then spent most of the ninety minutes rebuilding her Reading Comprehension approach around passage structure rather than detail retention, working two passages live, and closed by laying out a full weekly study schedule for the November retake.

## LSAT concepts

- **Read for structure, not detail.** The core claim of the session: "knowing the nitty-gritty details is one of the most devious things that the LSAT forces you to believe you should be fully focusing on because it's really a structure." The passage is not going anywhere; you need to know where information lives, not what it says.
- **The C-E-E-R move framework** (Joshua calls it "the SER method," spelling it out as "C E R"): every sentence, paragraph, and passage is the author making one of four moves — **comparison, explanation, evaluation, recommendation**. "Instead of knowing what he's saying, you want to categorize into a move. Why is the author telling me this information." Applies at three levels: whole passage type, paragraph move, individual sentence.
- **First line / last line reading.** You can recover a passage's arc by reading only the first and last sentence of each paragraph. Joshua demonstrated it on a passage he had never seen, correctly inferring the middle content: "we didn't have to read the content in between, we know we can almost fill in the information."
- **Main point in RC is one line, exactly like LR.** "It's just like LR, it's one specific component, it's not the whole passage." A main point answer may reference other parts of the passage, but "the one thing that is non-negotiable is that it has to be at the center of it." In the Chinese art passage the main point was the sentence starting *Ironically*.
- **Prephrase before reading answer choices.** "More than anything, RC, you want to have a really clear prephrase." Without one, "you're going to find yourself wasting so much time and getting tripped up because the questions are designed to trip you up."
- **Treat every RC answer choice as a necessary assumption.** "All it takes is one word, one segment of it to be wrong to dismiss it." Every answer choice must trace to a specific line.
- **Weak language wins.** "The answer choices have to sound like they're coming from a reasonable place. All of the passages are real people... they're never going to be extreme. Never look for answers that are extreme." Malaya's own version: "just choose the weakest answer... don't choose 'requires' or 'will.'"
- **Highlighting discipline.** Highlighting only works if you trust it. "If I see everything highlighted it kind of reduces the value of highlighting... your brain will naturally turn off."
- **Idea buckets.** Rather than remembering five items, remember that they are all "grocery list material" and where the list is. "If I need to figure out a detail, I can just — I know where to find that."
- **The lawyer-reading-a-contract analogy.** "You're trying to read like a lawyer. If somebody gave you a contract you wouldn't waste your time trying to memorize the contract details... you'd say, oh yeah, I know where everything is."
- **Two cavemen fighting.** For "which would the author agree with" questions, boil the passage to sides: "think of it like two cavemen fighting almost — good, bad, bad, good."
- **Annotation over summary.** Instead of writing paragraph summaries, diagram the move: a numbered letter per paragraph (C for context, V for viewpoint, V-with-an-X for a viewpoint attacking another). Joshua credited the RC Hero (Dustin) system and said he used it himself for a while.
- **LR structure imported into RC.** "You can literally use LR premise, conclusion, intermediate conclusion — instead of doing that for a full RC you can do that for each paragraph."
- **The five passage archetypes** (Malaya's 7Sage vocabulary, which Joshua accepted): critique/debate, phenomenon/hypothesis, problem/analysis, spotlight, and one more she could not name.
- **LR difficulty curve.** Questions do not get progressively harder. "Between question 11 to 19 you'll find the trickiest," and Joshua pushed the hardest band later still: "the hardest questions are 16ish, between 16 to maybe even upwards to 23."
- **Necessary assumption is timing-friendly.** Malaya: "you can only do two things here — make sure it's true, and if I negate it, it falls apart." Joshua agreed and noted the type rewards heavy investment: "once you really figure them out... I get where the traps are going to be, I see why that would be a tempting answer."

## Teaching moves

- **Interview the existing method before replacing it.** Joshua spent nearly half an hour asking Malaya to narrate her own highlighting and summary system before offering anything: "I want to see if there's anything I can do rather than just trying to change everything you're doing now. That's why I'm asking a lot of questions. I was just going to throw in things to maybe optimize if it works." He explicitly withheld his own view — "I want to bite my [tongue], I won't say anything" — so her process would come through uncontaminated.
- **Watch her work live, silently.** He had her share her screen and read a passage as if he were not there: "don't read it out loud because I want you to treat it as though I'm not here."
- **Solve a passage he has never seen, in front of her.** "This is an exercise I like to do with students... I actually haven't even seen this passage." The demonstration is the argument: if he can reconstruct the passage from first and last lines cold, the detail-memorization instinct is refuted.
- **Ask the question that exposes the missing framework.** When Malaya described looking for "the purpose of the paragraph," he pushed: "What are the roles that could potentially serve? Is there a finite limit of numbers of roles?" She said no. That gap is what C-E-E-R fills.
- **Reward the good answer explicitly.** "Oh, you're cooking right now. This is good. You're giving me very good answers." He validated her diagnosis rather than replacing it: "your system is pretty good right now... it's about streamlining it to make it more efficient."
- **Import the LR skill she already has.** Rather than teaching RC main point from scratch, he asked "what would you do for a main point question in the LR section?" She answered correctly, and he said: "So why does that change here?"
- **Don't accept a category answer as final.** When she called the last sentence an evaluation, he corrected gently but drew the boundary: "evaluation is usually evaluating an ongoing problem... they're giving us more of an explanation. They're telling us a state of affairs." He also warned against overworking the classification: "you don't want to waste time trying to force yourself to see exactly — you have a bucket, you want to keep it."
- **Escalate difficulty on purpose.** For the second passage he asked her to pick one he had not seen and to make it hard: "I actually want you to choose a harder passage. If you can choose a level five, that would be good."
- **The accommodations conversation as a set piece.** Joshua has delivered it so often he apologized for the speed: "I've had this conversation probably once every two or three days for the past three months, so I just kind of have to get it out." The structure: (1) reframe from advantage to disadvantage — "not getting accommodations isn't a benefit anymore, it's just that not having accommodations essentially pulls you down"; (2) kill the application-stigma objection — "the LSAC is not allowed to disclose that your test was accommodated, so your score is exactly like anybody else's score"; (3) reframe the test's design — "the test now is testing more or less on timing because they almost expect you to have more time... more power, not speed"; (4) social proof by numbers, including the number of his own students; (5) disclaim authority — "I'm not going to tell you what to do, that's completely your thing" — immediately followed by "kind of am."
- **Show, then withhold, the score claim.** He offered a thought experiment rather than a promise: "if I gave you another 35 minutes, 70 minutes, what do you think would be the difference in your score?"
- **Reframe running out of practice tests as a non-problem.** "It doesn't matter, because if you do every test and you understand why every answer is right, you're not going to [struggle]. I've never seen somebody say 'oh I did all the tests, I understood every single question, and I just did poorly.'"
- **Name the emotional register and use it.** When Malaya said "I never want to feel that way again," Joshua answered: "And then this is your villain arc, right?"

## Worked examples

- **RC passage: the Cultural Revolution and Chinese art.** Malaya read it live using her own method (yellow = content, orange = perspective/groups, pink = author's attitude, plus a two-to-three-word "low-res summary" per paragraph). Her paragraph labels: "background / art by politics," "scar art rebellion," "scar art co-opted, native soil movement later romanticized." Her main point: "a description of the evolution of Chinese art after the revolution... the impact of politics on Chinese art."

  Joshua then reran it first-line/last-line only. P1 first line: the Cultural Revolution "has had lasting repercussions on Chinese art" (an explanation of a state of affairs). P1 last line: the only reality artists could portray was one "thoroughly colored and distorted by political ideology" — the mechanism of enforcement. P2 first line: "Ironically, the same set of requirements that constricted artistic expression... has had the opposite effect since." P2 last line: scar artists chose "the private, mundane, and the particular" and "the bleak realities of modernization" against revolutionary realism's demand for the public, monumental, and perfect. P3 first/last: scar art co-opted for political purposes and abandoned, then native soil painting "trivialized by a tendency to romanticize certain qualities of rural Chinese society in order to appeal to Western galleries and collectors."

  The correction: the main point is not the survey of political influence but the *irony* — "the unexpected, unintended consequences of this restriction on art expression in China." On the title question Malaya had eliminated every choice, disliking "unforeseen" because she read it as *undiscovered*. Joshua's fix: "unforeseen like unexpected." She conceded she had overlooked the credited answer entirely. She also noted she had spent 47 minutes on the passage in-session.

  On the purpose-of-first-paragraph question, C-E-E-R did the work directly: the paragraph is an explanation, so "let's find anything that does not talk about explaining" — hypothesis-presenting and demonstration answers die immediately.

- **RC passage: soil restoration after agricultural overproduction (level five, chosen by Malaya, unseen by Joshua).** Read in roughly three to four minutes. Her main point: "the rehabilitation of land damaged by agricultural overproduction can be accelerated by means of a two-pronged strategy aimed at restoring biological diversity." Joshua's version: restoring "a balance that's natural in the soils of microorganisms as well as a diverse spectrum of species of plants." Passage classification: evaluation, arguably evaluation/recommendation — "they don't say we should, but they do say the best way to do this is this." Structure: problem, study, implications. On the organization question she prephrased and matched "a problem is presented, a study addressing the problem is described, and a course of action" against distractors she rejected for single wrong words ("a study is evaluated" — the passage does not start with a study; "modification of current agricultural practices" — "it's not like we're trying to produce food here, it's a restoration of soil"). Joshua's takeaway: "your system is pretty good right now... it's about streamlining it."

## Student state

- Just sat the October LSAT; had cancelled her August score. Hoping for "at least like a 163" but worried October came in below the cancelled August score.
- **The split is stark.** LR is strong: "I probably didn't get any lower than like a minus five for each of them," finishing all 25 questions with six minutes to spare. Joshua: "You steamrolled it." RC is the crisis: she called the October RC "my worst RC section in the history of sections."
- Historically RC was her *stronger* section with no method at all — minus eight untrained. She now gets minus eight with the full 7Sage system, which she flagged as the problem: "today I get a minus eight and I'm using all the strategies."
- **RC timing is the leak, not comprehension.** Standard plan is first two passages in 15 minutes, 10 minutes each for three and four; October broke it because passages got progressively harder, which she called atypical. She is trying to compress reading time from four minutes to three.
- **She over-writes.** Self-diagnosed: "I know I'm such a bad writer... I feel like if I don't write it down it will be lost, and it's not." Joshua pushed back that she writes because she is a good writer. She wants two-to-five-word paragraph summaries that work as "a road map" and "skeleton" with no granular detail.
- **Stated/implied questions are her worst RC type** and she knows why: "I'm very rarely touching the passage again... the passage is right there."
- Doubts the 7Sage RC method's front-loaded reading: "they really encourage a lot of time upfront reading and then the questions you just speed through," which no longer matches an RC section full of application questions.
- **Does not prephrase enough in LR either.** "I don't prephrase enough... I want to have a gap in mind." Her plan is to read stimuli without looking at question stems and name the gap cold.
- LR method she trusts: first 15 questions in 15 minutes, trust instinct on the first 10 ("they're not trying to trick me there"), flag anything under 100% confidence, return at the end. Credits earlier flaw work with making this possible: "I wouldn't have been able to do the first 15 in 15 minutes if I weren't like, oh that's that flaw, that's that flaw."
- **Not accommodated, and had never considered it.** "I've never had accommodations and it just felt hard to get." Her prior belief: "I just assume that the people who need it need it and the people who don't don't." She was visibly rattled to learn she is the outlier.
- Enrolled in a 7Sage prep course, Sundays 3-6.
- Reports the October RC as brutal across the board; Joshua corroborated from five or six other students, plus an unusual density of necessary assumption questions ("literally one section had like 10") and tricky point-of-disagreement questions.

## Data-quality note

The Gemini summary at the top of the source misstates the framework as "the 'SER' method (Comparison, Explanation, Evaluation, Recommendation)" — four moves under a three-letter acronym. The transcript shows Joshua himself calling it "the SER method" and then spelling it "C E R," so the confusion originates with him, not the notetaker. The four moves are the stable content; the acronym is unreliable and should not be treated as a fixed name. The Gemini summary also renders the Cultural Revolution as "1996 to 1976" (transcript: 1966 to 1976).
