# Fiorella Meyer, 2025-11-17

## LSAT concepts
- **Sufficient assumption** — find the answer that, plugged back into the argument, makes the conclusion "airtight." Solve it up front: "your goal is to know the answers before you look at the answers, verbatim, at least as close as you can."
- **Three-step method** — 1) find the conclusion, 2) find the evidence and see where it leaves off, 3) design an if-then link: if the last point of evidence is true, then the conclusion is true.
- **Validity** — the conclusion must be guaranteed by the evidence, impossible to falsify while the evidence holds.
- **The new-concept / "foreign DNA" rule** — any concept in the conclusion not present in the evidence must appear in the correct answer. 100% reliable for sufficient assumption, roughly 85 to 90% for necessary assumption.
- **The core LSAT trick** — every conclusion contains an unsupported component, and the test swaps it with a similar-sounding idea from the evidence. Sometimes a term shift, sometimes a subtler shift of scope.
- **Intermediate conclusion** — a conclusion that also serves as evidence for the main conclusion. It becomes the point you must bridge from.
- **Argument structure indicators** — the "fabs" (for, after all, because, since) signal that what precedes them is a conclusion. "And" joins two statements, and two joined ideas cannot be the conclusion, so the unjoined statement is.
- **Conditional logic** — "essential" and "required" mark the necessary condition. "Without" behaves like "unless": negate one idea and make it the sufficient condition.
- **Answer strength** — for sufficient assumption, stronger is better. "Some" answers are too weak when the conclusion claims certainty.
- **Comparison as a trap signal** — when a stimulus introduces two groups, expect a faulty comparison.

## Teaching moves
- **Opens by naming her progress concretely** rather than generically: "I remember when you were starting off... now we're talking about 18, that's the 160s range." Adds the reassurance that untimed performance still counts: "the horsepower is there. The hardest part is getting to the 18. It's not the timing part."
- **Prosecutor framing, escalated to something more vivid**: "we're looking to almost rig the game. We're looking to be like dirty prosecutors and we're trying to introduce dirty evidence that could guarantee the conclusion to be true."
- **Train-track metaphor** for the argument: evidence lays track that takes you close but not all the way; the sufficient assumption is the missing stretch to the destination. Reused later as flight legs (premises, then intermediate conclusion as "the layover," then main conclusion as the end point).
- **Defibrillator image for answer strength**: "you're trying to supercharge the argument. So the stronger it is, the better."
- **Closed-circuit metaphor** for the completed argument: "if the circuit's open, we're going to have a problem with the light bulb turning on. If we close that circuit, it's a free-flowing electrical circuit."
- **Deliberately withholds the cheat code until after she has struggled**: "I'm going to hold off on that. I'm going to let you do the questions first. Then you'll see why it's important... whenever I teach it prior, it always goes over people."
- **"Read like sociopaths"**: "if it's not stated, I don't take anything, even the most basic truths of this world, to be true." Paired with the warning that "common sense will almost betray you sometimes in this test."
- **Parents-and-child genetics metaphor for validity**, recalled from an earlier session: "the genetic makeup of the kid has to come from one of the two parents or both. The intersection of the premises is where validity lives." Anything else in the conclusion is **"foreign DNA."**
- **Magician imagery for the swap**: "slight of hand," "swapping the cups... one of the cups has the ball under it." Also calls the unsupported term a **"dangling variable."**
- **Horses-and-animals illustration of scope shift**: evidence about horses, conclusion about animals, or animals with manes. "They seem like they're one for one, but are they truly one for one? Is it possible where an animal with a mane is not a horse? Maybe like a lion."
- **Credits her for reaching the insight and tells her what it means**: when she asked whether the conclusion always contains something unsupported, he stopped and elevated it, "if you can truly understand what you just asked me, and I think you do, you understand the LSAT... most people will never understand what you just said."
- **Self-aware about the gaming**: "I hate doing this because it's kind of degenerate what we just did here," while still teaching that elimination by new concept alone typically gets you to a coin flip.
- **The last-premise tip**: the premise immediately before the conclusion usually contains the idea being swapped, so bridge from there. Then he immediately teaches the exception on the hardest question, where the argument is inverted and the last evidence point is elsewhere.
- **Restates the method through her**, not at her, before each new question: "just to rehash, I kind of ranted there a little bit... what are we looking for first?"
- **Escalates difficulty deliberately and says why**, with warmth: "you're making this look a bit too easy right now, so I have to. You can't show me up like this, I don't like it."
- **Handles a wrong sufficient/necessary call with a test question rather than a correction.** When Fiorella flipped the conditions on "trust is essential to happiness," he asked "what is required for what?" and let her find it. "Think of require and necessary as meaning the same thing."
- **Suggests personalizing conditionals to make them concrete**: "if you want to turn it into a personalized if-then, put yourself into the story. If I'm happy then I have to have the ability to trust other people."
- **Uses highlighter color on screen** to track which idea is sufficient and which is necessary across chained conditionals.
- **Explains where difficulty comes from** so she can anticipate it: "the harder questions are going to bring heavy conditionals into them. That's how they add up the difficulty, by throwing in a lot of conditionals, and your goal is to link them."
- **Rejects a repeat-the-evidence answer on principle**: "if we just repeat something stated, it's a waste of our time. It doesn't move our argument forward."

## Worked examples
- **Sufficient assumption, controlled information and economic crises.** Same item Joshua ran with Moses the day before. Fiorella was between B and C and picked B correctly before analysis. Path: the new concept in the conclusion is "more frequent economic crises," which the argument tries to swap with the population being denied vital information about factors determining their welfare. Scan for the new concept eliminates A, C, D, and E outright. B states that economic crises become more frequent as the information available to the population about factors determining its welfare decreases, which is exactly the bridge. Answer B.
- **Sufficient assumption, art criticism.** Many artists claim critics find it easier to write about art they dislike; whether or not that hypothesis is correct, most art criticism is devoted to artworks that fail to satisfy the critic; hence most art criticism is devoted to works other than the greatest works of art. New concept: "the greatest works of art." A through C lack it and are cut. Fiorella articulated the missing link herself, "if the artwork fails to satisfy the critic, then it's not a great work of art." E states that the greatest works of art inevitably satisfy all critics. Answer E. She had chosen E already, on the reasoning that D imported "long after the time of creation."
- **Sufficient assumption, free speech.** A government ought to protect and encourage free speech, because free speech is an activity conducive to a healthy nation and thus is in the best interest of its people. Structure work: "and" joins two premises; "and thus is in the best interest of its people" is an **intermediate conclusion**, the layover, and counts as the last point of evidence. Bridge from best interest of its people to what a government ought to do. Answer A: anything in the best interest of the people ought to be protected and encouraged by a nation's government. C was rejected as out of scope (universal human rights); another choice spoke of the interest of the government rather than of its people.
- **Sufficient assumption, trust and happiness (Joshua's "hardest look," ran roughly fifteen minutes).** Conclusion: the ability to trust other people is essential to happiness. Located via the "for" indicator plus the "and" joining the two premises. Conditional work: "essential" makes the ability to trust the necessary condition, so happy → able to trust. Premise one, "without trust there can be no meaningful emotional connection to another human being," translates as no trust → no meaningful emotional connection, or equivalently meaningful emotional connection → trust. Premise two chains: no meaningful emotional connection → feel isolated. The evidence therefore stops at **isolation**, while the conclusion introduces the brand-new concept **happiness**. The bridge must connect isolation to happiness. B fails (never mentions isolation), C fails (no happiness, and merely restates the evidence), D fails (only "at least some," too weak for a certainty claim), E fails (proves emotional connection, not happiness). Answer A: no one who is feeling isolated can feel happy. Fiorella's initial pick had been B.
