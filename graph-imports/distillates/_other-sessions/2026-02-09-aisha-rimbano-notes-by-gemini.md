# Aisha Rimbano, 2026-02-09

## LSAT concepts
- **Main conclusion**: the entire session was main-conclusion identification, which Joshua frames as the foundation for every other question type.
- **Two-step conclusion test**: a statement is the conclusion only if (1) it is the author's opinion and (2) it is explicitly supported by another statement in the argument.
- **Argument structure**: premise, subconclusion (intermediate conclusion), main conclusion, and the surrounding non-argument material (background information, opposing viewpoints).
- **Indicator words / FABS**: "for, after all, because, since" always introduce a premise, and by proxy point backward at the conclusion.
- **Meaning match vs. word match**: the credited answer restates the meaning; word-for-word echoes are usually bait.
- **Opposing viewpoint**: when the first sentence is not the conclusion, expect it to be someone else's view that the author rebuts.

## Teaching moves
- **Rephrase and recall, line by line, out loud.** The core drill of the session: read a sentence, look away, restate it in your own words. "Ideally, not looking at what you read and rephrasing it into words you understand. It shouldn't deviate... we just want to simplify the language into the most natural English possible."
- **Labels unpronounceable content words to remove friction.** She stumbles on "Poinsettia"; he immediately says "we can call it P plus. We'll give it a label."
- **Teaches unknown vocabulary by valence first, not definition.** She does not know "indignant." He asks: "Does it sound positive or negative?" She says negative, and he rebuilds the sentence with "negative" substituted in.
- **Refuses to accept a rough gloss when precision matters.** She reads self-knowledge as "it's not good to think like that." He: "I wouldn't say good, close, but it's more so they're saying it's not the easiest knowledge to find out."
- **Reframes an easy answer as evidence the pre-work paid off.** She distrusts answer E because it matches too exactly. He: "That's because we did our homework. That's why it's too good to be true... once you do that, the rest of it becomes a highway. You just have to look for the answer you did to solve for up front."
- **Blocks answer-choice reasoning until the argument is understood.** When she jumps to D because "none of the other ones," he refuses to confirm: "tell me the argument in your own words before I say yes or no."
- **The first-sentence rule of thumb**, taught explicitly with the reason behind it: "either the first sentence is going to be the conclusion... they want you to think the last sentence is the conclusion because people expect it to be. It almost never will be for the main conclusion, because they expect you to think that. The conclusion is usually going to come prior to the evidence."
- **The FABS acronym**, credited to a former student: for, after all, because, since. Taught for its dual function: "it tells you that whatever is following is a premise for what was said prior to that word. So it's like an indirect conclusion indicator as well."
- **Names indicator words as the highest-return memorization**: "these indicator words are your best friend. They'll make your life a million times easier... you want to ideally tattoo them on you."
- **Justifies why main conclusion comes first in the curriculum**: "these are the most important types of questions to really get really good at because it follows you with every other question type. If you cannot do this perfectly, you'll get slammed on every question. It's going to feel like a free-for-all."
- **Progressive release of support.** Q1 fully guided, Q2 "I'm not going to help you in this next one, you'll do this out loud but I'll just be in the background," Q3 "I'm not going to intervene at all," with a reminder of the first-sentence heuristic before she starts.

## Worked examples
- **Q1, Poinsettia plant safety (main conclusion).** It has long been believed that people with children or pets should keep the plant out of their homes. Although this belief has been encouraged by child-rearing books that list the plant as poisonous and therefore dangerous, it is mistaken. Research has shown conclusively that the plant poses no risk for pets or children. Aisha correctly identified the research line as the conclusion and the "this belief is mistaken" line as the support. Answer E, a near-exact restatement, correct.
- **Q2, self-knowledge and desires (main conclusion).** Most people are indignant at the suggestion that they are not reliable authorities about their real wants. Such self-knowledge, however, is not the easiest kind to acquire. Indeed, acquiring it often requires hard and even potentially risky work. To avoid such effort, people unconsciously convince themselves that they want what society says they should want. Aisha picked D (people avoid making difficult decisions). Correct answer E: people are not necessarily reliable authorities about what they really want, the first sentence's embedded claim, supported by everything after it. Joshua walked the support chain backward to show the last line supports the third, which supports the second, which supports the first. She objected that E contains "necessarily," which does not appear in the stimulus, and he used that to teach meaning match over word match.
- **Q3, "the trial was worthwhile" (main conclusion).** Structure established using FABS: the final statement is a premise (introduced by "after all"), the middle statement is a subconclusion (introduced by "since"), and "the trial was worthwhile" is the main conclusion. Aisha identified the conclusion and picked D correctly on her own.
