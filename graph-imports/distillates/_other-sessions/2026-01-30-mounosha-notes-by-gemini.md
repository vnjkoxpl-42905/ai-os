# Mounusha Nunna, 2026-01-30

## LSAT concepts
- **Flaw**: both questions were level-5 flaw questions, drilled as the session's whole focus.
- **Sufficient vs. necessary**: the correct answer to Q2 ("confuses a sufficient condition with a required condition"). Joshua calls it "the most popular fallacy in history of the LSAT."
- **Conditional logic**: "if" and especially "only if" as indicators; noticing them is the trigger to look for sufficient/necessary.
- **Percentages vs. absolute vs. relative amounts**: you can never reach one of the three from the other two. Percentage evidence can only support a percentage conclusion.
- **Equivocation fallacy**: what "ambiguity in one of its terms" points to. A term must have two meanings; conceptual vagueness about which survey is not equivocation.
- **Circular reasoning**: recognized behind "contains a premise that is implausible unless the conclusion is presumed to be true."
- **Causation from correlation**: what "infers a particular causal relation from a correlation that could be explained in a variety of other ways" points to.
- **Sampling / representativeness**: taking a sample is not itself a flaw. The issue is sample validity.
- **Method of reasoning**: classifying each argument as conditional, causal, or comparative before touching answer choices.
- **Answer choice styles**: descriptive vs. objection vs. assumption styles, with a fallacy interleaved behind the framing.

## Teaching moves
- **Do it cold first, then recap together.** Opens by giving her a minute and a half on the question alone, has her read the stimulus out loud, asks for her answer before any discussion.
- **Asks the conclusion first, backwards from the stimulus**: "I'll actually ask you backwards. What's the conclusion here?" Then "in your own words, what does that conclusion really mean?"
- **Tests whether the evidence guarantees the conclusion**: "let's see if based off the evidence explicitly, does it give us the ability to confirm that's true with a certainty?"
- **A structural cue about "and"**: "especially when we see that word 'and,' it almost always entails a set of two premises that lead to a conclusion. If you see the word 'and,' it's never going to be included into the conclusion."
- **Corrects his own framing mid-explanation** rather than letting a loose analogy stand: floats correlation/causation for the smog overlap, then withdraws it, "I wouldn't say correlation causation. It's not the right way to put it. It's the overlap. It's unverifiable overlap."
- **Reads answer-choice grammar as a tell**: "from" or "by" always introduces the premise, "infers" introduces the conclusion. Matching the argument's conclusion to the evidence is "paramount."
- **Names the goal as pattern recognition, comparing it to language fluency**: "it's almost like learning a language that also likes to speak... I don't have to think through that answer choice too long. Pattern recognition will show me that's circular."
- **Downplays memorizing names when the student already sees the mechanism**: "you know, it doesn't really matter because you knew it, you know the problem. The naming is not so important."
- **Gives a time-pressure fallback heuristic**: if you are stuck on a flaw question and see conditional indicator words, "if you want to just choose one and run, you'd be the safest to look for a sufficient versus necessary."
- **The core argument for why flaw questions should never be missed**: "the answer choices can never descriptively describe the stimulus wrong in any way... it doesn't require for you just to get the stimulus wrong. It requires for you to misunderstand the answer choice. So you have to fail at multiple levels to get a flaw question wrong."
- **Corollary: the answer choice can rescue you.** "if we don't have the perfect formulation of the answer for the stimulus, the answer choice can lead us towards understanding it by just matching it."
- **Eliminate before engaging**: "we want to eliminate all the ones that are wrong before we try to engage with the ones that could conceivably be right."
- **Zero tolerance on descriptive mismatch**: "If it's characterizing it wrong in any capacity, we eliminate it, even if it looks super attractive." On D: "that looks like it makes sense with the vibe of the stimulus, but the details are where it gets wacky." Prescribes eyes prodding back and forth between choice and argument.
- Illustrates equivocation with "dark" meaning evil vs. complexion, echoing the ambiguity-of-a-term test.
- **The horseshoe / U-curve observation**: the person who doesn't know what they're doing and the expert can land on B by the same route, one by blind pattern-match on conditionals, one by full analysis.
- **Frames level-5 practice as calibration for the real test**: after wrestling questions like these, level 3 and 4 flaw questions "become kind of ridiculously obvious... you've already been in the ring with questions that twisted every ounce of mental horsepower out of you."
- **Validates slow thinking as a good sign**: when she apologizes for taking long, "you're wrestling with this," and notes the questions were the hardest tier flaw ever gets.

## Worked examples
- **Q1, Hill View smog (flaw).** Premises: on some hot days the smog in Hill View reaches unsafe levels; on some hot days the wind blows into Hill View from the east. Conclusion: on some days when the wind blows from the east, the smog reaches unsafe levels. Flaw: the two "some hot days" sets share only the category "hot days," so an overlap between them is assumed, not established. Mounusha picked B (objection style) correctly and unprompted. A rejected as merely descriptive ("mistakes a condition that sometimes accompanies..."), flirting with sufficient/necessary without being it. C is equivocation (on "unsafe"), not occurring. D is circular. E is causation from correlation.
- **Q2, elected officials resigning (flaw).** Premises: in a survey, 50% of people polled believe elected officials should resign if indicted of a crime, whereas 35% believe they should resign only if convicted. Conclusion: more people believe officials should resign if indicted than believe they should resign if convicted. Problems catalogued: percentage evidence to an absolute/relative conclusion; unknown sample size and representativeness; unclear whether both figures come from the same survey; group overlap, since conviction requires prior indictment, and believing indictment suffices does not exclude also believing conviction suffices. Correct answer B, confusing a sufficient condition with a required condition, driven by the "only if" in the evidence versus the plain "if" in the conclusion. A rejected (sampling is not inherently a flaw). C rejected (ambiguity is conceptual, not a term with two meanings). D rejected on a single word: it says "a specific belief" but the conclusion is about two beliefs.
