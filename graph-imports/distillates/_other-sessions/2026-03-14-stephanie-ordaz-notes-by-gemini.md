# Stephanie Ordaz, 2026-03-14

A ground-up foundations session. This is the fullest recorded version of Joshua's argument-structure lesson.

## LSAT concepts

- **Argument**: on the LSAT an argument is only a premise plus a conclusion. Both must be present. "The LSAT has a very specific definition... These are words that we use commonly in a way that's very specific. That's a very common thing with the test."
- **Stimulus versus argument**: the stimulus is the passage; it *contains* the argument but is not all argument. Background information sits outside the argument. "Not every statement is a premise and conclusion."
- **Main conclusion**: the author's specific claim, one statement, never a summary. "That's not what the conclusion is on the LSAT. That's what you learn in school or in English class." Joshua's images for it: the tip of the iceberg, the verdict, "the case that the author is trying to fight for."
- **Premise**: simply the evidence. Premises support but are never themselves supported, and need nothing to support them.
- **Premises are never disputed.** "No matter if it says the sky is green, we take it to be true." True for every question type without exception. The only question is whether granting the evidence guarantees the conclusion.
- **Conclusions are always fought.** "We accept the evidence, fight the conclusion." Take the opposing side on every stimulus regardless of question type: "You are the opposing counsel."
- **Premise indicator words, FABS**: for, after all, because, since. A student taught Joshua this mnemonic years ago ("think about fabulous, but just FABS"). Whatever follows one of these words is a premise, immediately, no searching required.
- **Indicator words work in both directions.** The dual function most test-takers miss: an indicator word is "a bridge between a premise and a conclusion," so whatever came *immediately before* the word is the conclusion. "Most people that take the LSAT don't realize this."
- **Conclusion indicator words**: therefore, thus, hence, accordingly, it follows that, so.
- **Order is not fixed.** "Monica is never in the room while Batman is. Therefore, Monica must be Batman" and "Monica must be Batman, because she is never in the room while Batman is" are the same argument reversed. Do not assume conclusion follows evidence.
- **Two-step conclusion test.** Step one: the statement must be the author's opinion, not an empirically measurable fact. "Jonathan is six foot tall" is a brute fact; "Jonathan is a good basketball player" inserts opinion, because "what does good mean?" Step two: it must receive explicit support from at least one other statement. Both must be present.
- **Where the conclusion sits**: expect the first sentence. "Most people think it's at the end. They bait us into thinking that... so they put it at the beginning." Roughly 90 percent of the time it is not last.
- **Rebuttal-style conclusions**: whenever the author attributes a view to another group (some biologists, some economists), that view is raised only to be taken down. "They're never going to say, yeah, some biologists believe in evolution and I think they have a good point. Never, ever, ever. It's always going to be only present the other side just to take them down." In that structure the conclusion follows the rebuttal, usually after "but."
- **Referential words** (this, that, they) as a deliberate LSAT trap: "they're condensing this whole idea into the word *that*." Substitute the referent in as you read. "But that is not a fair evaluation" must be rewritten as "the criticism that his policies benefit only the rich is not a fair evaluation."
- **Meaning match, not word match**: the right answer "doesn't have to be a verbatim match, but has to be a meaning match." Two named trap types: extreme language ("valid measures cannot be devised" when the author never said that) and extrapolation, where students pick something the author would probably agree with rather than what he actually concluded. "It's a matching game. It's not a game of what do I interpret it as probably him agreeing with."
- **Strengthen and weaken preview**: strengthening is the defense lawyer's job, weakening is the prosecutor's, and both require finding the gap first. "You have to know where I'm weak before you can patch it up."

## Teaching moves

- **Diagnose the layman's definition first.** Opens with "when I say the word *argument*, what's the first thing that comes to mind?" She says "points of view," then defines conclusion as "the main point." He validates the colloquial answer, then names it as the exact trap: "I've done it so many times where students will naturally always think conclusion entails the summary or the general point. That's not what the conclusion is on the LSAT."
- **The wedding cake analogy**, his image for adequate support. The conclusion is a three-tier wedding cake, the premises are blocks. He role-plays handing it to her without her extending her arms, gets her to say it falls, then escalates: what if the blocks are only under one half? "It would fall through the other side." Payoff: "Conclusions can't keep themselves off the floor. Conclusions need to be supported by their premises to be worth anything."
- **The Ontario courtroom analogy** for granting premises. She is his lawyer, in Canada, and the judge disallows a piece of evidence. Would she tell the judge "that's not fair, we could do that in Texas"? "You have two choices. Either you accept the law of Ontario, or your other option would be to take Canada over horseback style. The more reasonable approach would probably be take the law of the land, and then beat the other side with their own rules, at their own game."
- **A deliberately absurd argument to isolate the target.** "Donald Trump is the best president ever, because he has beautiful hair." The point is that even here you do not attack the premise: "We're not here to fight whether his hair is beautiful or not. Our goal would specifically be to fight against the conclusion." Checks her politics first and jokes about it rather than assuming.
- **The engineer/architect frame** for reading: "you want to think of this more from the perspective of an engineer. It's not an interpretive exercise, it's more architectural, because you're trying to understand how the parts fit together." Later, for homework: "You'll be like a surgeon. You're going to be dissecting the argument."
- **The one universal question**: "Why should I believe that?" applied to every claim as you read. If there is an answer elsewhere in the stimulus, the claim you asked about is the conclusion and the answer is the premise. If nothing answers it, the claim is a premise.
- **Rephrase every complete thought, out loud, in your own words.** "We're not reading to sound out words. We're reading to be able to comprehend." Say it "as though you're telling it to a friend that's not studying for the LSAT... the more you can dumb it down" the better, because that is what saves you on dense science material. A complete thought can be half a sentence; a comma often marks one.
- **Cover the screen to force real recall.** He physically hides the stimulus while she rephrases. "You're going to look away and rephrase it, or you're going to pause your screen like I did. You're going to compare what your retelling was to what was actually said. And you'll be shocked how that will solve a lot of your problems." He flags it in advance: "I'll be annoying at first, but you get into the habit of forcing yourself to check yourself."
- **Prove the point with her own words.** After she paraphrases the publisher stimulus correctly with the screen hidden, he reflects it back: "It's interesting, because when I took away the screen you were able to articulate it perfectly. You said the conclusion, you said the evidence, and you said it intuitively, which is a good sign." Same move at the end with "unjust": he hears her say the word and sends her to find it in the choices.
- **Locate the answers on the correct side of the screen.** "People always think the answers to their questions are on this side of the screen. No, your answers are always here [the stimulus]. If you crack this part, the rest of it is easy sailing. But when you run into the answer choices without knowing this, good luck. You're just shooting in the dark."
- **Cut unmemorizable detail immediately.** "Numbers and names, you don't want to even try to remember them. Who McKin is doesn't matter. You can put anybody's name in there."
- **Decode intimidating vocabulary by context and valence.** When "economic well-being" stalls her, he does not define it. He splits it: what does "economic" sound like? Money. "Well-being"? Overall well-being. "Whenever you see words that are out there, think about what the word means in context. Is it a positive thing, a negative thing, what are the relative terms around it?"
- **Accept a coarse student paraphrase and use it.** She summarizes the publisher's evidence as "because it's the new year and everybody wants to be a fitness baddie." He does not clean it up: "Exactly. Right on."
- **Name the stakes of the foundational skill.** "If you cannot do this, everything else will fall. You might get questions right and get questions wrong, but it'll be like chaos. You won't know why you got it right, you won't know how to repeat it, and if you get it wrong you won't know why."
- **The cookie-jar alibi story** to motivate attacking before defending. A future client tells her "they caught me with my hands in the cookie jar," crumbs in his beard. "You need to be able to say, that's not a good look. But before you can defend me, you need to know where I'm weak, because they're going to bring up this situation, that situation. You have to know where the gaps are before you can patch it up."
- **Correct himself openly** when he says "conclusion" instead of "premise": "Completely ignore what I just said."
- **Reassure ahead of a heavy assignment**: "It's going to be quite a bit, so don't feel overwhelmed. It's going to be a lot of stuff you're going to look at, but just follow the process and you should be good to go."
- **Substantial off-topic rapport building** at the close: Texas versus Canada, ethnicity, her fiance's Venezuelan flag, where to move in Canada. He runs the session well past the material into ordinary conversation.

## Worked examples

- **Publisher / New Year fitness books (main conclusion).** "The new year is approaching, and with it the seasonal demand for books on exercise and fitness. We must do whatever it takes to ship books in that category on time. Our competitors have demonstrated a high level of organization and we cannot afford to be outsold." Path: rephrase each thought aloud with the screen covered; she produces "they need to be on top of demand for the books, because of the new year"; Joshua points out her first clause is the conclusion and her "because" clause is the evidence. Conclusion is "we must do whatever it takes to ship books in that category on time." Opinion check: "whatever it takes means literally whatever it takes. Does that mean I have to abuse kids and start a cow factory?" She first picked A, then corrected to D, which is correct. D uses "imperative," a meaning match, not a word match.
- **Economists and consumption (main conclusion).** "The obsession of economists with consumption as a measure of economic well-being has prevented us from understanding the true nature of economic well-being. We get very little satisfaction out of the fact that our clothing wears out, our automobiles depreciate, and the gasoline in our tanks burns up and must be replaced." Path: decode "economic well-being" by splitting the words; test the first sentence as opinion (yes); ask why we should believe it; the wearing-out claims answer that. Conclusion is the first sentence. Answer A, correct. Discussed traps: "satisfaction is possible without consumption" (extrapolation the author likely agrees with but did not conclude) and "valid measures cannot be devised" (too extreme).
- **Mayor McKin (main conclusion, rebuttal structure).** "Mayor McKin's policies have been criticized on the grounds that they benefit only wealthy city residents, but that is not a fair evaluation. Some of McKin's policies have clearly benefited the city's less affluent residents. McKin actively supported last year's proposal to lower the city's high property taxes. Because of this tax decrease, more development is taking place in the city, helping end the housing shortage and stabilize rents." Path: strip the name; translate "criticized on the grounds that they benefit only wealthy residents" to "people say his policies help the rich"; translate "less affluent" to "poor" ("I don't say less affluent. Maybe I'm just not as cool"); identify "but that is not a fair evaluation" as the author's own claim, distinct from the reasons that follow; resolve "that" back to the full criticism. Conclusion, restated: the criticism that he only helps the rich is unreasonable. She narrowed to B and D, said "unjust" in her own paraphrase, and chose D, correct. B fails because it reports only that he has been criticized and drops the "not fair" evaluation.
