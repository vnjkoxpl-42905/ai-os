# Moses Bryant, 2025-11-23

## LSAT concepts
- **Assumption** — "the invisible bridge that would connect the evidence to the conclusion... the part the author believes but didn't write down." Deliberately omitted. Arguments are simply "what do they tell you and what do they want you to believe," and there is nearly always a gap between the two.
- **Sufficient assumption** — a statement that if true guarantees the conclusion. What the argument **desires**. Look for strong, extreme language: always, every, all, most.
- **Necessary assumption** — what the argument **requires**. The bare minimum for the conclusion to have a chance. It does not prove the conclusion. The right answer should be "dry and boring and basic," and works one of two ways: rejecting something that would destroy the argument, or affirming something the author must agree with.
- **The negation technique** — negate an answer choice; if the conclusion becomes impossible, that answer is the necessary assumption. If the conclusion could still conceivably stand, it is not required, only helpful or irrelevant.
- **Validity is binary** — "the conclusion has to be impossible for it to be wrong." A valid argument has zero assumptions.
- **The golden rule** — "the evidence we accept as it is. The conclusion we never accept to be true."
- **Intermediate conclusion** — a sub-conclusion that also functions as evidence, so it must be accepted as given.
- **Literal reading of quantifiers** — "no one" means not a single individual on earth, zero exceptions. One exception breaks the claim.
- **Universal default** — an unqualified claim is read as universal. "When they don't give us a statement of qualification, sometimes or most times, they mean that in a definitive universal claim."
- **Overall versus partial** — a claim about net or overall effect requires weighing positives against negatives, not just listing negatives.

## Teaching moves
- **Corrects "summary" to the argument frame.** Moses defined an assumption as a kind of summary; Joshua narrowed it: assumptions only exist inside arguments, and an argument is just evidence plus what they want you to believe.
- **Robotic arm image for sufficient assumption**: "a sufficient assumption is like a robotic arm that grabs a bulb and screws it in for me. It does the whole job and leaves it. No doubt, it perfectly screws it together." Reused later as the metaphor for validity: a valid argument is "perfectly screwed in," an assumption means "it's not tightly screwed in."
- **Oxygen example for necessary assumption**: recording a video requires oxygen in your lungs. Deliberately absurd. Having oxygen does not guarantee a good video, but remove it and no video is possible. "That's the essence of a necessary assumption."
- **The frat boy versus the old man.** Sufficient assumption is "the frat boy, walking in with his shirt off, let's party, let's go," excited and extreme. Necessary assumption is "the conservative old man," making sure the electricity works and the bills are paid, "the staples." Directly generates the answer-selection heuristic: the more boring the claim, the better.
- **Rent versus lottery.** If the conclusion is "I will pay my $1,000 rent," the sufficient assumption is "I won the $10 million lottery," overkill but fine, "we don't mind overkill." The necessary assumption is having a bank account: having one does not mean you can pay, "I might be broke, but if I don't have a bank account, I definitely can't pay the rent."
- **Jenga for the negation technique**: "if I take this block out of the tower, would the whole thing collapse? If it does, that means it's correct." Clarified the common confusion when Moses asked whether the conclusion gets negated too: no, only the answer choice.
- **Insists on finding the gap before touching answers**, and frames it as a universal procedure: "no matter the question type, no matter what, you want to see conclusion, evidence, you want to think of a situation where you can think of a loophole, a gap, and then you want to work from there backwards."
- **Models the loophole himself when Moses stalls**, with a plausible real-world reason: maybe utility trucks are exempt from car standards precisely because they are inherently more durable, so "if they don't have the safety standards, maybe it's because there's no safety issue."
- **Reframes "is this argument valid" as binary** when Moses answered "pretty valid." "Remember, valid is binary. There should be no conceivable way the conclusion could be invalid."
- **Sets a live adversarial frame**: "let's fight back. Let's take the opposing counsel," "don't you be a shark," "play devil's advocate."
- **Substitutes a familiar example when the abstract one stalls.** On the technology question, when Moses could not generate an objection, Joshua rebuilt the same argument with Zoom and FaceTime, then asked for a counterargument. Moses immediately produced "it also had positive effects," which was exactly the gap.
- **Names the net-effect principle explicitly**: "you can't say something is overall bad unless you count also the positives. It's like, does the positives outweigh the negative? Something can have a negative to it. Doesn't mean that it's overall negative."
- **Concrete self-sufficiency test**: losing the ability to hunt and cook like our primitive ancestors is a real loss of self-sufficiency, but it does not obviously make humanity worse off, since "we can delegate those tasks to other people."
- **Attacks extreme conclusions by lowering the burden**: to break "no new inventions will be forthcoming," "I don't even have to go to the extreme that there will be. I just have to say it can be. All we have to do is introduce a 1% chance or one exception and then you win."
- **Refuses to let common-sense reading soften a claim**: "if I heard it like that I'd be like, dude, you're tripping, you're going too far."
- **Frames the objection habit as the central LSAT skill and gives four reasons.** It filters answers fast; your objection is often nearly the correct answer verbatim; comparing your objection to the answer trains you in the test's phrasing, "it's almost like the LSAT has a personality, you'll get to know them very well"; and it functions as a **guardrail**, "if I can't anticipate an objection, that means I didn't read it properly," so do not go to the answers.
- **Unifies question types under one skill**: once you have found the hole, the question type only decides what to do with it. "Do I patch that up with a bridge? Do I just break that up? Do I expose it and say you made a mistake there? Or am I just trying to say hey buddy, I'll give you a pass here."
- **Anti-goal for answer reading**: "you do not want to go into the answers looking for the answer. You want to confirm your answer that you've already got."
- **Connects his background to his aptitude** after learning Moses studied economics: "economics, philosophy, and physics are the three best performers on the LSAT... you have that natural intuitive ability" for incentives and rational viewpoints. Flags it as genuine, "I don't just say that as flattery."
- **Gives a directive but leaves the reasoning to the student**: "I don't want to tell you what to do, you're smart, so you'll know I have reasons why I say this. Do February."

## Worked examples
- **Necessary assumption, light utility trucks.** Light utility trucks are popular for their rugged appearance, yet although tough looking they are exempt from government car safety standards dictating minimum roof strength and impact resistance. Therefore a truck driver in a serious high-impact accident is more likely to be injured than a driver of a car subject to those standards. Gap identified: the exemption might exist because trucks are inherently safer, or for revenue reasons unrelated to safety. Answer E (light utility trucks are less likely to meet the car safety standards than cars subject to those standards) verified by negation: if trucks were equally or more likely to meet the standards, there is no reason one would be safer, and the conclusion collapses. A (the government has established safety standards for the construction of light utility trucks) fails the test: negating it still leaves the conclusion conceivable.
- **Necessary assumption, technology and well-being.** A columnist argues that relief from the drudgery of physical labor afforded by modern technology renders users dependent on the technology and on the energy systems required to run it; this leads to a loss of self-sufficiency; clearly then, in addition to undermining life's charm, modern technology diminishes users' overall well-being. Structure: the loss of self-sufficiency is an intermediate conclusion and therefore must be accepted. Gap: a loss of self-sufficiency does not establish a net negative, since technology also has upsides. Answer B (self-sufficiency contributes to a person's well-being) verified by negation: if self-sufficiency has no connection to well-being at all, the argument collapses.
- **Necessary assumption, patent rights.** New inventions are promoted by granting patent rights, which restrict anyone but the holder from profiting for a period. Without patent rights anyone could copy an invention, so inventors would have no financial incentive to invest the time and energy to develop new products. Thus it is important to continue granting patent rights, or else no one will engage in original development and consequently no new inventions will be forthcoming. Joshua drilled the extremity of "no one" and "no new inventions": zero exceptions, so one counterexample breaks it. Assumption: financial incentive is the only motivator. Moses produced the objection, people who are already well off might invent anyway. Answer A (financial reward is not the only incentive effective in motivating people to develop new inventions), verified by negation: if financial reward is the only effective incentive, other motives are ruled out and the conclusion holds; grant that other incentives exist and it does not.
