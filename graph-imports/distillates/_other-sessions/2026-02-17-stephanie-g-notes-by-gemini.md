# Stephanie Gurico, 2026-02-17

## LSAT concepts
- **Main conclusion**: the demo question, and the concept Joshua uses to demonstrate his whole method.
- **The two-step conclusion test**: (1) it must be a statement of the author's opinion, (2) it must receive **explicit**, not implicit, support from at least one other statement.
- **Fact vs. opinion**: "a fact statement can never be a conclusion." If a claim is measurable or empirically verifiable, it is descriptive and disqualified.
- **Referential phrasing**: "this," "they," "that" pointing back to earlier ideas, chained until the referent is lost.
- **Active reading, defined as hypothesize then validate**: form a guess about where the stimulus is going from the first word, then update it with each new sentence.
- **"Normally" as a status-quo marker** that predicts a contrary case or exception.
- **Indicator words as a limited tool**: "but" often introduces a conclusion, which is exactly what makes it exploitable as a trap.

## Worked examples
- **Epson campaign ads (main conclusion).** Normally political candidates send out campaign material in order to influence popular opinion. But the recent ads for Epson's campaign were sent to too few households to serve this purpose effectively. The ads were evidently sent out to test their potential to influence popular opinion. They covered a wide variety of topics, and Epson's campaign has been spending heavily on follow-up to gauge their effect on recipients. Path taken: predict a contrary case from "normally"; substitute "influencing popular opinion" for "this purpose"; rule out sentence two because the household threshold is empirical and therefore fact; identify sentence three as opinion (an intention, inferred from "evidently"); see the last sentence, wide topic variety plus heavy follow-up spending, as support for sentence three. Sentence three is the main conclusion. Answer C. Stephanie's answer was B, pulled by "but."
