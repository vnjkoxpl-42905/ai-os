# Janvi Mehndiratta, 2026-03-12

## LSAT concepts

- **Main conclusion**: the single highest-leverage skill. Two hard requirements: it must be a statement of opinion (never an empirical measurement or bare fact), and it must have explicit support from at least one other statement in the text. "If one of those two things are missing... it can't be a conclusion."
- **Main conclusion location**: expect it in the first sentence; it can be the second sentence or mid-argument, but if it is, "it's almost certainly because they're giving us some sort of opposing viewpoint." Never expect it in the last sentence.
- **Rebuttal structure**: when the author denies someone else's view, rephrase the denial into the positive claim the author is fighting for. "New York is not better than Toronto" equals "Toronto is not inferior to New York." Joshua: the LSAT "won't put it as just negating the other side. They'll just say the positive claim from the side of the author. That's the one way that they get students, because it looks like it's not the same idea, but it's just the inverse."
- **Evidence versus conclusion**: evidence is granted as true and never disputed. The only question is whether the evidence guarantees the conclusion. "If the conclusion is not a restatement of the evidence, basically not verbatim but conceptual overlap, there's even one tiny gap, one potential objection... then we have the problem. We've identified the gap."
- **Conditional logic**: "if introduces hypotheticals in a stimulus." A conditional is not a claim that something is or isn't the case, it establishes a rule. Conditionals are "rules / like hypotheticals."
- **Whether to negate**: an important correction. "We only negate it when it's required for us to. We don't just automatically do it." Negation applies when the author denies another party's position, not whenever a rebuttal marker appears.

## Teaching moves

- **The court case metaphor**, Joshua's central framing for argument work: "What side of the court case am I taking here?" Extended at the end: "You can't do that until you understand what you're even trying to fight for. You have to know what you're fighting for, what case you're taking, because the case you're taking on is the author's case. Whether you're trying to dispute it or you're trying to prove it, but you can't do that unless you know the case you're fighting for in the first place. It's like fighting a court case without knowing what your client is in for."
- **Naming the failure mode by score band**: "It's not enough to say I think it's this line or I feel like it's this line or I get the vibe of the general argument. That's where the mistakes happen, and that's where surprisingly, even decently high score ranges, people fall into."
- **Read-and-pause protocol** taught live, then assigned as homework: "Every time you read a stimulus, first sentence, the first half of it, pause. Get your brain acquainted with it. Then you'll find that you'll be way faster as you read the rest. It's like a little two second sacrifice to make it all come together." Joshua pauses at each complete thought, sometimes at a comma, and asks the student to restate before advancing.
- **Personalized analogies built from the student's own life.** He asks where she is (New York), where he is (Toronto), then builds the rebuttal-rephrasing example from the New York versus Toronto comparison, joking through her repeated wrong answers ("I don't know if you're just trying to rub it in my face... This is a test. If you say it again, I know you're doing it on purpose"). Also uses water bottles being bad for the environment, and a height comparison ("guy A is taller than guy B" equals "guy B is shorter than guy A") to make the inverse-equivalence point three different ways.
- **Escalating difficulty deliberately and naming it**: after the easy hardware-store question he says "I'm going to up the ante here," and afterward flags what he did: "that was a harder question, just to give you the exposure to a harder question."
- **Front-loading the work**: after the first question resolves instantly, "we kind of just did the homework up front, so it's a piece of cake when we got to the answer choice."
- **Forcing visualization**: "I want you to try to visualize what they're saying, like intentionally try to force yourself to visualize."
- **Antecedent hunting.** On "as plausible as this may sound," he repeatedly stops and asks "what's the *this* they're referring to?" until she names the current theory, then re-reads the sentence with the referent substituted in.
- **Letting the student arrive at the answer, then reflecting it back as the answer choice.** She paraphrases the stimulus in her own words as "we don't know the real reason for earthquakes"; he seizes on it: "you used the word not explainable, which was actually a very good choice of words," then shows the correct answer is nearly her verbatim phrasing. "Funny enough, when you were explaining to me, you just said this verbatim, almost as you read it."
- **The why test for finding the conclusion**: "ask yourself which statement can I say that I'm trying to explain for, or which statement can I say *why* to, and then they give you at least an attempt to give you support for it."
- **Deflecting content anxiety**: on the unclear physics of plate resistance, "I don't even know exactly what that means, but I just know there's a force and it's not important. That's why the LSAT is not a knowledge test. It's purely an understanding of the logic."
- **Praise is specific and immediate** when she assembles conclusion plus support unprompted: "You just did it perfectly. That's exactly what you did."

## Worked examples

- **Hardware store (main conclusion)**: "Contrary to recent speculations, no hardware store will be opening in the shopping plaza. If somebody were going to open a store there, they would already have started publicizing it. But there has been no such publicity." Path taken: read to the first comma, identify the rebuttal marker, then note this phrasing does NOT require negation since the author states his side directly rather than denying an opponent's. Conclusion is the first sentence. Supported by a conditional (open store, therefore publicity) plus the reality (no publicity). Janvi chose B, correct.
- **Earthquakes (main conclusion)**: current theory is that earthquakes come from adjoining rock plates sliding past each other, pressed together until powerful forces overcome the resistance. "As plausible as this may sound, at least one thing remains mysterious on this theory. The overcoming of such resistance should create enormous amounts of heat, but so far no increases in temperature unrelated to weather have been detected following earthquakes." Path taken: first sentence is other people's opinion, not the author's, so it cannot be the conclusion. Anticipate pushback. Identify the mystery: theory predicts heat, no heat is observed. Conclusion is the unstated-but-implied claim that the current theory does not fully explain earthquakes. Joshua notes most students get tripped up here because the wording is convoluted, and that you cannot weaken this argument without first knowing the precise claim.
