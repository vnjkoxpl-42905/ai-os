# Elias Tazi, 2025-05-27

## LSAT concepts

- **Paradox (resolve the discrepancy)**: framed as reconciling a point of tension by finding a deliberately omitted factor. "There's something that they're omitting deliberately, because if everything they said was true and there's nothing else to the story, then we know that it wouldn't make sense, but of course there is." The answer supplies the mechanism, and something else must have changed in the same time frame.
- **Supply and demand**: the one piece of outside knowledge Joshua says the LSAT expects. Low supply or high demand pushes price up, high supply or low demand pushes price down. "Scarcity creates the value." He confirmed there is essentially nothing else in the outside-knowledge category.
- **Flaw**: attacked by anticipating the reasoning pattern from the first few words. "Most of them are going to be sufficient versus necessary or correlation to causation, the two most popular."
- **Correlation vs causation**: three standard rebuttals kept live at once, reverse causation, an alternate cause, or a third factor causing both.
- **Part vs whole (and whole to part)**: a claim about one component of a single thing transferred to that same thing entire, or the reverse.
- **Generalization / sampling**: what is true of one or a few instances asserted of all instances of that type. Joshua drew the line between this and part vs whole explicitly, since they "have a very similar feel to them."
- **Small sample size** as a species of generalization flaw.
- **Ad hominem**: triggered by any answer impugning the arguer's motives, bias, or hypocrisy.
- **Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence**: unproven does not equal proven false.
- **Necessary assumption and the negation test**: negate the candidate and check whether the conclusion is destroyed.
- **"Fails to address" / "fails to consider"**: identical phrasings, and both reduce to a weakening test.
- **Parallel flaw**: not a separate question type. "Parallel flaw questions are literally flaw questions. There's no difference between the two."
- **Method of reasoning** as the substrate under flaw: "Role to method is what method is to flaw. They're the same family, one level building on each other."

## Teaching moves

- **Trigger-word anticipation, the session's central skill.** Joshua trains Elias to call the reasoning pattern from the opening words before finishing the stimulus. "If the first word says if or whenever, you know that it's going to be sufficient versus necessary. If I see a correlation, instantly I'm almost waiting for the causal claim, waiting for the causal claim." He is explicit that this is the mastery marker: "It's so predictable. That gives you superpowers, you can just breeze through it."
- **"Pause, pause, pause" mid-read.** He repeatedly interrupts Elias's reading of a stimulus at the exact trigger phrase ("tested in three separate studies", "for all three of the cars I have owned") and asks what he should now be expecting. Then he lands the pattern by juxtaposing the two stimuli: "Three studies, three cars, even the same number. Car, studies, doesn't matter, you can replace that content with anything."
- **Deliberate disengagement from content.** "It's useful at times to disengage with it. Be like, okay, don't get caught in the brain area, but what are they telling me? These two things are likely to be together, so they're saying one causes the other."
- **X-ray glasses framing for answer choices.** Every answer choice is a flavor of fallacy and should be labeled on sight, whether or not it is correct. "Once you're able to use your X-ray glasses you can look through them very quickly. You should be able to identify what flavor of answer choice it is by the wording and the feel for it."
- **The "mute the phrase" trick.** For "the argument fails to address the possibility that X," he tells Elias to mute the framing and test only X: "Just say, if whatever they say after that happens, what happens to the conclusion? If it makes it weaker, it's the right answer."
- **Diagnostic questions in place of explanation.** Asked what could counter the musician argument, Elias said "hereditary" unprompted. Joshua then let him misread answer B, and instead of correcting, walked him back to his own word: "Earlier you said a factor. There's a word. It starts with a G." Payoff: "That is literally bang on what you said earlier."
- **Personalized analogies drawn from the student's own life.** He asked what car Elias drives (a 2019 Alfa Romeo Giulia Ti), then built the whole part-vs-whole vs generalization distinction on it. Part to whole: the back seat is bad so the car is bad. Whole to part: the car is nice so the engine must be nice. Generalization: what's true of your Alfa is true of all Alfas. Also used sneakers, Yeezys vs Skechers, gold vs rocks, for supply and demand.
- **The FSU billionaires drill**, to break the "unproven equals false" move. Three students studied, all successful, all children of billionaires. He walks Elias through the sampling bias, then springs the second, subtler error: "Can I then go on and say therefore that school produces terrible outcomes? All I have to say is that's a bad sample size. I can't go on and say the opposite thing." He named the pair explicitly: "Unproven versus proven false."
- **Letting the student find the abstraction.** Elias, on the parallel flaw, produced the mapping himself: "Since they didn't have something to show why it would be the best, they're claiming the opposite because it's not proven, and since it's not proven they're taking the absence of the claim to be the opposite as true." Joshua's whole reply: "Beautiful. Well said. Right on. That's it. Bang."
- **Correcting his own error openly.** Joshua mistakenly implied B was correct on the housing question, realized mid-conversation, and reversed cleanly: "I really realized something happened here. B is wrong. Maybe I made a mistake. E is right. My bad."
- **Praising engagement over correctness.** When Elias admitted he chose an answer without conviction: "It's good that you're engaging with it. That means you're not just saying yeah yeah I get it, it's right. Sometimes you see the right answer and you just want to say it's right because you know it has to be right, versus really engaging with it. That's a good sign."
- **Capping over-sophistication.** When Elias proposed a conditional reading of "three studies," Joshua validated it but redirected to the cruder read: "You're right, you made it work, that's because you're able to think outside the box. But the dummy would be, oh, three studies, they're probably going to compare them. Yours is more advanced." Same with the statistics question, where he told Elias his stats background is overkill: "I don't want to disrespect your stats classes with the level they'd need you to know."
- **Deferring a topic to protect focus.** Elias started reaching for necessary assumption mechanics mid-flaw discussion. "For now, hold on to the overlooks-of-possibility. Don't think about that too much. Worry about the flaws first, then we'll return to that."
- **Warning against the fallacy-only trap** while setting homework: "Not every flaw question is a fallacy. About 50 percent of them are, give or take. For that 50 percent you want those unlocked because those are free points, easy money, you have a cookie cutter answer prior to even seeing the answer choices."
- **Rapport as connective tissue.** Comfortable slang throughout ("that's fire," "bang on," "my friend"), a running joke about not confusing Moroccans with Egyptians or Algerians, and a forward-looking aside on Elias's car costs: "When you're a big hot lawyer, you'll have three of them in your lot."

## Worked examples

- **Q6, Brewsterville housing** (paradox). New apartment construction increased housing supply, which normally lowers rents, yet rents for existing apartments rose. Path: if price rose, either demand rose or supply fell. A rejected because building fewer apartments than planned is still an increase, so rents should still have fallen. B rejected because "more desirable" new apartments does not entail lower demand for the old ones, since desirability is not demand without ability to pay ("I like houses in Malibu, does that mean I have the means?"). D rejected as backwards, people moving out lowers demand and should lower rents. Another choice about nearby areas rejected as off-target. E correct: the apartments went up at the outset of a trend of increasing numbers of people seeking residence, so demand outpaced the new supply.
- **Q9, musicians and brain area** (flaw). Premise: the brain area for distinguishing piano sounds tends to be larger in highly skilled musicians than in people who have rarely played. Conclusion: playing an instrument alters brain structure. Correlation stated as causation. A rejected: it is a part vs whole answer about pianists vs other skilled musicians, and the stimulus never mentions pianists at all, only the sounds a piano makes. B correct: fails to address that people may become skilled musicians partly because of the pre-existing size of that brain area, that is, reverse causation, possibly hereditary. C rejected because it would strengthen, not weaken, the causal claim. D rejected as irrelevant, the stimulus draws no distinction about practice levels.
- **Q11, muscle pain treatment** (parallel flaw). A promising treatment was tested in three studies, all three had critical methodological flaws, therefore the treatment is probably not effective. Flaw: taking failure to prove as proof of the opposite. Correct parallel: the baking contest judges had no uniform criteria for selecting a winner, therefore the cake is probably bad. E rejected as ad hominem (city council members with a financial stake, so their proposals are self-interested). C rejected as part vs whole (some foods have little nutritional value, so people who eat them are probably malnourished), and Joshua confirmed Elias's earlier instinct had been right about that one.
- **Q15, car fuel economy** (flaw). Manufacturers advertise fuel economy under normal driving conditions, the author could not get close to advertised numbers on any of the three cars he has owned, therefore manufacturers probably inflate the numbers. Correct answer: draws a conclusion from too small a sample. B rejected: it presents a necessary assumption that driving conditions are the same in every geographical region, which fails the negation test, since the author need not assume literally every region is identical. Joshua noted the ironic near-hit, that comparison problems often do surface as faulty distinctions.
