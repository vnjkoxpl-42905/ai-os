# Azaria Lavender, 2025-10-23

## LSAT concepts

- **Necessary assumption comes in two forms**: **linking** (positive, what the author was actively thinking must be true) and **defender** (negative, what the author is passively rejecting, protecting the argument against an objection).
- **Assumption and objection are negations of each other.** "An assumption is just a negated objection. An objection is just saying that the assumption is not true. They're the inverse of each other." So attacking an argument is how you find its assumption.
- **Negation test**: negate the answer choice and see if the argument collapses. Best suited to defender assumptions, where the mechanical shortcut is to delete the "not" and read it straight. Works on linking assumptions too, just less cleanly. Note Joshua's caution that *linking and defender are not opposites of each other*; both are assumptions, and negating either yields an objection.
- **Predicting which form to expect from the logic**: conditionals, causal chains (A leads to B leads to C, therefore A leads to C), or a new concept in the conclusion all point to a **linking** assumption. Plain cause and effect from a correlation, or a comparison between two groups, points to a **defender**.
- **New concept in the conclusion**: if a term appears in the conclusion and nowhere in the evidence, the author is assuming something about it, and the credited answer will almost certainly contain that term. Joshua put it at roughly 85% likelihood for both necessary and sufficient assumption.
- **Two places the gap can sit.** Usually the conclusion is shifted from the evidence, so the link runs premise to conclusion. But sometimes the conclusion matches the last link in the evidence perfectly and the two *premises* fail to overlap, so the link must be built inside the evidence side.
- **Comparison arguments have two symmetric holes**: an omitted downside of the group being praised, or an omitted upside of the group being dismissed. "Either B is better than we thought, or A is worse than we thought."
- **Necessary assumption answers must be guaranteed by the argument.** "Think of the answer choice as an inference. It must be a true statement based off the conclusion."

## Teaching moves

- **The twenty-questions game.** Joshua opened by having Azaria guess a person (Michael Jackson) using only true/false questions: male, North American, long hair, older, singer, not alive. He then converted the game into the concept: the traits she actively asked about are linking assumptions; the traits silently ruled out (not a student, not a rapper, not alive) are defender assumptions. "If you saw Michael Jackson, you wouldn't recognize him and say, oh, that's a person that's not a student. That would be absurd. But you're passively rejecting them."
- **The same game supplies the "traits are not the whole" point.** "If I told you I saw a male yesterday, you wouldn't say, whoa, was that Michael Jackson? These assumptions are traits of the main assumption. They're components, not Michael Jackson himself." This is why a necessary assumption can look weak and still be correct.
- **Built the example from her own goal.** Azaria wants Howard Law School, so the worked argument was "Azaria wants to go to Howard Law School, therefore Azaria wants to go to a great law school." She supplied the linking assumption herself ("Howard Law School is a great law school"), and Joshua derived the defender form from it ("Howard is not a bad law school").
- **Jenga for the negation test.** "Your goal is to see if I take this block out, will the structure collapse? Certain pieces look like they're pivotal, but the only way you'll really know is once you take that block away."
- **Made her state the negation test before using it**, then corrected her application. She said the test suits linking assumptions; he told her she was not wrong but that defenders are the better use case, then gave the mechanical trick: delete the "not," and if what remains reads as an objection, the answer is right.
- **Instant review, not batch review.** The session's biggest process change. One question at a time, unlimited time, choose an answer, then immediately hit show answer. Rationale: "When you get to question 25, you don't really remember what you did in question one. You're trying to refresh yourself, and you don't get the full value from the review." Also: skip reviewing questions you got right. "Why spend time on questions you're already going to get?"
- **A section is not a unit.** "A section is just 25 individual questions in a row. You're doing one question at a time, 25 times." The value is locating where the wall is: "Maybe I get to question 14 and my mental energy is not there."
- **Review must name a micro skill, not restate the explanation.** "Review is not just, oh I see how that went, I get the explanation now. When the explanation is given to you it'll make sense, but how do I replicate that process?" He pushed on vagueness: if a passage was "complicated," complicated how, long words, science content, or conditional indicator words? "You want to get rid of the vagueness and be razor sharp."
- **Review has a third step: the action.** "What's the exact action I'm going to take to address that? Not just, oh, I'm going to read it more slowly. How slow? You want to give yourself hard lines and be able to say I passed the test or I didn't."
- **Only the student can do this part.** "That's self-inquiring. Really the only person that has the answer to that question for sure is you."
- **Schedule built short on purpose.** Five days, not two weeks. "If I do a fixed schedule and you find it doesn't work for you, you don't have that time." He also let curriculum follow the data rather than the plan, deciding Saturday's topic from her section results plus what other students were struggling with: "I kind of take the wisdom of the masses."
- **Asked for her unbiased read before giving his.** "Give me your feedback before I say anything. I don't want to influence your personal subjective feel."
- **Structure check, then logic check, as a fixed two-pass routine.** On every question: conclusion and evidence first, then "do we see any logical moves being employed, conditional, comparative, or causal?" then the new-concept scan, then the answer choices.
- **Pre-eliminate on the new concept.** "We can almost say certainly it's not going to be the answer, nearly 90% likelihood. You can jump the gun. You want to scan them quickly to see if they make sense, but almost guarantee they're not right."
- **Normalized a notoriously hard miss.** On the Antarctic ice sheets question: "That's a notoriously hard one. Even of level fives, it's one of the hardest."
- **Abandoned a political example mid-sentence** when it stopped fitting ("actually no, that's not a good example because it's the most"), rather than forcing it.
- **Test recency is overrated.** Asked which PTs to use, he said stick to the 140s but flagged the framing as overblown: "There is nuance between older and newer, but it's slightly overrated. If you can do the question, you can do the question." He also noted an easier test means a harsher curve, so it averages out.

## Worked examples

- **Railroads, necessary assumption.** Evidence: service suffers when a railroad combines freight and commuter business, because dividing attention between the two customer types means it serves neither particularly well. Conclusion: therefore, if a railroad is to be a successful business it must concentrate exclusively on one of those two markets. Structure and logic: conditional in the conclusion, with an underlying causal claim (dividing attention causes poor performance). New concept in the conclusion: **successful business**, absent from the evidence, so the answer must contain it. Credited answer: a railroad that does not serve its customers well will not be a successful business. Distractors eliminated fast for lacking the new concept (freight and commuter service have little in common) or for being too strong (a "first priority" framing). Azaria got it right unaided. Joshua: "You pretty much slam dunked this one."
- **Island law, necessary assumption.** Evidence: the most vocal proponents of the proposed law are wealthy summer residents who will have left before the resulting problems set in, so they benefit from passage without having to deal with the problems. Conclusion: therefore anyone who supports the proposed law is serving only the interests of a few outsiders at the cost of creating problems for the island's permanent residents. Logic: conditional ("anyone who," "only") plus an implicit comparison between two groups. The gap: knowing one group avoids the problems does not establish that the law is net bad for everyone else, so the argument needs the trade-off to come out negative. Credited answer B: the problems associated with the law would outweigh any benefits it might provide to the island's permanent residents. Azaria picked E (most permanent residents oppose the law), which fails because the conclusion cannot guarantee it: "Just because it's not in their interest, what if they're delusional? What if they think it benefits them?"
