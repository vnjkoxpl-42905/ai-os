# Sabrina Egal, 2026-01-27

An 87-minute Must Be True clinic. Joshua taught a single organizing method (track groups, track traits, link them as you read) and a taxonomy of the only three shapes these stimuli take, then had Sabrina drive five questions aloud. The hardest stretch was a long, stubborn fight over necessary versus sufficient.

## LSAT concepts

- **Must Be True is an organization game, not a reasoning game.** "Must be true questions are just a game of keeping everything organized." And: "Must be true questions are testing your ability to organize your thoughts. It's all they're really doing."
- **Groups and traits.** The unit of analysis is a category of things plus a trait attributed to it. "We want to know the groups and the traits and we want to keep track of them and we want to see how these groups and traits overlap, and then you'll be able to see the ability to make an inference."
- **The three shapes.** Joshua's complete taxonomy for the question type: "If there's more than one conditional, expect to chain the conditionals. If they have one conditional and other sets of facts, that means they're going to ask us to apply that conditional to whatever sets of facts they gave us. And if they have no conditionals at all, then they're going to do that thing we were talking about with category traits... That's really the only ways that they really play with."
- **The repeated idea is the bridge.** "Another thing to pay attention to is when I see an idea repeated. That's their gold mine." He named "delegates to the convention" as "the big bridge — anybody that caught that would have got this question right."
- **Universal statements run both ways.** From "every delegate is a party member" you also get "some party members are delegates." "If I said all Canadians speak English... that means that in the group of people that speak English, some of them also have to be Canadians. But we don't want to say all of them have to be Canadians." He framed this as "extracting knowledge from any sort of claim" and "stretching this just to get every single ounce of truth out of it."
- **Quantity and conditional statements overlap.** "Conditionals also are technically quantity statements too... 'all' is a conditional and a quantity amount." Hence "every delegate is a party member" gives the contrapositive "if you're not a party member you cannot be a delegate."
- **Some-statements are symmetric.** "If some of one group is the other, that means that some of the other group is also part of the first — there's almost like a full overlap between both."
- **"Most" is a hard line.** "Most means over 50% have this thing, possess this ability." It licenses an inference about the majority, and its complement about the minority.
- **Necessary is not sufficient.** The session's central and hardest idea. "A requirement doesn't mean that you're guaranteed to have the outcome. So everybody that has the acid doesn't mean that you always have to have the saliva, but everybody that has the saliva has the acid. Those are two different things." And: "Requirement just means you have the threshold to even be considered having it."
- **"Only" is not "all."** "Only humans is one thing, all humans are different... Only a human could do calculus doesn't mean that all humans can do calculus, but to be able to do calculus you must be a human."
- **Conjunctive requirements fail on one leg.** For "any sale item purchased can be returned for store credit but not for a refund": both traits must hold, so "all it requires is for one of them to fail" to disqualify. The contrapositive uses *or*, not *and*.
- **"All" in an answer choice is a red flag.** "Every time you see an answer choice that says 'all,' be very careful to choose that answer, because it has to be guaranteed... 'All' should be a huge red flag always. If in this even potentially conceivable world there's an exception, then that answer choice has to be wrong." He extends the caution to "most" and "requires": "pay attention to the strength of the language."
- **Must Be True answers are unique by construction.** "If it has to be true, then there can't be two answers that have to be true. So by default, this is going to be the right answer. That's why you don't need to read all of them... you could theoretically get away with it if it's a provable answer."
- **Universal rules apply to anything, including things not mentioned.** On a computers answer choice: "They made a universal rule, then you cannot do it, so it doesn't matter what it is... whether it's about computers, whether it's about aliens."

## Teaching moves

- **Never enter the answer choices empty-handed.** The strongest rule of the session. "We never want to go into the answer choices without having an inference that we made. If you have not made an inference, do not bank on the answer choices to bail you out. That's the worst habit ever, because that means if you didn't see the inference, you missed something." And: "The way to get better at must be true questions is if you go into the answer choices looking for the answer versus having an answer solved up front. You'll win. If you don't do that, you'll fail. You might get away with it bailing out... but then you'll get punished for it."
- **Infer as you read, not at the end.** "When you're making inferences as you read versus trying to read it all and see 'ah what do I know, what do I know' — it's chaotic... Do not wait till the end. Follow them as you're reading it."
- **The island-jumping image.** "It's like you're jumping off island to island and you're seeing where the bridging points are." Missing one link strands you: "If you're not able to jump on the islands with them, the idea is that you'll miss them at a certain point." He explicitly connected this back to argument structure: "Where does a premise leave off? Where does the next premise pick up from?"
- **Rewind a question the student found easy to show *why* it was easy.** After she said "that was really easy," he refused to move on: "Let's rewind why that was easy... The reason why this question went so smoothly is that if you just ran through that without catching that connection, you would have got punished for this question. I'm not trying to test you. I'm trying to say the reason why this question was easy is that you caught the link."
- **Set up the analysis before reading the answer.** "It's always super useful to say, okay, before they tempt me to think about what they're saying, let me have my room in order."
- **Hand over the wheel.** "I want you to do it autonomously, by yourself with me beside you... We'll take off the training wheels." Later: "I'm going to only step in when I need to. You're going to do it out loud." He also worked one question cold alongside her: "I honestly haven't even looked at the answer, so I'm already ready. I'm going to do it with you in real time."
- **Model his own near-miss.** On the language question: "Even I had a moment, had to catch myself. This is the thing that I was not being disciplined — this is what they were trying to bait us into. Animals can vocally express hunger, but they didn't say that animals cannot do that for any objects. I was wrestling with that idea. I was like, did they just throw this as a red herring?"
- **Slow is the whole training method.** "You're not trying to be fast. You're trying to unpack it and be thorough." The compression is automatic: "It might take you instead of 10 minutes to understand it, it might take you eight minutes, and then it becomes three minutes, and then it's like, oh, I don't even have to think about it. It's just like 1 plus 1 equals 2." And the promise: "Be very slow as you do these questions in practice. You'll become very fast, but don't try to be fast while you're trying to absorb the information. That's the biggest mistake — people often think I have to be able to do this at the same speed as test. It's not test day." He backed it with track record: "There's never been a situation where I saw somebody get the answer right and keep doing that process where that process doesn't just become streamlined."
- **"What you do in your conscious practice will become subconscious."** The core learning claim, repeated several ways: "Your brain's a machine... you really want to bring it to your conscious and then it'll subconsciously get bought into your thought process without you realizing, but it requires you to have multiple exposures."
- **Analogy chain for necessary conditions.** He cycled through four when one did not land: being a woman to give birth ("doesn't mean you could be an 80-year-old woman — you're still a woman, but it's a requirement"), taking the LSAT to apply to law school ("everybody that has a law degree took the LSAT, but that does not mean everybody who took the LSAT has a law degree"), a high school diploma for law school, and doing calculus.
- **Name why the confusion is legitimate.** "It's very tricky and it twists your brain a lot, because what you're saying makes sense. It's a very important distinction that they love, because our human common sense will tell us otherwise. It's playing with our common sense. It's very counterintuitive." Also: "If you tell this to your friends, they're going to think you're speaking Chinese."
- **Practice inference-making beyond what the question needs.** "Practicing your inference making skills even when they're not needed is so useful. Then you'll never miss an inference... if you practice that muscle so well you'll catch all of them, which then you'll be a master."
- **Check the tank mid-session.** "Do you have the sauce right now?" — explained as "usually there's a point where you burn out. I'm not saying you're at that point now, but just checking in. I just don't want to make you feel like you're ripping your hair out."
- **Rein in the student when she jumps ahead correctly**, then credit her: "Oh, you made the jump. You're getting too fast. Wait, wait, slow down. You're right, but you made the inference for me."
- **Praise calibrated against the cohort.** On her (A) elimination: "That's like a level five question. I feel like a lot of my students would have got that one wrong. They would have thought A was right."
- **He does not pretend a term is cleaner than it is.** On "verbal signs": "Verbal signs are just a bizarre way of saying it." He then corrected his own simplification to "words" when Sabrina pushed back that animals don't say words: "That was a bad choice. It's not words, more so it's just making a sound... all words are just sounds that are able to be attached to things." He reached for Somali as the second-language example, twice.

## Worked examples

- **Laboratory mice, must be true.** Lab mice used for research aimed at improving human health are usually kept in small cages; such an environment is neither normal nor healthy for mice; the reliability of research using animals is diminished if those animals are not in a normal environment. Structure: facts plus one conditional, so apply the facts to the rule. Sabrina caught the implicit link (lab mice are a subset of mice, so the "not normal" claim reaches them) unprompted. Answer (C): research using laboratory mice aimed at improving human health is compromised by the conditions under which the mice are kept. Joshua's Quebec/Canada scaffold ran underneath: laboratory mice are to mice as Quebecers are to Canadians.
- **Convention delegates, must be true.** Every delegate is a party member; some delegates are government officials; each government official at the convention is a speaker. Chain built live: some government officials are party members, therefore some party members will speak, therefore some speakers are party members / delegates. Answer (C). Sabrina made both inferences herself.
- **Heliosublex caterpillars, must be true.** HS caterpillars eat only fruit of genus *Bisilus*; those fruits lack a certain acid; the acid is necessary for growth and maturation of many insects other than HS; the acid is also necessary for production of a chemical V; most caterpillar species have V in their saliva. Inferences stacked: HS don't get the acid, therefore HS don't have V; most caterpillar species have V, therefore most have the acid in their diet. Sabrina's original pick was (C), which reversed the conditional — having the acid guarantees V. This is what triggered the twenty-minute necessary/sufficient argument. She kept circling: "It's just something about this one that just doesn't make any sense to me," and, revealingly, "I feel like I understand the conditional aspect of it and that's the only reason it makes sense to me. But if I think about it too hard, it doesn't."
- **Sale items / gardening equipment, must be true.** Any sale item purchased can be returned for store credit but not for a refund; every home appliance and every piece of gardening equipment is on sale, along with selected construction tools. Sabrina answered (D) — no piece of gardening equipment is returnable for a refund — correctly and first time. She then asked her own contrapositive question, which Joshua upgraded: the disjunction, not the conjunction, is what triggers it.
- **Language and conceptual thought, must be true (level 5).** Language requires verbal signs for objects as well as for feelings; many animals can vocally express hunger, but only humans can ask for an egg or an apple by naming it; using verbal signs for objects requires the ability to distinguish objects from other objects; that requires conceptual thought. Chain: language → verbal signs → distinguishing objects → conceptual thought. Sabrina built the chain and reached "you need conceptual thought for language." Joshua flagged the second sentence as a probable red herring and warned that the correct answer's "all humans are capable of conceptual thought" would normally be disqualifying language, survivable here only because the work was done up front.
- **Great artwork and computers, must be inferred.** To be great, an artwork must express a deep emotion such as sorrow or love; an artwork cannot express an emotion its creator was incapable of experiencing. Therefore a creator incapable of experiencing an emotion cannot produce great artwork — a requirement, not a guarantee. Answer (D): as long as computers are constructed so as to be incapable of experiencing emotions, they will not create great artworks. Sabrina hesitated on scope ("isn't it weird that they just randomly start talking about computers?") and Joshua resolved it as a universal rule. She then eliminated the trap answer (A) — a computer can create artwork expressing sorrow or love only if it has experienced such an emotion — on her own, correctly, because it says *artwork*, not *great artwork*.

## Student state

- Scoring 18 correct on LR (Joshua: "if you're at 18 to 20 range"). Target set at 21-22, which he called "conservative. I'm making it so it's very comfortable goals."
- RC baseline is fine and largely untouched by instruction so far: "You're in a good spot for where we are at for RC. Since we haven't really got into RC, when we just start doing RC, you're going to make a quick bump just based off a few adjustments." He referenced an RC result she sent, roughly "18... 27."
- Must Be True is the current bottleneck and the highest-leverage fix, because it carries over: "If you get the must be true questions confidently down, you'll get better at most strongly supported as well. It's like a downstream effect... getting better at those two question types by itself would have brought you over the 20 line comfortably."
- Strength: she makes inferences correctly once slowed down, and she caught the level-five trap (great artwork vs artwork) before Joshua raised it. She also generated her own contrapositive questions unprompted.
- Weakness: reversing necessary conditions into sufficient ones. This one misconception ate a quarter of the session and she never got fully comfortable — she conceded understanding while sounding unconvinced, and Joshua called it: "You sound iffy. I don't like the iffiness in your voice."
- Her real objection is a time objection, not a comprehension one: "This is a lot. There's a lot to unpack, and you feel like you don't always have the time to unpack it." And later, "maybe I would have gotten that wrong in time conditions."
- She tends to volunteer that she has already reviewed a question and to signal fatigue; Joshua noted "you hit me with that a lot."
- Habit being replaced: reading straight through and then hunting the answer choices for something plausible.
