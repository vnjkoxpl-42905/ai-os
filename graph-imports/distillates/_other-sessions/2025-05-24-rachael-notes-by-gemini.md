# Rachael Marks, 2025-05-24

## LSAT concepts

- **Sufficient assumption**: the answer must *guarantee* the conclusion, not support it. "Could be true is not what we want. Sufficient assumption is it has to be guaranteed." Any answer that leaves a scenario where the conclusion is false is wrong.
- **Main conclusion**: highlight it first, every time, so your eyes can bounce back to it instead of holding it in short-term memory.
- **Subject and predicate split**: Joshua's core operating move. Break the conclusion in half: the subject is the thing being named, the predicate is what is being claimed about it. Both halves have to be serviced separately.
- **The "textbook definition" of the predicate**: Rachael's own phrase, which Joshua adopted on the spot. Before you can prove a subject satisfies a predicate, you need the criteria that define the predicate. If the stimulus does not give you those criteria, the answer must.
- **New-concept-in-the-conclusion rule**: "If the conclusion has a brand new idea that's in the predicate, even in the subject, guaranteed that has to be mentioned in the answer." Used as a pre-reading elimination filter.
- **Strengthen vs guarantee**: an answer that makes the conclusion plausible ("that's maybe possible, but we don't know") is not sufficient.

## Teaching moves

- **The prosecutor frame**, the spine of the whole session. The subject is the defendant, the predicate is the charge. "If I'm trying to prove that somebody's guilty of second-degree murder, I have to find out what the requirements for second-degree murder are, and then I have to show that the person did that thing." Two-step: get the definition of the crime, then show the defendant meets it.
- **The Quebec arson drill**, used to make the "define the predicate first" step land. Joshua refuses to let her jump to evidence: "I want the actual definition, and I want it to be for the Canadian law. Quebec." When she finally says "we want to see what the textbook definition of arson is," he answers "Beautiful. That's the answer." Then he shows why the step matters: "If you said, voila, I found out he was burning a flag, that's arson, right? Then you find out in Quebec that's a celebration for some French holiday."
- **The corrupt lawmaker frame**, for how permissive a sufficient assumption may be. "You're the lawmaker here, you can be as corrupt as you want. You could literally say he's guilty because his name starts with M." The point: the answer does not have to be sensible or capture the whole argument, it only has to close the gap. "We don't care about whether it's sensible. We just care that we need to make sure it fits." Paired with the counter-warning against a merely plausible answer: "Do you want to risk it with Max and say he has to have done the fire at 8 p.m.? You might be able to convict him, but if you can just say he's blonde and I don't like blonde, you want to make sure this person's guilty."
- **De-abstracting by parallel**: when Rachael stalls on the potato stimulus, Joshua refuses to explain it and instead makes her build a parallel argument in her own domain. "Don't use potatoes. I want you to do something else that's more real to you." She picks apples. He then escalates to Canadian vs American students on a test to surface the buried idea, that a comparison claim requires measuring both sides.
- **Withholding the answer on purpose.** When she asks "Wait, is B the answer?" he redirects: "I don't know, but it's not really so important. It's more so how do we get the right answer so you can make sure that you never have to ask me that. You'll tell me, you'll say no, B is the right answer. That's what I want."
- **Blunt error correction.** When she folds the predicate into the subject: "Wow. Okay. That's an absolute blunder." Then he rebuilds the split immediately rather than moving on.
- **Repeat-it-back looping.** Repeatedly makes her restate the subject, the predicate, and the goal aloud mid-analysis ("What's our goal right now?", "Repeat to me the rule") rather than letting the target drift.
- **Predict the answer before reading the choices.** After she articulates that the printing error must make the stamp rare, he stops her: "Bingo. That's going to be our answer." Then, after she confirms B: "You said the answer before you looked at it. That's why it's very good to do that, because when you do it, it makes it a lot easier."
- **Elimination without reading.** Demonstrates screening choices purely on whether they contain the undefined predicate term: "C, do we see habits of thought? No. That's literally impossible for it to be the right answer, so I wouldn't even have to read it. You got two out of the way without even looking at them."
- **Reframes the purpose of the drill away from the answer key**: "Getting the answer right is so useless for getting into law school, because they just don't care. They care about what's your test score. So ultimately it comes down to, how do I replicate this?"
- **Closing prescription**: "Read to understand, not read to finish."

## Worked examples

- **Potatoes and solanine** (sufficient assumption). Conclusion: peeling wild potatoes makes them at least as safe to eat as unpeeled domesticated potatoes of the same size. Subject: peeled wild potatoes. Predicate: at least as safe as unpeeled domesticated. Path taken: a comparison claim requires measuring both sides, so we need the solanine levels to come out equal after peeling. A fails because "the proportion in the skin is larger in wild potatoes" leaves residual non-skin solanine unaccounted for. C is correct because it establishes the skin holds all the solanine, so removing it removes the entire differential.
- **TV commercials** (sufficient assumption). Conclusion: the TV commercial has influenced North American habits of thought. Evidence: the average 40-year-old North American will have watched over 1 million commercials. Subject: TV commercials. Predicate: has influenced habits of thought, a term never defined in the premises. Path: no rule of law exists, so the answer must supply one. C and D eliminated on sight for never mentioning "habits of thought." B correct: things people are exposed to in great quantity will influence their habits of thought. Joshua flagged his own residual discomfort with "great quantity" as undefined, then set it aside as an LSAT-standards concession.
- **Company health program and heart attacks** (sufficient assumption). Conclusion: increased risk of heart attack among employees due to the new health program. Evidence supplies the predicate rule: a sudden increase in exercise increases heart attack risk for people of any physical condition. Missing half is the subject side, that the employees will actually increase exercise suddenly. A correct. E ("all employees regardless of condition will participate") rejected because participating in a program is not the same as a sudden increase: "Going from being sedentary to being very active, that's risky. It's not just changing programs." B rejected (no information about a previous program), C rejected (says "regularly," stimulus says "sudden"), D rejected (nothing about company policy).
- **Printing-error stamp** (sufficient assumption). Conclusion: this stamp is probably highly valuable. Predicate criteria supplied in the stimulus: rarity and age, assuming good condition. Two of three already met ("fine specimen" covers condition, "quite old" covers age), leaving rarity as the only gap, and the printing error as the only unused fact. So the answer must convert printing error into rarity. B correct: printing errors are always confined to a few individual stamps, and "by definition, rare is few." C rejected: most error stamps being in collectors' hands says nothing about how many exist.

## Logistics and commitments

Homework prescription for Rachael's own practice: highlight the conclusion, split it into subject and predicate in writing on paper rather than in her head, define the predicate's textbook definition, set a specific goal, and only then read the answer choices. Joshua stressed the writing-it-out step specifically as the fix for her vagueness problem. Session ended with no scheduling discussed. A written guide on sufficient assumption questions was produced for her afterward covering all four examples.
