# Kaia Reid, 2026-07-13

A 46-minute consultation with a high-scoring self-studier (151 diagnostic, now 162-165) who has plateaued. Joshua worked one Main Conclusion question with her as a diagnostic, then made his pitch: her RC problem is a structure problem, and Role/Method/Main Conclusion in LR is the lever that fixes it. She had already met several other tutors and said his approach was the best of them.

## LSAT concepts

- **Hypothesize and validate.** His operational definition of active reading: "A very important practice with RC and LR is the ability to hypothesize and validate the hypothesis with new information. People say actively read — they often don't have a way to actually operationalize that. It's a common flash term, but how do I do that? You want to have a hypothesis as soon as you possibly can about why the author took the time to write the stimulus in front of you. Even the first couple of words."
- **Being wrong is proof you know what's going on.** "How will I know my hypothesis is correct? What if I get my hypothesis wrong? Good. You can only know your hypothesis is wrong in the event that you know what is actually true. So that tells you by default that means it's a great thing."
- **"Normally" signals an incoming exception.** "If I say normally, usually it's typically going to tell me that something's different about the specific situation we're in now. Understanding that, we can anticipate the direction the stimulus is going to go... We don't want to wait for it to come to us, we want to be on the offense."
- **Referential language.** "LSAC is very good with referential language. They use words like *this*, *they*, *that*, put in the place of whatever term they're referring to... It's compacting it all into the reference, but it's very sneaky, because we lose track and get disorganized." The fix is mechanical: substitute the referent back in. "Now it's very organized and very clear. We're not letting them play and move us around."
- **A fact can never be a conclusion.** The core content of the session. "There's something that's understated on the LSAT — for some reason people don't really learn this — but a fact can never ever be a conclusion, no matter what." He distinguished it from colloquial usage: "People say Donald Trump is the worst president or best president and that's a fact. That's not actually a fact, that's an opinion disguised as one. A fact is just describing the world as it is. You don't justify the way the world lives... it's just empirically verified."
- **Two tests for the main conclusion.** It must be a statement of opinion, and it must be explicitly supported by at least one other statement. "The way we figure that out is by asking yourself — this is a phrase I find very useful — **why should I believe that?** And the answer to that should be another line or another statement."
- **Recall and rephrase, don't hunt for synonyms.** "It's not that you should aim to rephrase it. I call it recalling it and rephrasing it, but you don't want to change words for the sake of changing words... If *influence* is the first thing that comes to mind" — use it. He named the source of her habit directly: "That's what Loophole focuses on too much."
- **Illustration over paraphrase.** "Using an example is very useful, very powerful — creating illustrations. Normally politicians send out flyers, whatever campaign material, to get people to like them, to get people to vote for them."
- **Structure over content; why over what.** "You're not reading for content, you're reading for structure... When you are able to read structure and be able to categorize rather than just trying to understand what is being said, you're more so trying to understand *why* it's there, not *what's* there. The what is way less important than the why."
- **The Role/Method/Main Conclusion family is the master skill.** "It's literally all the same question type, the same family — role, method, main conclusion. When you're really able to have a strong grasp of that, translating that to RC is usually pretty natural." And: "That's one of the best indicators for your ability to do RC. Getting better at that skill will increase your RC skill by a dramatic amount." He also flagged why students undervalue it: "Most students don't realize how important these are because they learn it earlier on in prep. They don't understand that for RC, for flaw questions, there's a lot of micro sub-skills under them."
- **The LSAT is not a content test.** "No passage is the same. It's not a test of your ability to know about bumblebees. It's about understanding the underlying structure — basically having LSAC glasses on, X-ray glasses, being able to see behind the syntax, the words, and see what the moves are that are happening behind the words."
- **Repeatable process produces repeatable score.** "You need to have a repeatable approach to get a repeatable, predictable outcome."

## Teaching moves

- **Fly-on-the-wall diagnostic first.** "I'm just going to let you do the question completely on your own. I'm going to be in the background. I'm not going to say anything. I'm a fly on the wall. So then I'm not here. Take as long as you want." Only after she answered did he engage.
- **Explicitly de-emphasize correctness.** "It's not so important to worry about whether you get the answer right or wrong. It's more the methodology of how you get there." He never told her whether B was right.
- **Take the stimulus off the screen and make her retell it.** "The reason why I took it away from the screen was that if you're not able to articulate it in a natural setting... If you don't have a strong grasp of the stimulus, what's on the left side of the screen, it's very difficult to know what to get on the right side of the screen, which is the answers." He warned her first: "I'm putting you on the spot, so I know that's not going to be super easy."
- **Announce the interruption style before starting.** "I'm going to have you read out loud the stimulus, and I'm going to interject quite a bit. So forgive me in advance."
- **Stop at the first word.** He cut her off after "Normally" and asked what that word does in ordinary conversation, before any LSAT framing. She produced the answer herself and he confirmed: "Phenomenal. Great. So that's a great hypothesis."
- **Ask for the operationalized procedure, not the concept.** Twice: "What's the actual way to operationalize that? What's the actual approach — step one, step two? How would you go about it?" And on active reading: "How do you do that? Pen to paper, how would I do it?"
- **Reward the small catch loudly.** When she spotted "evidently": "Very good, you're very quick. Most people don't notice. That's a very good eye for detail."
- **Warn against keyword dependence — chosen deliberately for an LSAT Lab user.** "The reason why I chose this question was... especially at LSAT Lab, because I've gone through a lot of the paths, there's a very natural reflexive behavior to see the word *but*. The approach is good for many things, but the big thing I find is sometimes it's too reliant on keywords. **We don't want to go on a keyword hunt.** When people see the word *but* they automatically think [the conclusion] is going to follow, which is usually the case — but it's not always the case. To be a dynamic LSAT taker, a person that will score in the highest range, you can't allow these little hacks to get in the way."
- **Diagnose from profile, then check.** He guessed her academic background before asking: "I have a guess, but I want to see if I have a good idea here. How did you do in your undergrad?" She said 3.8, premed. "Yeah, I had a feeling. That's why I asked." Then the diagnosis: **"A lot of students who are naturally bright, who have a very high aptitude academically, get punished with RC — because it's not as easy for them to see a structured approach. It's more so vibes, and they get away with vibes a lot. What ends up happening is they'll do decent in one section and another section they won't do so well, because it's not a repeatable structured approach."** She confirmed immediately: "Yeah, I have the same exact experience."
- **The score-in-your-room line.** "It's great that you did well in your room in the middle of wherever you are, but unfortunately law schools don't care about that score in your room. I wish they did. I would be the president if they did."
- **Refuse to quote a point gain.** When she asked how much students improve and how fast: "It's so contingent on so many factors... If you're a single mom with three kids working another job, that's a different experience than someone doing this full-time. For me to give you an answer like *you can get nine points before that day* — I'd be dishonest if I just gave you a number." He did separate the two horizons: "October is a different conversation. That's like you can make a world of change by October. Even the August test, you can cover a decent amount of ground."
- **Hedge his own read repeatedly.** "I'm being very premature here." "Don't take what I say as gospel." "I could be wrong, take this with a grain of salt." "I'd have to see your analytics. I'd have to see your data."
- **Honest, specific resource ratings.** PowerScore RC: "It's good to get you an idea of what the LSAT is testing, a very general sense, but it's not a good resource to be able to improve your RC score by any means." Loophole: "A really really good book, one of the best books in the market." LSAT Lab RC: "It's okay, it's solid." He was candid about his own past: "I went through all of it — 7Sage, LSAT Lab, Loophole, PowerScore. I was just desperate. It was pretty embarrassing and actually very counterproductive. I would not recommend it. I didn't know what I didn't know."
- **Sell customization against "factory style."** On LSAT Lab's passage taxonomy: "It's cool, but it's very factory style. You want it personalized towards Kaia. It can't just be factory style — PowerScore even worse, it's just very generic. It doesn't give you a way to really have a repeatable framework."

## Worked examples

- **Campaign ads, main conclusion** (her self-declared worst question type; she answered B). Stimulus: *Normally* political candidates send out campaign material in order to influence popular opinion. But the recent ads for Epson's campaign were sent to too few households to serve this purpose effectively. The ads were evidently sent out to test their potential to influence popular opinion. They covered a wide variety of topics, and Epson's campaign has been spending heavily on follow-up to gauge their effect on recipients.
  - Line 1 read as the normal case, with "normally" flagged as an exception signal.
  - Line 2 is the trap: it follows "but," so students take it as the conclusion. Joshua argued it is **factual**, not opinion. The reasoning: "too few households" implies a threshold number that would have been needed, and he either met it or did not. "That's an objective empirical fact. If there's a certain number that needs to be met and he didn't meet that number, it's not an opinion." He also caught Kaia over-reading it: she paraphrased it as "his campaign strategy was not effective," and he pushed back — ineffective *for that purpose* is not ineffective in general, and we don't know that influencing opinion was his purpose. "We can't make that jump."
  - Line 3 ("evidently sent out to test their potential") is the main conclusion. Kaia spontaneously flagged "evidently" as a conclusion indicator. She then articulated the contrast herself: "He wants to see what the opinion is already, not to change the opinions of people." Joshua: "He's doing a trial run. He wants to see what the lay of the land is."
  - Line 4 (wide variety of topics, heavy follow-up spending to gauge effect) is support for line 3. He had her substitute the referent for "their," and then asked which line line 4 supports. She got it.
