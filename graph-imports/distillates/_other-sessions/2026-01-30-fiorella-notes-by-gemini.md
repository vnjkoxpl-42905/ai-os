# Fiorella Meyer, 2026-01-30

## LSAT concepts
- **Flaw** (the session's spine): both questions worked were flaw questions, attacked by first isolating the conclusion, then asking whether the evidence guarantees it.
- **Necessary assumption / unstated link**: the correct answer on Q1 (D) states "an understanding of DNA is essential to make well-informed personal medical choices or to make good public policy decisions," the bridge the author never supplied.
- **Scope shift**: 20% knowing enough about DNA to follow a news story is used as the standard for the far broader "medical concepts, personal choices, and public policy."
- **Main conclusion**: Joshua's claim that "at least 50% of the mistakes that people make" come from letting the conclusion "slip." A partly-restated conclusion is how answer A on Q1 traps people.
- **Equivocation fallacy**: named explicitly when answer choices B and D said "uses the term universal/order ambiguously." Illustrated with the word "arms" (biceps vs. guns).
- **Appeal to emotion**: answer C's fallacy. Illustrated with "think about the children" and both sides of the ICE debate.
- **Method of reasoning**: scanning each claim for its striking feature, percentages, conditionals, causal claims, and noting which groups a percentage refers to.
- **Belief vs. fact**: "90% believes itself to be well informed" is not "90% is well informed."

## Teaching moves
- **The criteria framework, his central teaching of the session.** Every conclusion needs two things: an objective criteria (a standard, a yardstick) and proof that the facts meet that standard. "One of those two things are going to always be the problem. Either standard or the facts are not falling into the standard. Always. It's always going to be that. That's really the LSAT if you understand that really well."
- Makes criteria concrete with a non-LSAT claim: "if I make a claim to say that America is better than Canada... I can't just make a claim without having a criteria for what would constitute better and worse." Extends it to court: "the criteria would be the law... it has to meet these conditions to be able to say this person is guilty."
- Teaches suspicion of undefined standards by pushing on the phrase itself: "knowing enough about DNA to understand a news story. Is that high level? Is the news like Scientific American, or is the news like CNN?" Then the principle: "the scary thing about any argument is when they leave things kind of open-ended for interpretation because we know that they're going to play within that gap. That's where they have fun."
- Insists the evidence must **guarantee** the conclusion: "It has to be like 1 plus 1 equals two. If the conclusion has anything that could be possibly false based off the evidence, we do want to notice it."
- **Rewrites the author's argument to see what was missing**: "if they said in the evidence, to make informed personal choices you need to have met this threshold of knowing enough about DNA, this argument would have been valid. But they didn't say that. They didn't do their work, their homework."
- **Praises the instinct before correcting the answer.** On A vs D: "You're so close. It's like 50% right... A is technically not wrong. It's just not enough." And "you were knocking on the door."
- **Method for dense or unfamiliar stimuli**: break it into bite-sized pieces, don't chase what a term means in the world, work from the base word. "What does universality sound like? What's the base word there?" -> universe -> "whatever you see the word universal is talking about all encompassing, everything under the universe." Also: "you don't really want to engage if it's not instantly available to you as you read it. They'll give you enough information."
- **Analogy for historical relativism**, built live from what the student was doing (her husband was cooking steak): in 300 years people may see us as barbarians for killing an animal for enjoyment, "there's a time in history where they would literally look at a live animal, split its neck and eat it like barbarians." Also uses slavery and marriage age as normal-in-context examples.
- Maps the abstract stimulus onto US politics to make it recognizable: objective standard vs. subjective standard as "the definition of like the Democrats versus the Republicans," gender as the worked example. Names it as one of the biggest dividing lines in her country.
- Names the **he-said/she-said argument pattern**: "where the author seems to arbitrarily side with one view over the other," without justifying why.
- Points out the argument is **self-refuting**: "if you say that truth changes over time, then how about the truth that you're saying that truth changed over time, will that change as well? It kind of collapses on itself."
- **Gives permission to lose one**: "these are one of those questions you can kind of forgive yourself from getting wrong if you don't know the context." And on this stimulus: "they overloaded the content matter. So it's really just there to intimidate you. But he doesn't really give us an argument. He just kind of tells us that it's true because it's true."
- **Sells fallacy memorization as leverage**: knowing the names "saves you so much mental energy," helps with speed, "it's like using a cheat code." Prescribes keeping the fallacy sheet beside her while drilling, and hunting fallacies in the wrong answers too.
- Validates her self-study loop rather than correcting it: "you're wrestling with it, and as you wrestle with it you'll get better."

## Worked examples
- **Q1, DNA and healthcare (flaw).** Premises: 90% of the population believes itself well informed about healthcare; only 20% knows enough about DNA to understand a news story about DNA. Conclusion: apparently at least 80% do not know enough about medical concepts to make well-informed personal medical choices or good public policy decisions about healthcare. Path taken: flag the percentages and the groups they cover, separate "believes" from "is," attack the undefined standard "a news story," identify the conclusion's two halves, then find the missing criteria. A was rejected as covering only half the conclusion, B as talking about what needs to be rather than what is, C as making one half of the conclusion a prerequisite for the other. D correct.
- **Q2, postmodernism (flaw).** Premises: the postmodern view rejects modern assumptions about order and the universality of truth; grand theories of the modern era are now seen as limited by the social and historical context in which they were elaborated; belief in order has given way to belief in the importance of irregularity and chaos. Conclusion: it follows that we inhabit a world full of irregular events in which there are no universal truths. Path taken: strip the vocabulary down (universal = all-encompassing, grand theories = big theories, historical context = what was normal then), recognize two competing views, notice the author simply picks the postmodern side without justification. B and D rejected as equivocation, which does not occur. C rejected as appeal to emotion, which does not occur. E rejected because it attacks a premise and because examples are not required for an argument. A correct.
