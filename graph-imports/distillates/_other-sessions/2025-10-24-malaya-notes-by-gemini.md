# Malaya Jules, 2025-10-24

Two weeks out from the November test. Malaya is getting flaw questions right but too slowly, so Joshua diagnosed the bottleneck from her drill timings, then ran a progressive time-pressure ladder (1:20, then 1:00, then 1:10, then 1:30 on a level five) to find where her accuracy actually breaks.

## LSAT concepts

- **Accuracy is not the problem; fallacy recognition speed is.** Reading her drill breakdown, Joshua ruled out conditionals ("flaw doesn't really ask you to do diagramming, it's more just understanding it") and landed on fallacies: "it's not that you don't get them right, it's that you're not efficient with the fallacies." The image: "it should be almost like a speed bump for you," not a stop.
- **Two kinds of flaw answer choice.** Descriptive ones "are just describing the premise and conclusion, and you have to be able to identify what part they're talking about with the premise, what part with the conclusion." Fallacy-naming ones are the separate skill, and Joshua's hypothesis was that Malaya recognizes fallacies in stimuli faster than in answer choices, which she confirmed: "there are some I don't recognize as well... recognize it in the stimulus, but recognizing it in the answer choice" is harder.
- **Match-attack.** For descriptive answer choices, map each clause to a part of the argument. "Infers" = the conclusion; "merely from the claim" = the single premise it rests on; "on the basis of" = the evidence. Do it fast and only glance back at the stimulus "as a touch-up, to confirm what you already know."
- **"Fails to consider" is a weakener frame.** Malaya's own rule, endorsed: if the content that follows "fails to consider" is actually *consistent* with the conclusion, the choice is dead.
- **Correlation and conditional are not exclusive.** On the arms/war question Malaya said she had read it as causal but thought it was really conditional. Joshua: "they're both — you can be both correlated and conditional. That's why it's interesting, because you notice there's two ways of looking at it."
- **Indicator words are cheat codes.** "Use the cheat codes, like indicator words. Use them. Never ever go the long path." Specifically: *only* signals a strong conclusion and, on a flaw question, all but guarantees a sufficient/necessary confusion — "if I know the flaw is based off the only, I know they're going to introduce mistaking something sufficient for necessary." *For* introduces a premise. *And* introduces another premise, so "we don't usually see 'and' before a premise from a conclusion." *Thus* and *after all* mark the support structure.
- **The five-second keyword scan.** Before engaging the stimulus, spend four or five seconds scanning for indicator words. "If I don't see something, don't linger, just go into the question." Deliberately non-committal: "you don't have to commit to finding something. Just whatever pops up."
- **This-and-that means argument by analogy.** "If they're comparing two ideas, two things... whenever you see this and that, you know instantly that almost certainly the argument is going to be an argument of analogy — saying one is true, the other must be as well, or one is true so the other one isn't."
- **Belief language flags the appeal-to-opinion flaw.** "When you're seeing a lot of words about belief, believe, it instantly gives you that reflective feeling of, yeah, we're talking about beliefs here and there's no justification except for beliefs."
- **A true-but-not-flawed statement is not a flaw answer.** On the Smith assault question, choice E ("having a violent character is not necessarily associated with the commission of violent crimes") fails because it names no reasoning error: "it has to be bad reasoning."
- **Don't treat every question the same.** Getting a flaw right slowly steals time from the questions that need it: "if you get the answer right and it costs you time that would have been useful for questions that... would have needed more time, like must-be-true conditionals."
- **Warm up before the test.** "Do a set of questions, even ones you've already seen before... a light preview, don't exhaust yourself, and then you're in law mode."

## Teaching moves

- **Read the timing column, not the score column.** Malaya reported the drill as a success ("I got them all right"), and Joshua immediately asked to see the score breakdown again, then a specific question, then the breakdown again. The diagnosis came entirely from per-question durations, not from correctness.
- **Test the diagnosis rather than assert it.** Having guessed the bottleneck was fallacy identification in answer choices, he said so and then built the rest of the session as an experiment: "I would guess it's not — it's actually arbitrary, but I'm assuming it's likely to do with that. But I want to actually test that theory."
- **The time-pressure ladder.** Untimed for the first two questions to establish a baseline, then 1:20, then a deliberate tightening: "you're doing really well here, so I'm going to tighten it up and bring it down to one minute. I want to see where the pressure starts cracking." When one minute broke her ("I don't even remember the answer choices"), he immediately restored 10-15 seconds rather than letting the failure stand.
- **Name the pressure effect explicitly.** "Even if you could do it in a minute, knowing that you only have a minute makes even more pressure. It's like, I maybe could have done this if I didn't know I only had a minute."
- **Ask for process after every timed question.** "Tell me how exactly, what was the process that you went through to get this answer?" and "what did you notice were the features of the different answer choices?" The answer, not the process, is the least interesting part.
- **Prescribe a pregame mantra for the hardest tier.** "Having like a pregame plan, a mission plan that you write... something you have as a mantra, because they're difficult but they're not difficult in different ways. They're just usually difficult in the way the answer choices look."
- **Correct the conclusion identification before the flaw.** Twice, Malaya's error traced to picking the wrong conclusion. Joshua did not go to the answer choices; he went to the first sentence and the indicator word.
- **Reframe her failure as difficulty, not decline.** When she blamed herself for overthinking what she assumed was an easy early question ("now that I see it's a question three, I just overthought it"), Joshua corrected the premise: "this one actually is a level four question." Her relief was immediate: "that makes me feel way better."
- **Give the green light to trust her own eliminations.** On the Walcott fossils question she asked whether any distractor was genuinely attractive. "I'd probably give you the green light... they're only difficult because they're fluffy, but they actually have nothing to do with the argument. I wouldn't even look at any of them as real contenders."
- **Notice the warm-up curve and turn it into a test-day instruction.** "The more we got into it, even as the questions got harder, you just see right through it. The juices were flowing." Malaya reached the same conclusion herself in the same beat: "I need to warm up before my test, I didn't do that last time."
- **Offer application help unprompted and without pressure.** "It goes without saying, feel free at any point to send me any of your material to look at. I would love to help."

## Worked examples

- **Flaw: arms buildup and war (untimed).** Sharp increases in weapons acquisition have consistently preceded wars, therefore arms control agreements will preserve peace. Malaya's instinct was right at the stimulus level — "they got weapons for war, the weapons didn't cause the war, so if you don't have weapons it doesn't mean peace" — but she picked D (fails to consider that a sharp increase in a nation's weapons acquisition may be a response to neighboring nations' armament) because it looked like reverse causation. Joshua's kill: it does not dispute the conclusion. "If we all buy weapons at the same time and then we all go to war after, it could still be because of the weapons." Malaya then produced the cleaner version herself: "'fails to consider' is usually a weakener, but what follows is consistent with the conclusion." Credited answer A: infers merely from events of one type consistently preceding events of a second type that an event of the second type will not occur unless an event of the first type occurs. She had been put off A because it read as describing a conditional argument while she was reading the stimulus as causal.

- **Flaw: the politician and the union leaders (untimed).** Union leaders would benefit from the policy they are advocating, therefore their argument should be rejected. Malaya prephrased correctly and chose C: treats circumstances potentially affecting the union leaders' argument as sufficient to discredit that argument. She rejected B ("presumes without justification that anyone whose political motivations are clearly discernible is an unreliable source") as too strong. Joshua's coaching was purely about speed: "the faster you can do that the better... you don't want to have to actually look at the argument every time. You want to have the idea of what the argument did, then review the argument as a touch-up."

- **Flaw: equivocation (1:20, answered in under a minute).** Malaya: "I never seen this before, but I very rarely see an argument with equivocation, so I was like, this is kind of juicy." Joshua: "You kind of bodied that, to be honest."

- **Flaw: Mr. Smith's violent character (1:20, answered with seconds to spare).** Miss Lopez testified that Smith shouted and threatened her; Smith never refuted it; therefore Smith has a violent character; therefore find him guilty of assaulting Mr. Jackson. Malaya initially identified "Smith has a violent character" as the conclusion and asked Joshua to confirm. He corrected: the guilty finding is the conclusion, and the violent character is the reason given for it. She still landed on C. She rejected A (aggressive behavior is not a sure indicator of violent character) because it "forces you to say that shouting loudly and threatening is aggressive behavior, which the stimulus just doesn't give you," and rejected two others on plain factual grounds (Smith never testified; nothing says Lopez is not loud or aggressive). She pushed back on why E was wrong; the answer, jointly reached, was that assaulting obviously is a violent crime, so the statement is simply false rather than a reasoning error. Joshua: "it's like saying being violent doesn't mean you're violent. It's like, yeah, it does actually."

- **Flaw: humorous television advertisements (1:00, then +15 seconds; she missed it).** Humorous television advertisements are the only effective ones, for they attract people's attention and hold it long enough for the message to be conveyed, and for an advertisement to be effective it must convey its message. Malaya diagrammed premise chains from the wrong starting point — "I said effective then humorous, humorous then attract, attract then..." — because she treated the *last* sentence as the conclusion. Joshua rebuilt it on two words: *only* in the first sentence flags the conclusion and its strength, and *for* immediately after flags what supports it. The flaw is sufficient mistaken for necessary. Malaya: "that's what I missed."

- **Flaw: Walcott's fossil classification (1:10).** A recent classification of an unusual fossil set is more likely to be accurate than Walcott's own, after all he is a prominent member of the establishment and thus unlikely to produce classifications conforming to anything outside it. Malaya chose A immediately and correctly, characterizing it as near-ad-hominem: "they're making a conclusion about this set of fossils based off of this guy and what his position is." Joshua used it to teach the *thus*/*after all* scan and the this-versus-that analogy tell.

- **Flaw: belief treated as fact (level five, 1:30).** Malaya chose D: supports its conclusion by appealing solely to opinion in a matter that is largely factual. Her own account of the delay is the useful part: "I was misreading D, which is why it took me so long. I thought it was appealing to opinion in a matter that is factual, but I think it's treating opinion as factual." She also had to rule out a circularity answer: "so much of the same words, is it circular? But it wasn't circular, it's just the must from belief to must." Joshua had planted the trap deliberately: "I was looking to see if you'd catch that."

## Student state

- **Test is two weeks out** (November). Her framing for the week: "optimizing my time and score, because I think that really is the difference."
- **Accuracy is essentially solved; speed is the whole remaining problem.** She got every question in her flaw drill right. The timings were the complaint: "40 seconds, 49 seconds, a minute over, a minute over, three minutes over." Joshua's read: "you're in a really good spot where you won't usually get a question wrong unless there's some unique situation."
- **Specific bottleneck: mapping a known gap to lofty answer-choice language.** "I know the gap in the argument and... it takes me too long to map what they're saying to the problem in the argument." She wants the translation of "fails to consider" and "takes for granted" to become automatic.
- Says she knows all the fallacies cold ("I definitely know them all") but recognizes them faster in stimuli than in answer choices.
- **Hard must-be-true questions at the end of a section are her remaining fear object.** "I don't want to fear any question type... it's a mini freak out." She proposed writing them off as too niche to be worth four minutes of checking, and Joshua agreed the type is "very niche."
- Value judgment questions "still isn't super comfortable."
- **Overthinks early, low-numbered questions** after a diet of level fours and fives: "it's kind of hard to go back to levels one, two, and three and just do the basics. Like they're not trying to confuse me in any way."
- **She warms up.** Both she and Joshua noticed she got faster and sharper as the session went on, even as difficulty rose. She did not warm up before the October test.
- **Two conclusion-identification misses in one session**, both under time pressure, both recoverable once she looked at indicator words. This is the single highest-leverage fix available before the test.
- Careful about re-drilling questions she has seen: "I'm trying not to just choose an answer when I know it."
- **Law school applications are barely started.** Resume and personal statement drafted; "everything else needs to be drafted in the month of November." Weekend plans: "just studying."
