# Azaria Lavender, 2025-10-26

Recap of the necessary assumption taxonomy (linking vs defender, and the reasoning types that signal each), a recap of the three kinds of flaw answer choice, then review of the questions Azaria missed on her drills and on PT 146. The session's spine is a claim that reasoning type predicts answer shape, so identifying the type early primes you for what the credited answer must look like.

## LSAT concepts

- **Linking assumption**: what the author was thinking but did not say. "It's ultimately seeing where the author was thinking something but he didn't say it out loud. So you're adding a piece to the author's train of thought." Joshua's illustration: "Azaria wants to go to Howard Law School, therefore Azaria wants to go to a great law school" — the unstated link is that Howard is a great law school. He noted this is what the word *assumption* means in ordinary speech: "whenever we say assumption we're usually thinking of a linking assumption... he must have been thinking that. We're not usually thinking of sufficient assumptions."
- **Defender assumption**: what the author must be *rejecting*. Azaria had it backwards ("something the author has to agree with"), and Joshua corrected with the Alabama example: "If you tell me you're from Alabama, I have to obviously be rejecting the fact that you're from Japan. No one's thinking of all the things that you're not. But you do passively, implicitly reject any other place but Alabama." The formal statement: "all the things that are against the argument that you have to assume I don't agree with, because if I did agree with it my argument would just get destroyed."
- **The negation test is "our golden ticket," especially for defenders.** "If I negate the defender, will my argument get weak? Because if this is technically defending my argument, this is an idea that I have to reject. I have to wax on, wax off. I have to wipe it."
- **Negate to the weakest sufficient form.** "Think of the logically weakest force that would take you over the line. Don't think of it as necessarily extreme." So *only X* negates to *there is at least one other thing*, not to *X doesn't matter*.
- **Reasoning types that signal a defender:**
  - **Causation / cause and effect** — "if I see cause and effect, expect it to be defender. You're going to rule out alternative explanations."
  - **Comparison**, which is broad and has sub-forms: *this versus that* (two groups, identities, or ideas); *recommendation*; *before versus after*; *net effect*; *assuming similarity or difference*.
- **Recommendation is a comparison.** Azaria produced the reason herself: "they're trying to say that that's better than the other options." Joshua sharpened it: "the world where this plan is instituted is better than the world as is... either there's more benefits on the other side, or there's less downside. The scale has to go towards being in surplus."
- **Before-versus-after is usually causation wearing a comparison's clothes.** "Before they took the medication they were very sick, and after they took the medication there was a miraculous healing. This is somewhat cause and effect, but it's also comparison." The check: "is it a fair thing to compare, if all things were equal, and was that one factor the difference-maker?"
- **Assuming similarity / difference.** "Because Azaria is from Alabama and Joshua is from Canada, they're both from North America, so they must have the same preference in the types of food they eat. Maybe not, maybe so." The test is always relevance: "they might be different in that one way. Does that mean they're different in the other thing they're talking about?"
- **Reasoning types that signal a linking assumption:** conditional, new concept in the conclusion, and causal chain. Azaria guessed correlation; Joshua ruled it out as a subtype: "correlation is actually under causation."
- **Causal chain.** A causes B, B causes C, therefore A causes C. "Think of chains as linking, linking, linking. You're linking the bridge. You're taking the missing piece and adding it to it."
- **New concept in the conclusion.** If a term appears in the conclusion that never appeared in the evidence, "they're assuming that thing is equivalent to something else in the evidence, because they're using it interchangeably." The task is constructive, not critical: "we don't want to call it out and say I'm not sure about that. We want to affirm them — if this thing is true, then that new concept also is true. We want to build that bridge for them."
- **The three kinds of flaw answer choice:**
  - **Assumption** — signaled by *fails to establish*, *takes for granted*, *presumes*. Test: if we took it away, does the argument collapse?
  - **Objection** — signaled by *fails to consider*, *overlooks*. "Objection is just weakening." Test: "if this is true, how would this address the conclusion? Would it make your conclusion directly weakened?"
  - **Descriptive** — method-of-reasoning phrasing: "the author infers X on the grounds of Y," "bases his conclusion on Y." Two checks: identify which structural part of the argument each clause names, and confirm the description is accurate.
- **"Fails to establish" and "fails to consider" are near-opposites.** "We do not want to mix those up. They sound very close... but they're almost opposite in a weird way."
- **Necessary assumption answers should be weakly worded.** "The answer should be very weak. We want soft language." Corollary: "*only* should be a red flag. Only is almost never ever going to be the answer" — because "all it requires is one conceivable exception and the answer choice is disproven."
- **The "only thing mentioned" trap.** "The reason *only* could even sound reasonable is that it's the only thing they mentioned. Remembering that the only thing mentioned does not necessarily mean it's the only thing that exists. It takes the stuff the author mentioned and acts like the author is assuming that's the only stuff."
- **Run to the "not" answers first.** "When we go to necessary assumption questions, run to the not answers first. Attack those first. Skim through them, look for the not answers." Absence of a *not* on a defender question is "a very bad sign," though not decisive.
- **Anti-causal claims are as strong as causal ones.** "Me claiming that there is no cause and effect is as bad as me saying there is a cause... those are both equally problematic claims." This is why an argument concluding a hypothesis is *wrong* is itself attackable.
- **Conditionals and trigger facts.** A conditional with several necessary conditions means missing any one kills the sufficient. "When we see a conditional we want to pay attention to if there are any facts that could trigger that rule, or even trigger the contrapositive." For must-be-true / most strongly supported: "if you see a conditional, expect the contrapositive is where they're going to go with it. Almost always the contrapositive."
- **Unless = if not.** "You want to turn it into an if-then. Unless whatever follows, you're just negating that idea. You're saying if not."
- **Group mentions signal comparison, almost always.** "Whenever they identify a group, think of comparison instantaneously, because that group is only going to be mentioned to say either that group is more likely or less likely than some other group, or than the general population. It's almost never going to be exclusively talking about just that group."
- **Prediction before answer choices.** Azaria named it as what helps her most, and Joshua endorsed it hard: "That's arguably the most important thing, because it tells you two things. If you cannot make a prediction, that means you do not understand the stimulus enough. And it also tells you what the answer's probably going to be like, which makes you more efficient."
- **Answer-choice inspection starts with the first few words.** *Compares*, *fails to consider*, *infers* — read them as instructions, then verify against the argument: "when we see they're comparing two things, ask yourself what is that comparison? What is that early stage? What is the later stage?"

## Teaching moves

- **Quiz the taxonomy cold before applying it.** The first fifteen minutes are pure recall: "what is a linking assumption asking you to get to?", "the defender assumption — remind me what that was about," "do you remember the types of arguments, the reasoning types, for defender arguments?", "do you remember the three types of answer choices?" He corrects the wrong recall on the spot and moves on.
- **Personal analogies drawn from the student's own life.** Every abstraction was grounded in Azaria specifically: Howard Law School for linking, Alabama versus Japan for defender, "because Azaria studied hard for the LSAT... therefore she goes to a good law school" for causal chains. He even self-corrected mid-analogy: "I always think it's Georgia, but it's Alabama, correct?"
- **Argue for the small early win.** A distinctive framing: identifying the reasoning type early is worth it partly for morale. "The fact that you have a small W at the beginning of your stimulus will encourage you to really engage with the passage. The quickest point you can touch base with the stimulus gives you the ability to have confidence as you go through it."
- **Check whether the vocabulary is landing.** When Azaria stalled, he stopped and asked directly: "when I say reasoning type, is that clear as to what you're referring to? I want to make sure I'm not speaking over [you]." He then re-listed the categories before asking again. She got it: "It sounds like it may be comparison, defender."
- **Break a confusing stimulus into checkpoints.** On the camera lens question: "let's break it into smaller pieces... up until the point where you get into the camera lenses in the first line, you're fine, right? And then the resolution of a lens being the degree of detail... that part is fine so far?" He isolates exactly where comprehension stops.
- **Admit when a question is genuinely hard.** "This is a tough answer. I won't lie to you. This is a very tough answer." And later, "the language is tricky."
- **Make her extract the takeaway rather than stating it.** "What do we take from this answer choice that we go forward with? What do we want to do next time?" Azaria: "I would avoid answers with strong wording like only."
- **Reward the cross-question connection loudly.** When Azaria connected the "longest known period" trap in the lobster question to the "only" trap in the camera question, Joshua broke stride: "Wow. Right. You got it. You made a perfect parallel. Exactly."
- **Trump as a wording heuristic.** For over-strong answer choices: "largest, the biggest, the greatest — whatever terms sound like something that comes out of Donald Trump's mouth. We want answer choices that are more conservative."
- **The lawyer-applying-the-law frame for must-be-true.** "If somebody came to you and said, hey, they caught me with my hands about to grab a piece of gold, but I didn't leave the store, what does that mean for me? Your goal is to say, let me look at the law and see how the law applies to your situation. Think of it as taking a fact and applying it to the rules. That's what most must-be-true questions are asking you to do."
- **Emphasize the next action over the explanation.** "The biggest thing is activating this idea of what's the next action I'm going to add to my toolkit to prevent myself from making this mistake. And it might be a micro action. It doesn't have to be a huge overhaul."
- **Deliberate slowness in practice, speed in sections.** "You want to do that intentionally in your practice, but in your practice sections you don't actually have to do that exact deep [check], because you'll do it faster, more efficiently."
- **On platform superstition.** Azaria asked whether to do her LR section on Law Hub. Joshua endorsed it for state, not accuracy: "sometimes people get a bit too superstitious, like the test is different on Law Hub or Demon. If you know how to get the answer right, it's not going to matter whether you use one platform or the other. But it's good to get yourself in the mental state."
- **Let the data decide the plan.** Asked whether to do a full practice test, he refused to commit: "tomorrow's timed section will tell us what the move is. That will give us a very exact answer."

## Worked examples

- **Necessary assumption: camera lens resolution (level five; she got it wrong).** Manufacturers advertise cameras by citing lens resolution, the degree of detail a lens can reproduce in the image it projects onto film. Differences between cameras in this respect are irrelevant for practical photography, however, since all modern lenses are so excellent that they project far more detail onto the film than any photographic film is capable of reproducing in a developed image.

  Joshua drove the reasoning-type identification first ("differences between cameras" → comparison → defender → expect a *not* in the credited answer), flagged *however* and *since* as structure markers, and flagged that "practical photography" is a new concept in the conclusion — "not that it has to be the answer, but it's a good sign."

  The prephrase: "is there a way that differences in lens resolution could be relevant to practical photography?" Azaria offered other camera attributes; Joshua added color capture, and viewing images on a computer rather than on film.

  Credited answer B: in determining the amount of detail reproduced in a developed image, differences in the resolutions of available lenses do *not* compound the deficiencies of available film. Joshua's concrete unpacking: camera X does 10,000 pixels per inch, camera Y does 5,000, film caps at 1,000 — but degraded film may only achieve 700 with one lens and 800 with another, so lens differences do reach the developed image. "The author was assuming that the different lens resolutions have no effect on the developed image, and this gives us a way in which they could."

  Azaria had chosen E, which contained *only*. Two independent kills: the word *only*, and the negation ("maybe there is at least one other factor") does nothing to the argument, because "our author doesn't need lens resolution and film resolution to be the only thing."

- **Necessary assumption: lobsters in traps.** Marine biologists hypothesized that lobsters kept together in traps eat one another in response to hunger. Periodic checks have found lobsters sharing traps for weeks, and eight lobsters shared one trap for two months without eating one another. Therefore the hypothesis is clearly wrong.

  Azaria initially could not see the gap ("I'm still confused"), so Joshua rebuilt it claim by claim. Once she named the first line as cause and effect, she found the gap herself: "they said they were eating them in response to hunger, so maybe the ones who were trapped together weren't hungry enough." Joshua's framing of the stance required: "we're arguing against the author, so we're taking the biologists' perspective. We're trying to make an excuse for why these little lobsters didn't eat each other."

  Distractor C fails on relevance: "it doesn't really matter if it's usual or unusual to see eight lobsters together." Distractor B (two months is the longest known period lobsters have shared a trap) fails on strength — "the author doesn't need to assume that two months is the longest period ever. She only needs to believe that two months is long enough for them to start eating each other."

  Credited answer E: it is *not* the case that any food the eight lobsters might have obtained was enough to ward off hunger. Azaria explained it correctly: "if it wasn't enough to ward off hunger, then they wouldn't have had the urge to eat one another in the first place." Joshua completed it: "a counterexample would involve hungry lobsters in a trap that still didn't eat each other. The lobsters in this trap weren't hungry, so they don't qualify."

- **Most strongly supported: the natural gas station (PT 146; she got it right but flagged it, split between C and D).** Any new natural gas power electrical generation station must be located close to a natural gas pipeline, a large body of water for cooling, and transmission lines. It must also be in a region where residents will not oppose construction. Our country has extensive transmission lines, but our gas pipelines run near only three of our large bodies of water, and residents will oppose any significant construction near those bodies of water.

  Azaria identified the conditional, produced the contrapositive, and correctly concluded that missing any one of the four necessary conditions kills it. Joshua: "Mama mia, you got it." She then killed D herself on two grounds: it says there are currently no such stations near the largest bodies of water, but the argument only discusses *new* stations, and only *three* large bodies of water, not the *largest*. Credited answer C used *unless*; Azaria converted it correctly on request: "if the existing system of natural gas pipelines is not expanded, then no site would be suitable." Joshua: "Phenomenal. Perfect. That's literally perfect."

- **Flaw: generational voting (PT 146; she got it wrong).** Voting records regularly show people over 65 vote in the highest percentages while young adults are least likely to vote; this indicates citizens are becoming increasingly disconnected from the political system with each passing generation.

  Azaria had chosen B, misreading it as being about the relative sizes of generations, and self-corrected on re-read. She then classified the credited answer A as descriptive and named the two stages: young adults and people over 65. The flaw is comparing an early stage of one generation to a later stage of another — a cross-sectional comparison read as a longitudinal trend. Azaria produced the substance independently: "maybe some people just started voting at 65... they're assuming the same people who were voting when they were younger are the same people voting when they're older, but it may be a whole different group of people." Joshua accepted her causal read but insisted comparison was the more useful trigger, since the stimulus names two groups.
