# Mounusha Nunna, 2026-02-03

## LSAT concepts
- **Weaken**: Q1 was a weakener. The test Joshua gives: "whatever is highlighted in yellow should be less likely to be true."
- **Correlation vs. causation**: "more likely" almost unanimously signals a correlation, not an influence. Two things can be correlated with neither causing the other.
- **Correlation to correlation reasoning**: the unorthodox structure in Q1, concluding B correlates with C from "A correlates with B" and "A correlates with C." Joshua flags this as rare and problematic.
- **Common cause**: raised as a weakener candidate and rejected, because a shared cause does not undo a correlation.
- **Relative vs. absolute**: a "more important factor" does not make another factor a non-factor. Also the deeper flaw in Q2: something can advance in absolute terms while relatively being slowed.
- **Comparable / analogy reasoning**: historical dramatists using ghosts compared to modern paranormal TV. The standing question is always "are the things they're comparing fair to compare or unfair to compare?"
- **Flaw**: Q2 was a level-5 flaw question.
- **Opposing viewpoint structure**: "some scientists" signals a conflicting viewpoint the author will push back on.
- **Answer choice styles**: objection vs. assumption vs. descriptive. The stimulus and question stem often force which style the credited answer must take.

## Teaching moves
- **Runs the session as timed cold attempts.** Two minutes on Q1, two and a half to three on Q2, answer stated before any discussion, then line-by-line rebuild together. "I threw you into the fire."
- **Keyword-first reading**: "the keywords are going to be your best friend... it gives us this ability to see where they're susceptible." Runs through the list live: "more likely" = correlation, "studies" = expect causation, "some [group]" = opposing viewpoint incoming, "factor" = cause, "explanation" = cause.
- **Traps the student deliberately to expose a habit.** Asks "what happens if I start smoking today?" She answers "you're more likely to develop health complications." He: "That's a trick. No, we don't know that. That's a danger... It's just a potential statistical overlap. It doesn't mean there's actually any influence."
- **Defines hypothesis by chaining the words**: "hypothesis is literally a potential explanation... and explanation means causation. So all research is doing is trying to figure out causation."
- **Diagrams correlations with a squiggle, not an arrow**, precisely to keep them visually distinct from causal claims: A ~ B, A ~ C, therefore B ~ C. "You don't have to do this, but I find it's really useful because it keeps the structure very clear."
- **Builds a parallel argument to expose the flaw**: law students are more likely than non-law students to have better reading skills; law students are more likely to be stressed; therefore better reading skills correlate with stress. "Being stressed out doesn't make you a better reader. It's probably the opposite."
- **Points out the setup as a bait**: the stimulus dangles the word "cause" ("not thought to be a cause of heart disease") to make you hunt for a causal flaw that isn't there. "They're playing a dirty game... most people won't notice that they didn't actually make a claim of causality at all."
- **A tidy analogy for relative vs. absolute irrelevance**: "just because John runs faster than David doesn't mean David isn't fast."
- **An LSAT-native analogy for advance-despite-impediment**: "Michael scored 170 on his LSAT, 15 points more than his previous score, so he surely was lying when he said slow internet was an impediment on his test." Who knows what he could have scored without it.
- **Makes the student translate abstract answer choices into plain language before judging them.** On answer A: "if we were to turn that into normal English, how would that actually manifest?" She produces the stock-price analogy (steadily rising while dipping) herself, and he confirms it.
- **The mapping discipline, his named highest-leverage skill**: "really abstract language and really forcing yourself to nitpick each word and map it on. Any abstract term should be able to be mapped on. If you cannot do that, then you know that... it's maybe the most underrated skill from any skill that most students are weak at, that ability to take abstract language and map it on." Notes newer LSAT tests exploit this harder.
- **Two-step method on abstract choices**: first check if the choice contains vague or abstract terms, then match those terms against the stimulus to make them concrete.
- **Asks the disambiguating question rather than giving the answer.** To break her wrong pick on E: "what causal relationship does our author think is baseless?" She answers, then sees the former/latter swap herself: "Oh, wait. I see what the issue is."
- **Frames hard practice as calibration**: after a level-5 question, "now when you see easier questions, it will always be okay."

## Worked examples
- **Q1, caffeine and heart disease (weaken).** Premises: recent studies show smokers are more likely than non-smokers to develop heart disease; other studies show smokers are more likely than others to drink caffeinated beverages. Conclusion: even though drinking caffeinated beverages is not thought to be a cause of heart disease, there is a positive correlation between drinking caffeinated beverages and developing heart disease. Correct answer A: smokers who drink caffeinated beverages are less likely to develop heart disease than smokers who do not, which asserts the opposite sign of correlation. B rejected (a more important factor is irrelevant to whether this factor exists). C rejected (strengthens). D rejected (confirms the correlation within a subgroup). E rejected (a common cause of all three is compatible with the correlation still holding).
- **Q2, paranormal TV programs (flaw).** Some scientists warn that popular paranormal TV programs will encourage superstition and thereby impede public scientific understanding. Author: these predictions are baseless, because throughout recorded history dramatists have relied on ghosts and spirits to enliven their stories, and yet scientific understanding of the populace has steadily advanced. Two problems identified: comparable-reasoning (historical dramatists vs. modern TV programs assumed analogous), and relative vs. absolute (advancement can be real while still being slowed by an impediment). Correct answer A: fails to consider that one phenomenon can steadily advance even when it is being impeded by another. E rejected: it swaps former and latter, describing the persistence of the wrong phenomenon. C rejected on "indirectly," which maps onto nothing in the argument. B and D eliminated by her earlier.
