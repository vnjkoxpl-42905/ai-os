# Fiorella Meyer, 2026-01-15

## LSAT concepts

- **Flaw**: the central LR question type, and the one Joshua treats as the master key. "Flaw is the head honcho of all the LR questions because they give you everything: necessary assumption, weakening, your ability to understand fallacies. It's the multi-dimensional question."
- **Three families of flaw answer choices**, of which two were taught this session. Family one is weakening-flavored ("fails to consider X," "overlooks X," "ignores the possibility that X"). Family two is assumption-flavored ("takes for granted X," "fails to establish X," "fails to demonstrate X"). A third family remains for a later session.
- **Weakening-type test (one step)**: assume X is true, and it must directly and severely weaken the conclusion. If it does nothing, the choice is wrong; if it helps, it is definitely wrong.
- **Assumption-type test (two steps)**: step one, if X were true would it benefit the argument. Step two, negate X; the negation must demolish the conclusion. Passing step one alone is not enough.
- **The two families run opposite directions**, which is why flaw questions feel unstable: within a single question some choices are weakening-shaped and some assumption-shaped.
- **Assumption defined properly**: not "something unproven" but "statements that the author takes to be true without explicitly stating it," that is, unstated premises. Joshua blocked the circular definition: "you can't use the word assume in your definition. That's cheating."
- **Necessary assumption**: any amount at or below the gap. It need not close the gap, but without it the gap cannot be closed.
- **Validity**: the evidence must guarantee the conclusion. "If there's even a sliver of difference between the evidence and conclusion, a potential world where it's not true, then the argument is 100% invalid." Validity is binary, never a scale.
- **"And" as a premise conjunction**: it joins two ideas that are both premises.
- **EXCEPT questions**: replace "except" with "the least."
- **Appeal to authority**: the underlying error in the meat argument, though it did not map cleanly to a named flaw answer.

## Teaching moves

- **The $18 for a $20 pack of cigarettes.** His central analogy for assumption, delivered as a scene: a customer dumps a pile of change on the counter hoping it will not be counted, "he starts sweating, he's just like, okay, she's going to catch on to me." The mapping, stated explicitly: "the evidence is what he pays you with, the conclusion is what he's trying to buy, and the difference between those two amounts is the assumption. It's the missing piece." If he handed over the full $20 or $25, no assumption exists. Necessary assumption is then any amount up to the shortfall: "10 cents is not going to pay you the difference, but if he doesn't pay you even at least 10 cents, he can't pay you the $2."
- **Named the argument's rhetorical intent, not just its structure.** "All of these arguments will make it look like he paid you the full amount for the conclusion to be valid, but there's something missing. The conclusion is a bit more, out of the scope of what the evidence is actually giving you."
- **Simulation language for the weakening test.** "Play a game of hypotheticals, almost as though you're simulating what would be the case if we saw that X were activated." And on the standard: it should hit "directly in the middle of the conclusion's forehead."
- **Built the negation test with deliberately absurd examples.** Ashley studied hard, therefore Ashley must have done well. Then a first candidate assumption: "takes for granted Jesus spoke to her during the test." He checked her background before running it ("are you from the Christian background?") and then abandoned it as too muddy, replacing it with a cleaner one: "takes for granted a 180 scorer wrote her test for her." That choice passes step one dramatically (it would obviously help) but fails step two, since Ashley can still have done well without a 180 scorer writing her test. The contrast case, "takes for granted Ashley sat for the test," passes both. The lesson: "just because this would benefit the argument doesn't mean that she actually assumed it."
- **Ordered the two steps for efficiency.** "Before we negate it, because negating it can be an annoying process or more work, first I want to say, would it benefit our argument at all in the first place?"
- **Reframed EXCEPT rather than solving it.** She raised that her "brain wants to do the opposite of what the question is asking." His fix: "replace the word except every time you read it, just instinctively say 'the least.' Which one of these would weaken the argument the least?" Then the second-order warning: most credited EXCEPT answers are not the opposite, they are neutral. "They're going to make it much more nuanced because that's too easy. They're just giving you an answer choice that sits on the middle ground."
- **Made her restate an argument in plain speech when she stalled.** She was hunting for the flaw in the meat argument and kept generating irrelevant objections. He stopped her: "often what happens is we take these arguments too seriously. Repeat the argument to me in the simplest terms, in your own language." Then he moved it into a real conversation: "let's say our friend Dr. Sanjay eats meat, therefore it's obvious that meat is healthy for you. What would you say back to me?" She immediately produced the flaw: "just because they're doing it doesn't mean that it's healthy."
- **Personalized the punchline.** "It's like you're a bodybuilder, so you know. People probably know that they should put the burger and fries down, doesn't mean that they do it." Then the compressed version: "do as they say, not as they do." And the precise formulation: "they're assuming that because they are knowledgeable, they do what they know, or they do what they should be doing. Two different statements."
- **Refused a partly-right answer to force precision.** She said the evidence has to "support" the conclusion. He accepted it, then pushed: "what level of support are we looking for?" until he supplied the standard, that validity requires a guarantee. His reasoning for the harshness: "if you keep it like validity is on a scale, you'll fall into the trap of saying a lot of the arguments sound reasonable."
- **Modeled testing the argument line by line, out loud.** On the fossil-leaf question he narrated his own read, including flagging a word he could not yet use: "they said prehistoric. I want to pay attention to that. I don't know if that means anything for us now, but at least we can keep that in mind." He justified the habit as training rather than as necessary for the answer: "using the questions not to get to the answer right, but to reinforce that pattern recognition."
- **Caught himself making the same assumption as the argument.** "I even said it, I kind of assumed as I was saying it, but I realized that's a problem, because they're assuming that you can tell the climate if we have a higher or lower altitude." He then pressed on the linearity assumption with concrete numbers: one meter could be minus 7, ten meters minus 7 again, one hundred meters back to 97. "We don't know the intervals of the changing of temperatures as we go up."
- **Separated the signal phrase from X.** A correction he had to make twice: "the part that says 'overlooks the possibility' is not the X part. That just tells you that whatever is X is going to follow." X is the content clause, and X is what gets plugged into the argument.
- **Set the expectation that this takes reps.** Stated at the top and again at the close: "it's going to take a few dozen exposures to these answer choices to understand what I'm getting at," and "you're not going to remember which one is weakening and which one's assumption. That will take multiple exposures." Meanwhile she should work with the note open beside her.
- **Told her she already does this implicitly.** "You probably already intuitively see this without realizing it... this just makes it explicitly clear, because you don't want to wrestle with what these answer choices are saying while you're doing the question." Goal state: "it becomes very instinctual."

## Worked examples

- **Flaw, meat in the diet (correct: E, assumption type).** Stimulus, Smith: meat in the diet is healthy despite what some people say; after all, most doctors do eat meat, and who knows more about health than doctors do. Conclusion is "meat in the diet is healthy" ("despite what some people say" is fluff). Two premises joined by "and." The gap: doctors knowing what is healthy does not entail that they act on it. E: "takes for granted that experts do not act counter to what according to their expertise is in their best interest." Step one, if true the conclusion becomes "a slam dunk." Step two, negated (doctors *do* act counter to their expertise) the conclusion collapses. Passes both.
- **Flaw, prehistoric plant altitude (correct: B, weakening type).** Stimulus: by examining the fossilized leaves of any prehistoric plant it is possible to determine the climate in which that specimen grew, because leaf size and shape are unique to a given climate; since the climate at a given location depends on the altitude at that location, it follows that leaf size and shape also indicate the altitude at which the plant grew. The unstated assumption is that climate maps onto altitude one-to-one and monotonically. A ("fails to demonstrate that no species of plant can long survive a violent change in its environment") does nothing to the conclusion when assumed true. B ("overlooks the possibility that locations at different altitudes can have the same climate") assumed true makes the climate-to-altitude inference unreliable, since the same leaf shape would be consistent with multiple altitudes. Directly weakens, so correct.
