# Malaya Jules, 2025-10-17

## LSAT concepts

- **Sufficient assumption**: an argument gave two independent sufficient conditions for one necessary condition, then concluded from the necessary condition failing that one specific sufficient condition failed. The gap: nothing rules out the other sufficient condition. The credited answer supplied "if one sufficient condition doesn't happen, the other doesn't either." Malaya had never seen a sufficient-to-sufficient link before and called it "just the contrapositive."
- **Parallel flaw**: match the *direction* of the part-to-whole error, and match conclusion certainty. Her wrong answer ran part-to-whole backwards (professional athletes to professional skaters) where the stimulus ran member to larger set (poets to artists).
- **Conclusion-scan shortcut for parallel questions**: read only the conclusions of the five answer choices before reading anything else. "If the conclusion in the argument says probably, the answer choice can't say must," and vice versa. "The definitiveness of the conclusion will give you a clear direction as to whether it's even a conceivable option."
- **Weaken/except**: the trap answer widened the gap between control and experimental groups, which weakens rather than fails to weaken.
- **Principle application (Simon says)**: three sufficient conditions must all be satisfied. The stimulus supplied one (more than one overdue book); the answer must supply the missing two, and every qualifier counts. The whole question turned on the word *overdue*.
- **Most strongly supported**: not fully supported by design. "It doesn't have to combine information, it just has to be supported." On the hardest ones there is "no straight line way to derive this answer," and the correct choice is the best available, chosen by elimination.
- **Reading goal for MSS**: read for four wording types: causal, comparative, quantifiable (many, most, some), conditional. "Your overall desire as you're reading is to combine at least two or more claims to derive some other idea."
- **Quantity inference**: when the stimulus is dense with most/many/some, look for a trait overlap. "Some A's are B's, and if all B's are C's, then some A's are C's." If no trait overlap exists, abandon the quantity route and look at the causal claims instead.
- **Causal negation ("no cause, no effect")**: "if doctors listened better there would be fewer lawsuits" does not license a clean difference-maker inference. Plausible but not well supported.
- **Paradox**: find a causal explanation for a discrepancy. "Why did this thing happen although this other thing was true." The resolver either builds on a change already stated, or introduces an outside change.
- **Number vs rate**: a proportional increase in the *number* of infections with population growth does not explain an increase in the *rate*. Malaya identified this as the real defect in the trap answer, above the unwarranted population assumption.
- **Strengthen, priority order for answer choices**: assumption first, then conclusion, then premise. "Strengthen the assumption, strengthen the conclusion, strengthen the premise. That's the order."
- **Negation as a tiebreaker on strengthen questions**: when down to two, negate each. "It might not completely destroy the argument, but one of them will move the needle more." Corollary: a necessary assumption can appear as the credited strengthener.
- **Strengthen is harder than weaken**: "We think more naturally about weakening. Strengthening is harder to see, what would be beneficial."

## Teaching moves

- **Student-led review.** Malaya drove the entire first hour, walking her own wrong answers one at a time with diagnosis already done. Joshua's role was to interrupt for the missed lower-level shortcut, not to re-explain the question.
- **Push down to the cheapest solution.** When she gave a sophisticated set-theory account of the parallel flaw, he stopped her: "You're doing more high level stuff, but there's something even lower level. You want to make your life as easy as possible when it's obviously an option." Then: "Look at the conclusion."
- **Against explanation videos.** The session's strongest correction. "That's the curse. That's the biggest problem with prep programs. Students will watch the explanation and it'll make sense, you'll be like yeah, yeah, I get it. But you didn't actually learn anything." The prescribed order: write out why the wrong answer was attractive, what exactly got you, what action you will take next time, and why the correct answer is correct, and *only then* compare against the explanation. He accepted her account that she had already done this.
- **No answer without a cited line.** "I only let you choose the answer on most strongly supported if you're able to underline exactly which sentence you're using to support it." Rationale: a wrong answer then becomes diagnosable ("I thought these two sentences combined for this information") instead of "it felt right."
- **Gut is fine once trained.** He agreed her instinct is strong and reframed the problem as scope, not intuition: "Your gut will take you pretty far, but it's dangerous if you don't have a context window to refine it to." Hence the reading goal. He explicitly wrote it down to quiz her on next session: "I'm going to ask you this next time, to repeat it back to me."
- **Naming which questions are forgivable.** "There's certain questions you give away if you get them wrong and you're okay with that." On the level-five MSS: "The LSAT writers were like, this is the question that even a 170 scorer should get wrong." He separated that from the principle question and weaken/except, which she said she cannot live with getting wrong.
- **Slow down on the ones that feel loose.** "Most strongly supported are the ones you want to slow down on. Reading, pausing, synthesizing, digesting, then trying to link with whatever you read."
- **Asked for the prediction before the elimination.** On the paradox question he interrupted her walk through the choices: "Prior to that, what was your prediction?" She admitted she had none.
- **Modeled uncertainty and shared history.** On the Byzantine seals question: "This is one of the ones I never forgot, because even after I got it I still didn't feel good about it." He withheld his answer to see her reasoning first: "I know which answer I think is right, but I'm not going to tell you. I want to see how you got there."
- **Let her find the keyword herself.** He kept re-reading the seals stimulus aloud rather than pointing, saying only "there's something there, I hope you caught something there," until she noticed the repeated *when the document was opened*. Then: "Big. That's big."
- **Named the meta-diagnosis of her error pattern**: distrusting a correct gut. She said she chooses "the answer that I don't think is based on vibes" and he let her draw the conclusion that eliminating hard and then trusting the gut is the right protocol for level 4-5 MSS.
- **Reframed her ceiling as rhythm, not content.** "You're in a good spot. It's going to be just getting you into a rhythm. You're not learning novel concepts completely."

## Worked examples

- **Q11, sufficient assumption (level 5, 59% correct).** Two sufficient conditions (use appropriate methods / devote significant effort) for broad mastery; conclusion infers one specific failure from mastery failing. Credited answer links the two sufficient conditions. Malaya knew what was needed ("what happened to my second sufficient condition") but had never seen that structure.
- **Q15, parallel flaw.** Stimulus: amoebas withdraw from damaging stimuli; humans do this because they feel pain; thus all organisms must be capable of feeling pain. Malaya chose C (professional athletes to professional skaters, part-to-whole reversed); credited answer A (poets, people under hypnosis, thus all artists) preserved the member-to-set direction. The available shortcut: only A and E had *must* conclusions. She spent over two minutes on it.
- **Q21, weaken/except.** She chose A first, second-guessed through to E, and switched to B. B enlarges the control/experimental gap, so it weakens. Her own read: "It's really hard to remember what you thought for A when you made it all the way to E."
- **Q10, principle application.** Principle: anyone with more than one overdue book must be fined if some of the *overdue* books are not children's books and the person has previously been fined for overdue books. Application supplies only "three books currently out are overdue." A gives non-children's books plus a prior fine but never says the non-children's books are overdue. B gives "one of the *overdue* books is a novel for adults" plus the prior fine. She missed the word *overdue* while writing her conditions. She called this type "literally just Simon says, checking things off a list."
- **Q23, most strongly supported (level 5).** Stimulus on malpractice: most suits arise out of perceived negligence; many doctors treat medicine as science not art and are less compassionate as a result; doctors sometimes treat patients rudely; lawsuits could be avoided if doctors listened better; economic incentives encourage rudeness. They tagged each statement (most + arise out of, as a result, sometimes, conditional, encourage), found no trait overlap for a quantity inference, and fell back on elimination. B drew an unsupported causal link between two things, which she recognized "is just as hard to support as calling something a main cause." E was weak but the only choice without red flags. She had wanted E and talked herself out of it.
- **Q16, paradox.** Medicine improving, lifespans increasing, overall health improving, yet a steady significant increase in the rate of serious infections. D (population growth yields a directly proportional increase in the number of infections) fails twice: population growth is never stated, and number is not rate. E (modern treatments for many otherwise fatal illnesses increase susceptibility to infection) resolves it by building on a stated change. She had avoided E because *many* "could mean like three"; Joshua noted many can also be nearly all, and that her usual caution about *many* was right in general but misapplied here.
- **PT 127, Section 2, Q24, strengthen (Byzantine lead seals).** Over 40,000 seals remain; apart from rare special-importance cases, most seals served their purpose when the document was opened; lead was cheap but not free, so most seals would have been recast; therefore the number of documents sealed must have been many times the number of remaining seals. Gaps Malaya generated: seals might have had other uses, and documents might never have been opened, so seals were never recast. Credited answer A (most seals were fixed to documents that were then opened during that period) protects the recasting premise. E (fewer than 40,000 seals were fixed to documents at any given time) is the bait; Joshua showed it is directionless because successive non-overlapping sets of under-40,000 could sum to far more. Malaya: "A is kind of a necessary assumption for this argument." Negation test: not "none" but "less than a majority."
