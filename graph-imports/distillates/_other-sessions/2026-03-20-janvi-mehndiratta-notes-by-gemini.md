# Janvi Mehndiratta, 2026-03-20

Role / argument-part questions, built directly on the conclusion work from 2026-03-12.

## LSAT concepts

- **Role questions (argument part)** are "cousins" of main conclusion questions. Same framework for locating the conclusion (first sentence or rebuttal structure); the difference is they point at a specific line and ask what it does.
- **The four buckets.** Every statement in an argument does one of four things: it is the **main conclusion**, it is **support**, it is **opposing**, or it is **neutral**. Joshua's central organizing device for the session.
- **Support** is the broad umbrella covering both premises and sub-conclusions (intermediate conclusions). "If it's an intermediate conclusion or a premise, it falls under support. Don't over complicate it."
- **Sub-conclusion / intermediate conclusion**: has the same two requirements as a main conclusion (opinion, plus explicit support from at least one statement), with the added condition that it in turn supports the main conclusion directly. Test it the same way, with "why should I believe that?"
- **Opposing** comes in two flavors, both of which land in the same bucket: **pure rebuttal** and **concession**. "All a concession is is acknowledging a fact on the other side that they would use."
- **Concessions predict a comeback.** "If they ever concede something, they take one step back, you expect them to come three steps forward. They never just say the other side's point unless they come back to rebut it."
- **Neutral** is background information: it introduces the topic but does nothing for the argument. Illustrated with New York being called the Big Apple as setup for an unrelated claim about its businesses.
- **Attributed views are attacked.** "Whenever they bring up a group of people or things or ideas that's not their own, they're almost always going to be pushing back on it."
- **"Should" always signals opinion**, so it flags a conclusion candidate.
- **A comma splits a sentence into two ideas.** In "but even if it were easy to discover what the past was really like, it is nearly impossible to discover its lessons," the opposing idea and the conclusion sit in one sentence. "If I see a comma, think of it as two separate ideas."
- **"Thus" is a conclusion indicator**, main or sub. "That's a huge flashing sign."
- **Descriptive accuracy is the second gate.** Getting the bucket right is necessary but not sufficient: "the correct answer doesn't say anything that's descriptively wrong of what's happening in the argument." Wrong answers often name the right category but misdescribe the function, or name support for the wrong claim.
- **LSAT answer language is deliberately abstract**: "even though you know what they're talking about, they might put it in a way that's disguising what's being said."

## Teaching moves

- **The four-step reading goal**, stated so process replaces intuition: "You don't want to read the argument just to read the argument and kind of feel out what the vibes are." Step 1, what is the conclusion. Step 2, verify it is the author's opinion and explicitly supported by at least one other claim. Step 3, find the supporting ideas. Step 4, check the question stem to see which part it is asking about.
- **"Why should I believe that?" run as an out-loud test, not a thought.** Joshua's signature device this session. He makes Janvi speak the sequence: state the conclusion, say "why should I believe this," then splice in the candidate premise and judge whether it sounds coherent. When she starts with the candidate premise instead, he stops her and resets the order. "If it doesn't sound right, that means that's not why we should believe it. So that's not the premise."
- **A live falsification test.** He reads "adults have the right to vote, so should adolescents. Why? Admittedly adolescents and adults are not the same." Then: "Would that sound reasonable? No. That cannot be support." Disqualifying a candidate out loud is the demonstration.
- **The dirty-dishes analogy** for the two flavors of opposing. Come home to dirty dishes and you have two options. Option one, pure rebuttal: "I'm tired of your BS, you need to put the dishes away when you're done." Option two, concession: "Listen, babe, I know you work so hard, you're the man of the house, but I need you to put the dishes away." Same function, different delivery. "You're giving the other side their flowers before you give them the hammer." The rebuttal route is "just choosing violence basically."
- **Fixed highlighter color scheme as cognitive offloading**: yellow for the conclusion, pink for premises, orange for intermediate conclusions or opposing views, sometimes underlining for opposing. "Keep the colors identical so you don't ever have to look at it and think about what a highlighted color is. Your brain will naturally associate the color with that argument part."
- **Refusing the indicator-word shortcut.** Asked why a line is an intermediate conclusion, Janvi answers "the indicator word, admittedly." Joshua rejects it: "We don't want to rely on indicator words. How do we figure out whether something's an intermediate conclusion? What's our method?" Structure test over surface cue, every time.
- **Maximum value per question.** He categorizes every line before ever looking at the question stem: "Ultimately all we want to do is identify what they're asking for us. But this is how you practice to get good in every scenario, so you get the maximum value from one question."
- **Answer choices screened by bucket first, description second.** He walks all five choices asking only "which of the four does that sound like?" before evaluating accuracy. "Once you eliminate all the ones that don't fall into the bucket you identified, you'll get rid of most of the answers. Then there'll be one or two you're fighting for, and that's where you ask, is this descriptively accurate?"
- **Repeated forced recall of the four buckets** across the session, deliberately: "I'm going to be asking that quite a bit as we go forward."
- **Sell role questions as the leverage point.** "Role questions for a lot of people are a nightmare because they're very abstract, and if you don't have a good grasp of the argument parts it exposes your hand, because it really focuses on that one skill. But once you get good at these, every other question by proxy you get better at, because you need to do this exact identifying of each statement for every single question type."
- **Progressive release of support**, question by question: question one fully guided, question two mostly guided with the answer choices walked jointly, question three fully independent including committing to an answer. "I'll let you do this one completely on your own. I'm going to be quiet."
- **Praise tied to independence** rather than correctness: "You got it completely on your own too, which is a good sign."
- **Warns against one-session knowledge**: "If you just do it for one session and don't remember it, it's kind of a waste. These are going to follow you for your whole prep. These are the foundation for everything else."

## Worked examples

- **Adolescent voting (role).** "Adults have the right to vote. So should adolescents. Admittedly adolescents and adults are not the same. But to the extent that adolescents and adults are different, adults cannot be expected to represent the interests of adolescents. If adults cannot represent the interests of adolescents, then only by giving adolescents the vote will these interests be represented." Mapping: sentence 1 is the main conclusion (flagged by "should"); sentence 2 is a concession, therefore opposing; sentences 3 and 4 support together, neither sufficient alone. Question asked about "adolescents and adults are not the same." Janvi answered E, correct, which names concession. Joshua ruled out "distracts attention from the point at issue" as opposing but descriptively wrong: "It's not just distracting attention, taking attention away. It's conceding. It's saying, yeah, you got a point here, but you're still wrong."
- **Lessons of history (role).** "People often admonish us to learn the lessons of history. But even if it were easy to discover what the past was really like, it is nearly impossible to discover its lessons. We are supposed, for example, to learn the lessons of World War I. But what are they? And were we ever to discover what they are, it is not clear we could ever apply them, for we shall never again have a situation just like World War I." Mapping: sentence 1 is the opposing view; the clause after the comma in sentence 2 is the main conclusion; the World War I material is premise. Janvi did not commit to an answer during her solo attempt. Working through: she initially tagged sentence 1 as support, ran the out-loud test, heard it fail, and switched to opposing. Answer D, "expresses the position the argument as a whole is directed toward discrediting," correct. A eliminated on bucket (a problem to resolve). B eliminated on bucket (compatible with both accepting and denying, therefore neutral). C is the near-miss: right bucket, wrong description, because "the argument simply takes for granted as false" is false, "he's actually fighting against it, he's trying to disprove it." E eliminated as support.
- **Bureaucracy (role).** Shorter stimulus. "Despite growing satisfaction" opens with a concession acknowledging public unhappiness; "thus" flags the conclusion that bureaucracy is unlikely to be simplified. The question asked about the first sentence, which functions as the premise, the reason bureaucracies will not get simpler. Janvi worked this one entirely alone, picked A ("a premise operating in support of the claim"), correct, and confirmed the claim it supports.
