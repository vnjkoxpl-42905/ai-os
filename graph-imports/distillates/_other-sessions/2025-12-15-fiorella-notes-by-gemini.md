# Fiorella Meyer, 2025-12-15

## LSAT concepts

- **Principle**: every conclusion of the form "X should be the case" needs a grounding criterion. Joshua's core framing for the whole session: "with every claim there needs to be a principle. There needs to be a metric that we measure against."
- **Sufficient assumption**: when "if" appears in the stimulus or question stem, the task is usually to supply the rule. "All they're asking you to do is create a standard. That's all you're doing here. You're creating a criteria, creating a rule of law."
- **Necessary assumption**: the gambling question turned on an unstated overlap between "enforceable" and "effective," a bridge the argument never actually made.
- **Conditional logic, "only if"**: "only if" introduces a requirement. If the necessary condition fails, the sufficient fails. Joshua used this to run answer A backwards to the conclusion.
- **Redundant answer choices**: an answer that restates a premise cannot be right. Fiorella got there herself, and Joshua handed her the credit: "You said it better than I could have. It's already been stated. Doesn't add anything to the situation."
- **Directionality of the answer**: the correct choice must lead toward the specific conclusion drawn. A criterion for when something *is* unethical cannot support a conclusion that something is *not* unethical.
- **Flaw**: named as the highest-frequency LR question type and the next session's focus.

## Teaching moves

- **The two-part structure he repeats all session.** Every argument needs (1) a criterion or "line in the sand," and (2) facts showing the case falls under it. He then teaches the two failure modes symmetrically: "sometimes they'll give you a metric and your goal is to say the specifics of the case meet that metric... Other times they will only give you the specifics but they won't give you any standard."
- **"Line in the sand"** is his recurring phrase for the criterion, used at least six times. Also "golden rule," "broader rule," "metric," "tipping point."
- **Absurd-comparison ladder.** He built the criterion concept from deliberately silly claims rather than LSAT text: "bacon is better than turkey bacon," then "Canada is better than the United States." When she said she'd want evidence, he pushed: "what would the evidence have to contain?" She said statistics; he rejected it as presupposing the standard. Then he supplied a bad argument ("Canada has maple syrup, therefore Canada is better") and asked her to reverse-engineer the implicit criterion. She got it: food. "That's the problem with the argument. I have to make it a criteria: food is determinant of what makes a country better."
- **Legal analogies as the bridge to LSAT abstraction.** He reframed the whole idea in a lawyer's terms: to establish guilt "I have to look at the rule book and say what does the law say you have to meet." Then the DUI worked example: the threshold (0.08) must be fixed *before* you ask what your client blew. "We need to have a threshold first of all."
- **Refuses to accept "why" as the question.** When Fiorella reframed his prompt as "like why are you making that claim," he corrected the level of abstraction: "why is too open-ended. Why sounds like there'll be a million reasons of why, but what's the criteria I'm appealing to in my evidence? Because there's only one statement that always will be the criteria."
- **Forces the recap in her own words.** Line by line through the stimulus: "give me the recap of that line without stating the line in verbatim." He kept asking "what such thing?" until she named the referent (exceptionally successful) rather than gesturing at it.
- **Holds the question open when she is close.** "You're right on the pulse. But right there, there's something even more particular we need." He let her sit and blank before supplying "we need a criteria. We need a metric."
- **Loud confirmation when she lands it.** "Bingo. Bingo. You got it. That's the answer." He reinforces the moment she produces the criterion herself rather than restating it for her.
- **Called the trap in E a double negative.** "This one's sneaky... it's almost like a double negative. This would give us a criteria for establishing whether something is actually bad. We need a criteria that shows us that something is not actually bad."
- **The train metaphor for answer directionality.** "The answer has to take you the path that if you were to jump on this train... it would take you to the end point."
- **Sold the fallacy list as a shortcut, not homework.** "They're the easiest free points on the LSAT... this gives you a cheat code." He set the bar explicitly above familiarity: "you don't only want to be familiar with them. You want to be able to restate them very thoroughly, in and out. Like the back of your hand."
- **Normalized her dislike of flaw questions.** She said "I hate them"; he answered "that's I hear that super often. It's expected, but once you get a hang of them they're actually very enjoyable," and named them his favorite type to teach.

## Worked examples

- **Question 21, gambling (assumption).** Stimulus: laws against gambling are not able to be enforced, and any law that is not effective should not be a law, therefore there should be no legal prohibitions against gambling. Joshua stressed both premises must be treated as "ironclad" and cannot be disputed. The gap: nothing links "not enforceable" to "not effective." A is correct because it supplies the overlap. C fails because it restates that gambling laws are not enforced, adding nothing.
- **Question 25, self-help book publisher (sufficient assumption).** Stimulus: a publisher's promotional material claimed the book showed readers how to become exceptionally successful; everyone knows no book can deliver to the many what by definition must remain limited to the few; thus although the publisher knowingly made a false claim, doing so should not be considered unethical. The needed criterion, as Fiorella stated it: if everyone already knows the claim is not true, it is not unethical. A is correct and runs through "only if": knowingly making a false claim is unethical only if it is reasonable for someone to be deceived; since it is not reasonable here, the claim is not unethical. E fails on direction, it establishes when a false claim *is* unethical.
