# Sonja Wilbert, 2025-12-07

## LSAT concepts

- **Parallel reasoning**: the strategy is abstraction. Strip the specific names and find "the skeleton of the argument." Match conclusion to conclusion and premise to premise, and match the *strength* and *type* of claim, not just the shape.
- **Principle**: any claim that something is or is not justified requires an unstated criterion. Joshua: "we need to have a criteria for everything we make a claim on... we need to have a moral standard to make that claim." The evidence must then show the facts of the case fall under that standard.
- **Conditional logic**: identify conditional indicator words, mark which side of the arrow is necessary, find the repeating middle term, and chain. A then B, B then C yields A then C, and the contrapositive not C then not A.
- **Contrapositive**: the LSAT's favorite disguise in parallel questions. "unless" translates to "if not," and the correct answer often states the chained inference in contrapositive form so it superficially looks like a mismatch.
- **Absolutes vs. hedged conditionals**: "could" kills conditional force. "A conditional is only relevant when it's absolute." If the stimulus says "if X then Y could happen," it is not an ironclad rule and the answer choice need not carry conditional structure.
- **Scope shift**: an answer that compares the subject to a different population (Brooks vs. "many students") when the stimulus compared alternatives within one group.
- **Prediction**: make a specific prediction of the principle before reading answer choices, out loud or written.

## Teaching moves

- **Watch first, teach second.** Joshua opened by having Sonja pull up her Seven Sage account and open a question she got wrong, then: "I want you to think, talk out loud, and work from this question stimulus down. I'm going to just watch you as you go through this and take notes." He had her justify every eliminated choice before he said anything.
- **Concrete parallel as a decoder.** For the principle question he reduced the abstract structure to a one-line street version: "if you break the rules to catch a cheater, you're not allowed to punish them," and later "it's like getting a speeding ticket but the cop was illegally running a red light just to catch you. You can't use a dirty trick to get the evidence." He framed this as a transferable skill: "if we were to use a parallel for the argument... if you can do that, you'll get through these answers super quickly."
- **Plug in variables you connect with.** For the politicians/scholarships question he mapped it onto current US politics (spending cuts, DEI programs, military budget) to make the "smoke and mirrors" structure land. He named the move explicitly and flagged its cost: "I kind of went everywhere there."
- **Named the argument's rhetorical shape, not just its logic.** "This doesn't actually tell us a full story. It's a smoke and mirrors." The correct parallel has to carry that same conversational move, not only formal validity.
- **Nudged toward decomposition, not answers.** Repeated pattern: "What's our conclusion here?" then "Why do they believe that?" then "Do you see a fallacy or a problem with this argument first, before I jump into it?" He let her sit in confusion rather than resolving it early.
- **Self-corrected his own questions when they were imprecise.** "The question I'm asking is very nuanced, so I'm not really asking it properly. Let me make this even more specific." Twice he acknowledged a bad prompt rather than letting her flounder.
- **Prediction as a calibration tool, not a scoring tool.** He argued that comparing a wrong prediction to the right answer teaches you how the LSAT thinks: "even that is a skill of developing to see how you can get your prediction more similar to the answer the LSAT would have presented. You can start to see how they think."
- **Pause while reading, not at the end.** "Just pausing a lot more is useful for these questions to synthesize it, because if you wait till the end it can get really tricky to piece and part everything together."
- **The conditional recipe.** "When you see conditionals with parallel questions, it makes it very friendly to turn it into almost like an algebra soup, but you can make a recipe out of it." Underline conditional indicators, label repeated terms A/B/C, then scan for the argument-indicator word ("so") to find the conclusion. He had her literally annotate on screen with the pen tool.
- **Corrected a hedge in his own vocabulary mid-explanation.** He first called "Phyllis has always bought only inexpensive clothing" conditional, then walked it back: "I probably should have steered away from saying it as conditional because that can be confusing. More so, it's a definitive claim."
- **Redirected a correct-but-shallow elimination into the real reason.** When Sonja rejected D on tone, he sharpened it: the stimulus is about *inefficiency*, a better path ignored; D is a *last resort*, no other path available. "The logic is kind of almost the opposite... The government was acting out of choice. They had better options on the table."

## Worked examples

- **Principle/parallel, the Ellison case.** Stimulus: Ellison admits illegal transactions, but the company obtained the evidence by illegally recording his conversations, therefore it cannot justifiably take punitive measures. Sonja initially picked E, correct answer is D. Required structure: a bad initial act, a tainted discovery, and the conclusion that punishment is unjustified. D (conservation officer trespasses, finds illegal animal traps, cannot justifiably punish) maps all three. E (board pardoned others guilty of similar improprieties) fails because it swaps the taint of the evidence for a fairness/precedent argument: "the original story is about how I caught you is illegal, and E is saying you were just unfair to others in the past."
- **Parallel, politicians and scholarship programs.** Stimulus: the goal of reducing spending does not by itself explain eliminating all government-supported scholarship programs, because spending could have been reduced even more by cutting military spending. Conclusion is the first sentence (the stated reason is not the real reason); support is the second. Sonja picked C, correct answer is E. A fails on an imported "only" (a definitive/necessary claim absent from the stimulus). B fails on scope shift (Brooks vs. many students). C fails twice: it swaps "work styles" for "communication," and it introduces two conflicting parties where the stimulus has one actor choosing between paths. D fails because it is a last-resort argument, the logical opposite of an ignored better alternative. E works: an easier alternative route to fame that was not taken.
- **Parallel with conditionals, winning/cooperation/motivation.** Stimulus: winning requires the willingness to cooperate, which in turn requires motivation, so you will not win if you are not motivated. Diagram: A then B, B then C, inference A then C, conclusion not C then not A. Sonja moved A to E; correct answer is C. A fails because the conclusion imports "wants to be healthy," a desire term absent from the requirement chain: "wanting doesn't necessarily mean you'll follow it." B fails for lack of the repeating middle term. C is correct but disguised: "our party will not retain its status unless it increases its campaigning" translates through "unless" = "if not" back into the contrapositive form. Joshua on the trap: "they usually will do the contrapositive move. It's very common. It's one of their favorite moves in the book."
