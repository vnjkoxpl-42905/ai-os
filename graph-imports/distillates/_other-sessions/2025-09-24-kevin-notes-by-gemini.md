# Kevin Carballo, 2025-09-24

## LSAT concepts

- **Flaw**: prioritized as the highest-value question type. "Flaw questions are the most important question type, because it's the most common type, and it's also the most common type in the most common section, which is LR." Also the most comprehensive, since necessary assumption logic lives inside it.
- **The answer-choice stem taxonomy**, the session's organizing framework. Each stem phrase dictates a different test:
  - **"fails to consider" / "ignores the possibility"**: mute the stem, assume the rest is true, and check that the conclusion gets weaker. A weakening test.
  - **"takes for granted" / "presumes" / "assumes"**: an assumption claim. Two steps, first confirm it benefits the argument, then negate it and confirm the argument collapses.
- **Negation applies only to assumption-type answer choices**, not to every answer choice. This was the session's opening correction of a misapplied rule.
- **Assumption**: something the author treats as already shared with the reader, bridging two points without stating it. Test of reliance: "The only way we know he's depending on that thought is by taking away that thought and seeing if what he says makes no sense."
- **Assumption answers must be weak**: "Any answer choice in a flaw question that says 'assumes,' the answer choice should be very weak, just like necessary assumption, because these are necessary assumption questions in disguise." A word like "never" is disqualifyingly strong.
- **Defining the predicate**: before evaluating any evaluative claim, extract the criteria that make it true. "Useful purpose can be subjective. We need to turn it into objective."
- **Conditional logic inside a flaw question**: "must" introduces a necessary condition, and proving a negative conclusion requires the contrapositive.
- **False dichotomy / false binary**: reasoning from two extreme groups while ignoring everyone between them.
- **Descriptive accuracy**: every word of a flaw answer choice must map onto the argument. "Does the noun exist in the argument? If it's an adjective, does it accurately describe? If it's an adverb, is the author doing that? If there's one word off, we do not accept it."
- **Answer-choice vocabulary as argument-part pointers**: "infer" always introduces the conclusion, "on the basis of" always introduces the evidence, and both halves must map correctly.
- **"Causal factor" and "a factor" mean causation**, not correlation.
- **Statistical overlap**: percentages from non-mutually-exclusive groups cannot be summed.
- **Untrue vs proven false**: lack of evidence is not evidence of lacking.
- **The five high-frequency correct flaw types**: assumptions that are not true, correlation for causation, sufficient for necessary, untrue vs proven false, and part/whole. The low-frequency ones (ad hominem, circular reasoning) are worth knowing only so they can be dismissed on sight.
- **The LSAT's standing posture toward background facts**: "Even if we accept your background facts, you still haven't proven your conclusion." Hence objections about a figure's source or an unmeasured variable are never flaws.

## Teaching moves

- **Owning the earlier miscommunication immediately.** Kevin had been negating every answer choice. Joshua: "Oh my gosh, if I didn't tell you that, I should have made that explicitly clear. My apologies." Then reframed it as a win: "Is it good that we caught that?"
- **A never-concede rule for flaw answers, taught through a courtroom scene.** "If we're looking for a flaw, we never want to concede the other side is right. Never ever. If I go to court on trial for my life, I never want you to say, your honor, my defendant, he did it. That's the worst thing you could ever say." Applied to eliminate the answer Kevin had chosen, which granted that a law might not serve a useful purpose. He flagged it as a recurring trap: "That's a very popular trick. It will say they're right, but they'll give a nuance. If it says they're right, we do not accept it."
- **Refusing an almost-right formulation.** Kevin said the answer needed "something that serves no purpose." Joshua pushed back twice, holding him at the definitional step: "I'm going to hold you there. We need something to define the predicate more. We can't know whether something's useful if we don't know what makes something fall into that category."
- **Context-dependent definitions.** "If I told you Kevin's very rich, how do I know if you're rich? If you go to Africa you're going to be rich, but somewhere else you might find yourself not rich."
- **Extending the definitional point beyond the test.** "Almost all arguments, the disagreements come down to definitions." Ran it through pro-life and pro-choice, where each side argues against its own caricature of the other's label rather than the definition. "If you don't have definitions clearly stated, you can't have a conversation that's fruitful."
- **Building the false binary from the student's own world.** Guantanamo Bay detainees on one end, the priest at Kevin's local church on the other, then: "For that reason, we shouldn't have any laws. What's the problem with my argument?" Kevin got there: "It's not representative of the population as a whole. It's a dichotomy." Joshua: "Exactly the answer I was looking for. But how about everybody in between?"
- **Explaining why weak answers still win.** Kevin's own formulation, endorsed: "It doesn't really have to weaken it 100%, just as long as it's a little flick, you found the crack in the argument."
- **Word-by-word answer mapping demonstrated live.** On "it confuses X with Y," he made Kevin locate each element: does a test exist in the argument, does that test incorrectly identify samples, is X the evidence and Y the conclusion. Formalized as a rule: "Whenever we see 'confuses something with something,' X refers to what the author is talking about in the evidence, and Y refers to how the author is thinking in the conclusion."
- **Making the student negate the answer aloud** rather than describing negation. Kevin produced "the use of physical evidence is sometimes mistaken," then Joshua asked whether one mistaken instance destroys the argument. It does not, so the answer fails. He added the standard: a properly negated necessary assumption should be near-oxymoronic against the conclusion, making it impossible rather than merely weaker.
- **Explaining why weak answers are provable**, in support-weight terms: "The answer choice needs to be lighter in weight because it'll need less to support it. So it's more likely to be supported."
- **The NBA/NFL analogy for statistical overlap**, the session's cleanest instrument. "50 percent of Americans follow the NBA, 50 percent follow the NFL, therefore every American is either an NBA or NFL fan. They're adding 50 to 50 to make 100. That's not how it works." Kevin's recognition was immediate and repeated: "He's combining it. I see it. I see it."
- **Constructing the counterexample numerically.** "If 27 out of 100 accidents had both rulebreaking and bad equipment as causal factors, that would show bikers partially responsible in 27 percent of cases, not over 50."
- **Warning about attractive-sounding stock answers.** On why the correlation-to-causation answer had to be rejected here: "B is a very attractive answer because it's a very commonly sounding answer that's correct. But it has to map onto the argument." And: "Usually it will be what you're saying. Here they're giving us two causal claims. It's an issue of reading the stats properly."
- **Pruning the fallacy list rather than expanding it.** On The Loophole's 10 to 15 flaws: "You're not going to go wrong with The Loophole, but there's a bit too many. You need to know the ones that are commonly the right answer, like five of them." Distinguished flaws worth knowing to select from flaws worth knowing only to dismiss.
- **Explicit critique of the standard resources.** Kevin: "The Loophole, 7Sage, they just give you a very black and white answer, but there's no really thought behind it." Joshua: "It's like factory style. They just give you, oh, you do this, but there's a lot of nuances, and that's the thing that makes the difference."
- **Study-method prescription: question, show answer, review instantly.** "Don't wait until the end. You don't want to deal with 25 questions of review. Instantly review it and figure out what the problem was." Plus journaling errors.
- **Catching a hard deadline the student had missed.** Mid-conversation about the color blindness accommodation letter, Joshua realized the registration deadline was the next day and told Kevin to check. It was correct. "Force it. Whatever you have to do."

## Worked examples

- **Mayor and the crossing-against-red-light law** (flaw). Conclusion: the law serves no useful purpose. Stipulated rule: to serve a useful purpose, a law must deter the behavior it prohibits, so proving the conclusion requires the contrapositive. Evidence: pedestrians who invariably violate the law are not dissuaded by it, and those who comply do not need it. Flaw: a false binary between two extreme groups, ignoring everyone in between who might in fact be deterred. Kevin chose C, which concedes that a law might not serve a useful purpose, rejected under the never-concede rule. D correct, a "fails to consider" answer identifying deterred middle-group pedestrians.
- **DNA exoneration** (flaw). Defense attorneys use DNA comparison to establish absence from a crime scene. DNA tests often fail to distinguish among samples from distinct individuals. Conclusion: it is a mistake to exonerate a suspect merely because their DNA does not match crime scene samples. Correct answer B, it confuses a test that incorrectly identifies samples from different people as coming from the same person (a false positive) with a test that incorrectly shows samples from the same person as coming from different people (a false negative). The stimulus establishes only the first, while the conclusion requires the second. A rejected: "assumes without warrant that the use of physical evidence is never mistaken" fails the negation test, since one mistaken instance leaves the conclusion intact, and "never" is far too strong for an assumption answer.
- **Bicycle traffic accidents** (flaw). Premise 1: failure of bicyclists to obey traffic regulations is a causal factor in more than a quarter of bicycle accidents. Premise 2: inadequate safety equipment is also a factor in more than a quarter. Conclusion: bicyclists are at least partially responsible for more than half of bicycle accidents. Flaw: summing two overlapping percentages as if the groups were mutually exclusive. Kevin first chose C (correct), then switched to B. B rejected on structural mapping: it says the argument "improperly infers the presence of a causal connection on the basis of" a correlation, but the evidence contains two causal claims, not a correlation, so the "on the basis of" half fails to map. A rejected: the author need not presume motorists are a factor in less than half, since "at least partially responsible" leaves room for multiple responsible parties. C correct, fails to consider that more than one factor may contribute to a given accident. D rejected, failing to provide the source of a cited figure is never an LSAT flaw. E rejected, injury severity is outside the argument's claim and would not weaken it.
