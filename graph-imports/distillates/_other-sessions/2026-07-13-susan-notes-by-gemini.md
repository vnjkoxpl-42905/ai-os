# Susan Lennon, 2026-07-13

## LSAT concepts

- **Main conclusion**: defined technically as "the specific claim that the author is supporting with at least one other statement," not a summary or general idea. Joshua pushed back on the vague definition Susan gave and rebuilt it from the ground up as a two-step test: it is the author's opinion, AND it receives explicit support from at least one other statement.
- **Fact vs opinion**: a statement presented as a fact can never be a conclusion, "because a fact is describing the world as it is. So there's no debating the world as it is." Empirical facts must be measurable and verifiable in a tangible way. Joshua called this "so powerful" and "surprising how many people do not learn this specific thing."
- **Intermediate conclusion**: a conclusion that supports another conclusion. Susan supplied this definition correctly and it became the key to the question.
- **Referential phrasing**: the LSAT hides structure in small reference words ("this," "they," "their"). The method is to find the closest preceding noun and substitute it in place of the reference word "to keep the thoughts in order." Susan noted she never treats "they" as a reference word worth tracking.
- **Opinion indicator words**: "evidently" flagged as an opinion marker via its root, evidence. "Off the evidence we can believe this to be true" is inherently a claim, "almost always going to be an opinion style statement."
- **Motivations and beliefs are never facts**: you cannot put someone's incentives "under a microscope or be able to weigh it," unlike "a cup with a liter of water."
- **Strengthen, weaken, flaw**: Susan's three self-identified weakest question types, driven by over-evaluating every answer choice.
- **Conditional reasoning**: Susan is comfortable here from her SevenSage foundation and rarely misses these.
- **Reading comprehension**: the same prediction habit is "the magic to be able to do RC."

## Teaching moves

- **The hypothesis validation framework**, Joshua's named method for active reading. He first attacked the cliche: "everyone says actively read, but like when someone says actively read, a lot of these cliche terms are very difficult because it's hard to operationalize them. How do you do that? Do I just look at the screen really hard?" Then converted it to action: form a hypothesis about why the author took the time to write the stimulus, ideally within the first few words, then validate it against each new piece of information. "Kind of like a science experiment." He stated the general principle: "turning everything to action is very useful to be getting better at this test."
- **Ran the framework live on the first word.** He asked what the first word was ("Normally"), asked what normally implies in ordinary conversation, then asked what that sets up. Susan produced "an example of someone doing something differently" herself. "Bingo. So we expect them to hit us with like a plot twist."
- **Reframed being wrong as the goal**: "that's amazing because if you get it wrong, that means you know what's right... So it's like you hope to get it wrong just so you can make sure that you can get the right answer." Also stressed the hypothesis is updatable, not a commitment.
- **Prephrasing correction, a distinctive framing**: "I don't want you to predict what the answer is going to be. I want you to predict in the passage what the passage is going to be talking about." This directly answered Susan's complaint that the test weaponizes her predictions into trap answers. Predicting the passage "forces you to engage with it. You're asking questions of the passage. Why are you telling me this?"
- **The "why should I believe this?" test** for isolating support structure. He did not explain it, he made Susan perform it out loud: read a claim, say "why should I believe this," read the candidate support, and listen for coherence. She heard the incoherence herself ("So that doesn't really answer"), then tested every claim in sequence until the structure fell out and she self-corrected from B to C.
- **Made her read the stimulus aloud and interrupted deliberately**, warning her first: "as you're reading out loud, I'm going to interject a lot. So forgive me in advance if I cut you off."
- **Observed before teaching.** He had her solve the question cold first, camera off, "pretend like I'm a fly on the wall," and explicitly de-risked it: "it's a process. I don't really care about the yes or no."
- **Asked for her own-words distillation before correcting**: "in your own words in the most natural way possible. Distill it down." He praised the answer against a baseline: "usually when I ask students what does the stimulus say, they often have that deer in the headlights and they talk something about politics."
- **Chose the question as a diagnostic trap.** He predicted her wrong answer in advance: "I told this question intentionally because I knew that with the demon approach... I was almost certain that B would be the one he chose because when they see the word 'but' usually people think that's going to introduce a conclusion." The lesson: "requires you to not just reflexively look for keywords... you'll get away with it but you'll get punished for it."
- **The athlete analogy** for his teaching philosophy: "if you could choose between being an athletic athlete or being a skilled athlete, which would you prefer to be? I would prefer to be both... it's a false dialectic." SevenSage is too rigid ("they want you to be like an AI, like a language model"), LSAT Demon has "no firm guard rails." The target is a systematic approach with intuition instilled into it.
- **The over-trained student anecdote**: a SevenSage student asked "if you're in New York you're in the US, right? So if you're not in the US, what do you know?" pulled out paper to diagram it. "If you just took away all this LSAT brain, before training you would have known instantly."
- **The bike-riding frame for granularity**: "when you ride a bike you're not thinking left foot, right foot. It just becomes muscle memory. But first it has to be conscious." Used to answer Susan's worry that this depth of analysis is unsustainable under time.
- **"Unknown unknowns" as the core value of tutoring**: "there's things that you don't know that you don't know... it's not even something you've identified as an issue." Backed by his own numbers: 16 months of study that "maybe would have been probably seven months."
- **Wrong answer journal, reframed as debate, not record.** He agreed the Demon position is wrong ("their approach is terrible... I would say you need a journal") but corrected her method: write it out BEFORE reading the explanation. "The journal is useful for you to flush out your thinking on paper. It's not the actual journal to look back later... You should debate with yourself." The move: "I looked at the wrong answer. I said, you're the right answer... and I looked at the right answer and I said, you're wrong. So something fundamentally I missed." The failure mode without it: "you just go through questions like ah I get it, I knew it, I was going to choose that one anyways."
- **Against volume**: "volume is the enemy of quality... you want to do three questions rather than doing 50 questions if you do them thoroughly."
- **Named the flaw in platform explanations** in a way Susan had felt but not articulated: they are "retroactively ad hoc, you're looking retroactively and saying this makes sense, but you would never see that without anybody telling you." His diagnostic question: "how do I do that next time when you're not here? How would I be able to replicate that process?" He was careful to praise the platform anyway: "Demon is a good platform and I would never say that's not, because I loved it in many ways."
- **Refused to overpromise on schedule**, twice: "I can't give you a one-size-fits-all approach... I'd be dishonest to you if I told you yeah, you need to do this thing four days a week. That would be just me being irresponsible." Flagged it as a personal principle: "I hate overpromising. That's my pet peeve."
- **Practice test position staked out against Demon dogma**: endurance is a trainable muscle. "You can do one section perfectly, but can you do three sections? Can you get to question 80 and have the same sharpness as you were when you're question three?... If you don't train that muscle, you shouldn't be surprised when your scores don't reflect it." Also warned the opposite extreme exists: "usually the other problem is people take too many practice tests."
- **Closed by matching approach to background**: an English major with high reading retention needs a different operationalization than "an engineer... they might have a different skill set. So these approaches don't account for that."

## Worked examples

One logical reasoning question, main conclusion type (question number not stated). Stimulus, roughly: "Normally, political candidates send out campaign material in order to influence popular opinion. But the recent ads for Epson's campaign were sent to too few households to serve this purpose effectively. The ads were evidently sent out to test their potential to influence popular opinion. They covered a wide variety of topics, and Epson's campaign has been spending heavily on follow-up to gauge their effect on recipients."

Susan initially chose B, the "but" sentence. Reasoning path taken:

1. Sentence 1 ("Normally...") triggers a hypothesis of an incoming contrast or plot twist. Distilled in plain words as "politicians doing what politicians do."
2. Sentence 2: resolve "this purpose" back to "influence popular opinion." Susan called it an opinion because of "too few" and "effectively." Joshua corrected: there is a specific number of households required and the campaign did not meet it, so it is measurable and therefore an empirical fact. "The softer language makes it sound like it's an opinion... It's technically a fact. Technically it's very sneaky." Facts cannot be conclusions, so B is eliminated on structure alone.
3. Sentence 3 ("evidently sent out to test their potential"): Susan first called it a fact. Two reasons it is an opinion, the second stronger: motivations and internal beliefs are unknowable and unmeasurable, and "evidently" has evidence as its root, so it signals inference from evidence.
4. Contrast surfaced by asking what Epson does differently from the normal case: normal candidates send material to influence, Epson sent it to test potential to influence. "He has different ulterior motivations."
5. Sentence 4: resolve "they" to the closest preceding noun, the ads / campaign materials. Wide topic variety plus heavy follow-up spending both fit the testing hypothesis.
6. Apply "why should I believe this?" to each claim in turn. Sentence 2 followed by sentence 3 is incoherent. Sentence 1 has no support. Sentence 3 followed by sentence 4 is coherent, so sentence 4 supports sentence 3, making sentence 3 a conclusion. Susan then saw the answer was C.
