# Yahunatan Zetvy, 2026-07-15

First working session. Full foundations lecture on argument structure, then one main conclusion question worked end to end.

## LSAT concepts
- **Argument (LSAT definition)** — strictly premises plus a conclusion and the relationship between them. "It's not about the amount of people speaking. It's not about disagreement." If either premise or conclusion is absent, there is no argument.
- **Main conclusion** — the author's single specific claim being proved, not a summary. "It could be a sentence, it could be half a sentence, it could be three words." Two-step test: (1) it must be the author's opinion, never a fact; (2) it must receive explicit support from at least one other statement.
- **Premise** — a claim actively supporting the conclusion. Facts can only ever be evidence: "We're not here to debate the truth of the premises ever."
- **Sub-conclusion / intermediate conclusion** — dual role, functions as a premise for the main conclusion and as a conclusion for its own support. Test: ask which claim makes the other more believable.
- **Opposing viewpoint** — a belief attributed to a group (scientists, economists, critics, philosophers) that the author will rebut. "Whenever they bring up any group of people, always remember they're going to bring it up just to destroy it."
- **Concession** — the author grants the other side a point before correcting it. Still counts as opposing, not neutral. "It is neutral in a way because it's middle ground, but it's building for the opposing side."
- **Background information** — context, relevance, definitions. Neutral. Distinct from premises, which must actively support.
- **Pivot words** — "however," "yet," "but." Whatever follows is almost always the conclusion. Often condensed to just "they are wrong."
- **Structure / skeleton** — named as the first of five levers of the LSAT and "might be the most important one."
- **Equivocation** — words that mean one thing colloquially and something technical on the LSAT. Named explicitly as a recurring theme students should expect from him.

## Teaching moves
- **Opens by declaring the priority: foundations over tricks.** "It's not the cherry on the cake, it's the foundational pieces... whatever you do with regards to foundations will pay you back forward in the future." And the standard: "It's not enough to just be loosely familiar. It's about having a mastery."
- **Asks before telling.** He opened with "What is an argument?" and let Yahunatan give the colloquial answer (two people supporting their points) before correcting it. Same pattern for conclusion, sub-conclusion, concession, background. He validates first: "You're correct with what you said," then narrows.
- **Insists on operationalizing every instruction.** "For me to tell you to find the conclusion is useful, yeah, but how do I operationalize that? What is the actual pen-to-paper action I take? That's very important for the LSAT. Avoiding the ambiguities, getting into the specificity."
- **The golden question: "Why should I believe this?"** Applied to any candidate conclusion. If the stimulus answers it, good or bad, you have a conclusion. If nothing answers it, "it's just useless, just saying something for fun."
- **Deliberately provocative example content** to make it stick: built the demo argument around "Donald Trump is a deceiver" supported by "he said no new wars and started new ones," then added "presidents are all fake" as a competing conclusion to teach the which-makes-the-other-more-believable test. "I like these very dicey things. Usually people remember it better."
- **The layover analogy for sub-conclusions:** sometimes the author goes straight from premise to conclusion, "but other times he takes a pit stop... a connector. Some people will get off the connecting flight and go to their vacation, but some people will take this to leapfrog to the final point."
- **The superstar / supporting cast analogy:** sub-conclusions are "conclusions that are not the main superstar of the show but they're like supporting cast."
- **The dishes-in-the-sink analogy for concession.** A wife can "start World War Three" over dishes, or she can say "Listen, babe, I know you work so hard, you're the man of the house, you take care of us" and then "but listen, babe, you need to remember to put the dishes away." Same disagreement, politically correct delivery. Point: "We don't want to fall into thinking that means the author agrees."
- **Four buckets as the whole taxonomy:** main conclusion, support, opposing, neutral. Every single line of a stimulus goes in one. Then the nuance layer (premise vs intermediate, concession vs opposing conclusion) is optional detail after the bucket call.
- **The conclusion as north star.** "Nothing is inherently neutral, support, opposing. It's only that in relation to the conclusion... the orbit around the conclusion is what we're trying to understand."
- **The structural shortcut for main conclusion and role questions:** the conclusion will be either in the first sentence, or the stimulus is rebuttal style (someone else's view first, then the author's rebuttal). "It's one or the other. Not both." Reasoning: "Most people read an argument expecting the last sentence to be the conclusion, so they do the opposite." He calls it "a life hack... not a guarantee, but 90% chance," and immediately guards it: "this doesn't replace the ability to dissect the argument, but it makes it a lot more effortless."
- **Recall and rephrase.** Read a claim, look away, restate it in natural language "as though you're talking to a normal person, a person that doesn't know the LSAT." He removed the stimulus from the screen to force it. Purpose: "It forces you to see what you actually have stored in your memory when you're reading questions, because when you're reading the answers, you have an idea of the argument." He made the student do this line by line, then chain lines one plus two, then the whole stimulus.
- **Corrects a bad rephrase by going back to the text, not by supplying the answer.** When Yahunatan's first attempt drifted ("it doesn't mean one weighs more than the other"), Joshua said "Good try. Let's look at it again because we want to make sure it's accurate to what's being said," and had him reread.
- **Word match vs meaning match.** "We're not looking for repetition of the words being said in the first line. We're looking for a meaning match. The word-match trick is what the LSAT will use to trick you." Also: "The meaning of 'necessarily' is more important than the word 'necessarily'."
- **The LSAT writes badly on purpose.** "They write very poorly on purpose. Very intentionally, they write badly... The content is not necessarily that difficult, but the language is written in a deceitful way." Hence: "This is an easy test. The reason why it's tricky is not because of the content, it's because we haven't been able to absorb it, internalize it, and distill it into a way that makes coherent sense."
- **Praises the specific act, not the student.** When Yahunatan turned "unscrupulous canning companies" into "there's no regulations stopping them," Joshua stopped the session to mark it: "You made it so it's real to yourself... You turned it into Yahunatan's language, not the language of the LSAT."
- **Names a shortcut and forbids it in the same breath:** "I'll be furious if you ever did this, but theoretically, because we know the first sentence is likely the conclusion, we could almost get away with just reading this first line and going to the answers." Then: "But we do want to read the rest to check if we're right."
- **Connects to the student's own domain.** Yahunatan is a front-end graphic designer who codes; Joshua: "The understanding of coding is very similar to how you understand arguments." Later Yahunatan produced the frame himself, "be like the stakeholders, I just want to know why this happened," and Joshua ratified it: a lawyer's job is not debugging the small things, it is the overseeing view.
- **Flags his own format.** "Today's going to be in kind of ranty, lectury style. Forgive me in advance... but usually it won't be as much of me just yapping."
- **Answers the access question honestly.** Asked whether tutoring is where this knowledge lives, Joshua: "This is the reason why I went into LSAT tutoring... you'll never just wake up in the morning, be watching the news and know this. This is the difference between the haves and the have-nots." He credits his own tutor for opening "a whole world that existed that I didn't even know existed."

## Worked examples
- **Main conclusion question, canned fruit and vegetables stimulus.** Stimulus: (1) even if one can of fruit or vegetables weighs more than another, the heavier can does not necessarily contain more food; (2) canned goods are typically packed in water, which can make up more than half the total weight; (3) nothing stops unscrupulous canning companies from including more water per can than others.
  - Yahunatan solved it cold first, narrowed to A and E, and chose A. Correct.
  - Rebuild path: identify question type → check for another group or viewpoint in sentence one → none present, so it is not rebuttal style, so the conclusion is sentence one → recall and rephrase each line → chain the lines → predict what the answer must say → check answers against the prediction.
  - Rephrases produced: sentence one, "just because one can weighs more doesn't tell us which one has more food"; sentence two, "they're usually stored in water, and the water can make up most of the weight"; sentence three, "there's no regulations stopping them from including more water."
  - (A) correct, a meaning match for the conclusion. (E) is the trap: attractive because it imports the water content from sentence two, which is a premise, not the conclusion. (C) is the last sentence, attractive to students who assume conclusions come last. Joshua's diagnosis of the trap: "They're testing us to see if we think the conclusion means summary of the argument."
  - Yahunatan wobbled toward C after the word-match warning; Joshua walked him back to his own prediction before touching answers.
