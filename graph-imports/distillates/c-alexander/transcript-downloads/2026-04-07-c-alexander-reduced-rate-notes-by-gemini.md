# C. Alexander, 2026-04-07

## LSAT concepts

- **Argument**: strictly premise plus conclusion. "On the LSAT it strictly just means premise and conclusion." Colloquial senses (winning, disagreeing, proving a point) are set aside as a technical divorce from ordinary English.
- **Premise**: "all it really is is it means evidence. Every other explanation is just like to sound smart." A statement actively supporting another statement.
- **Conclusion**: the statement receiving support. Explicitly not a summary. "It's the claim." Could be a line, a few words, sometimes half a sentence.
- **Two-step conclusion test**: (1) must be the author's opinion, never a statement of fact, (2) must receive explicit support from at least one other statement.
- **Fact vs opinion**: a fact is "describing the world as it is," descriptive. Key asymmetry stated twice for emphasis: "the evidence doesn't have to be a fact, but a fact can only ever be used as evidence" (or background information). Facts can never be the conclusion.
- **Main conclusion = main point**: the same thing. "The only purpose for that stimulus to be presented, the only reason why they wrote any sentence, any word, any letter, is to deliver the main point."
- **Not every conclusion is the main conclusion**: an opposing viewpoint can carry its own conclusion and its own evidence. That is their argument, not the author's.
- **Premise indicator words**: because, since, after all, for. Taught with the mnemonic "BABS" (because, after all, but/since variants), attributed to a former student. A premise indicator guarantees a premise follows it, and by definition also flags a conclusion, since it must be attached to one. "It's like almost indirectly a conclusion indicator word too."
- **Conclusion indicator words**: therefore, so, hence. Looser because there are multiple types of conclusions.
- **Background / peripheral information**: neutral context, definitions, why the topic is relevant. Does not move the argument.
- **Opposing viewpoint**: "any opinion or argument or belief that's outside of the author's." Always introduced only to be rebutted.
- **Concession**: acknowledging the other side before pushing back. A softer route to the same rebuttal.
- **Referential language**: words like "this," "they," "that" compact a whole earlier idea. Substitute the referent back in to keep the argument clear.
- **Pre-phrasing**: articulate the answer before reading the choices. "If you don't, that means you missed something. We don't want to find the answers in the answers."

## Teaching moves

- **Foundations-first argument for the session's agenda.** Opened by justifying why they are doing definitions at all: "people like to focus on the details at the end which they think has the most returns, but the biggest returns you'll get on your investment with time is understanding the foundations at not just a superficial level but at a very profound level."
- **Ask before telling, every time.** Each concept opened as a question: "when I say the word argument, what comes to mind?" "when I say premise and conclusion, what do you think I mean?" "when I say concession, what do you think that means?" He validates the intuition, then narrows it to the technical definition.
- **The wedding cake analogy for conclusion and support.** "If I were to hand you a wedding cake and you did not extend your arms, what would happen to that cake?" The cake is the conclusion, fragile and unable to stand alone; it must sit on a table. "There's no such thing as a conclusion without a statement to support it, because by definition a conclusion is a statement that receives support."
- **The pyramid / iceberg image.** "Think of it as the top of the pyramid. That's the tip of the iceberg. The things that are holding up that conclusion is the evidence."
- **North star framing.** The main conclusion is "the north star, the center point of everything."
- **Deliberately absurd examples to make definitions stick.** Used "Donald Trump is the greatest president you have ever had" because "he has beautiful hair" to show an argument can be terrible and still be an argument: "it doesn't have to be like really reasonable, but it at least has to attempt to support it." He read C's politics and leaned into the provocation openly ("I'm going to push your nerves today"), then switched the example to "Canada is better than the US at hockey" when it got uncomfortable.
- **"Why should I believe this?" as the manual conclusion check.** Ask it after every statement. Then run the reversal test: put the two candidate statements in each order and see which reads coherently. "The statement that follows 'why should I believe this' is the evidence, and the statement prior is the conclusion. This is how you do a manual check."
- **Logical hostility.** "A lot of people are very, I don't want to say submissive, but very agreeable. You want to develop a hostile mentality... come in knowing that they're trying to sneak something across, and they're not coming in good faith." Extended into the lawyer metaphor: "I don't want a nice lawyer, right? I want a really mean lawyer... there's something almost sociopathic about that component." The reading posture: "I'm not granting you anything more than I have to, I'm trying to extract everything I can from you."
- **The archaeologist metaphor for background information.** "You're looking to find the bones, the fossils. You want to dust off the fossils to be able to see, this is a T-Rex leg. You want to get past the fluff to get to the money."
- **The author as narcissist.** For opposing viewpoints: "Think of the author as a narcissist. The only context that brings up other people is to show why they're dumb. That's the only reason." Also "a sacrificial lamb, only there to juxtapose with what he believes and show why they're wrong." Practical payoff: any time a group is named (some biologists, some economists), the author will contradict them.
- **A domestic analogy for concession vs direct attack.** Partner comes home to dishes left on the counter. Direct route: "don't leave those dishes on the counter." Concession route: praise him first, then make the demand. "You're essentially taking two steps back to go three steps forward."
- **Cold solo attempt with think-aloud, then targeted feedback.** He gave the question with no scaffolding, went silent, and asked her to verbalize continuously: "I'm observing you, so it helps me understand how you think." He explicitly de-stakes the outcome: "I truly don't care if you get the question right or wrong." She replied "I care."
- **Redirects a conceptual answer into a textual one.** When C paraphrased the conclusion correctly but could not point to it, he accepted the understanding and then insisted on the mechanic: "you understand it conceptually, which is good, but the better practice is: which claim is the conclusion if I were to highlight it?"
- **Validates the "secret code" complaint honestly.** When C asked how anyone is supposed to figure this out, he agreed rather than mystifying: "it's not an intelligence matter. If you never been told this, how are you going to know this? There's no way you wake up in the morning and just come up with it." Then positioned his own value against the market: "a lot of the other content you're going to get is just fluff. Cut out all the fluff."
- **Homework framed as memorization, not practice.** Indicator word lists to be known "like the back of your hand, like literally tattoo them on if you can."

## Worked examples

- **Critic Lindsay main conclusion question.** Stimulus: "Lindsay has been judged to be a bad songwriter simply because her lyrics typically are disjointed and subjective. This judgment is ill-founded, however, since the writings of many modern novelists typically are disjointed and subjective, and yet these novelists are widely held to be good writers."
  - C answered B cold and correctly, reasoning that the conclusion must be the author's opinion and ruling out A because the critic is not claiming Lindsay is good.
  - Rebuild: "because" flags the first clause as a premise, so what precedes it ("Lindsay has been judged to be a bad songwriter") is a conclusion, but somebody else's conclusion, not the author's.
  - "This judgment" resolved to the judgment that she is a bad songwriter, then substituted back in.
  - Attribution check: who says it is ill-founded? The critic, that is, the author. So it is opinion, and it passes step one.
  - Support check: the novelist comparison supports it, so step two passes. Conclusion confirmed as "this judgment is ill-founded."
  - C's paraphrase, "she's a bad writer, but so are a lot of writers that are considered really good novelists," was corrected: the author is not conceding she is bad, he is charging a double standard. "He's not saying that she's a bad writer, he's saying that everybody else does it, and we like them. He's a double standard."
  - Joshua rated the question "beginner if you know what you're doing," but not beginner for someone new to the test.
