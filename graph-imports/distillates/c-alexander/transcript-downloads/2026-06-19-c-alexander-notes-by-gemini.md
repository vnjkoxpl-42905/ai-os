# C. Alexander, 2026-06-19

## LSAT concepts

- **Assumption**: "a premise that is not stated. The author is thinking this point but they didn't say it out loud."
- **Necessary assumption**: what the argument requires. "An assumption that is required for the conclusion to even be able to have a chance to be right. It's not that this would prove the conclusion to be true, it just makes it so it's even possible." Correct answers are weakly worded and conservative. Two equivalent tests: (1) negate it and the argument collapses, (2) which answer must the author agree with.
- **Sufficient assumption**: what the argument desires. "An assumption that's enough to guarantee the conclusion on its own." Correct answers are strong; overkill is fine.
- **Validity**: if the evidence is true, the conclusion is guaranteed true. Not likely, not plausible. "Every invalid argument has assumptions baked into it. Every valid argument has zero assumptions."
- **The conclusion should be a restatement of the evidence**: "one huge hack is the conclusion should essentially just be a restatement of the evidence. That's really the only way to have a valid argument. Anything you bring in new in the conclusion" is a gap.
- **Bait and switch / equivocation**: the stimulus talks about technology in the evidence and concludes about inventions, or premises about horses and concludes about friendly animals. "They're trying to swap these ideas without you noticing and seeing if you'll catch it."
- **Descriptive vs prescriptive**: a "should" or "ought" claim is about how the world ought to be and says nothing about how it currently is. "A should claim has to be substantiated with a should premise."
- **Direction-change indicators**: but, yet, however. "Everything I said prior to the word 'but' is probably going to be either destroyed or at least nuanced." These usually introduce an opinion.
- **"Should" flags opinion**: "should is always going to indicate an opinion... saying should means you're assuming a universal goal of some sort."
- **Answer-choice strength language**: "most" is near-fatal on a necessary assumption question. "If it's 'the most,' it will almost certainly never be the correct answer for the required." "Sometimes" is the ideal weak-answer word.
- **Objections / loopholes**: the universal method across every question type. "Is there any conceivable world where the evidence is true but the conclusion could still be possibly wrong?"

## Teaching moves

- **Starts with question-type identification, not content.** "My first initial question was the question type. What is the question actually asking you to find? What's the goal of this question?" Only after that does he touch the stimulus.
- **Probes vague answers instead of accepting them.** C said an assumption is "a blanket statement." He flagged it: "what you said sounds right, but when you say a blanket statement, elaborate on that idea." Repeatedly does this: the answer is directionally right, so he narrows rather than corrects.
- **Reframes her self-diagnosed problem.** C said she rushes to avoid "pontificating." He rejected the speed framing entirely: "it's not about reading too fast or too slow. It's just giving yourself the guardrails to not allow yourself to get out of pocket." The guardrail is restatement.
- **Restate-as-story, then visualize.** "If you're reading the argument as they present it, it becomes passive. You have to force yourself to explain it to one of your friends, like you're telling a story. Then you're forcing yourself to actually visualize it. Once you visualize it, the arguments become very obvious."
- **Demands a real-world instantiation of every abstract line.** "Give me an example of that. What does that really really mean?" And "give me the no BS version." When C offered a Taylor Swift example, he accepted it but offered his own (Pythagoras, quadratics, parabolas in high school) as the more universally resonant one.
- **Burden of proof taught through court standards.** Criminal: "without a shadow of a doubt... better to let 100 guilty men free than one innocent man perish." Civil: "more likely than not, over 51%." Then mapped onto LSAT validity: the evidence must guarantee, not make likely.
- **The New York / US guarantee image.** "There's no world where you can be in New York City and not be in the US. It's not a matter of likely."
- **The customs declaration metaphor for assumptions.** The conclusion is the border. "Whatever you do not declare out loud, think of it as the assumption. It's something you're trying to sneak over the border." Payoff line: "we don't grant them anything they don't say in the evidence."
- **The payment metaphor for necessary vs sufficient.** The conclusion is the price, the evidence is what is in your bank account. Cigarettes cost 20 dollars, the customer paid 18, so 2 dollars are owed. A sufficient assumption is anyone handing over 10 dollars, more than enough, and overpaying is fine. A necessary assumption is that at least 1 dollar must be paid: not enough on its own, but without it the 2 dollars is impossible. "It sounds ridiculous as I say this. If you don't pay 1 dollar, obviously you can't pay 2. It's almost so ridiculous when you say it out loud, but it's just literally what it is."
- **The Jenga image for negation.** "If you take certain pieces away, the whole thing will fall. But that one piece alone doesn't guarantee the whole thing will stand."
- **The frat boy and the old man.** "Think of the frat boy, the party boy. That guy goes crazy, that's the sufficient assumption guy. The necessary assumption is the old man that's paying the bills, making sure the electricity is running. Very simple, very conservative." Sufficient is what the argument desires; necessary is what the argument requires.
- **Warns that necessary assumptions hide by being boring.** "The thing that makes them challenging is that sometimes they're too straightforward. They're too obvious... it can just kind of go past us." He described the correct answer approvingly as "very weak, very safe, very fluffy, very boring."
- **Blocks premature answer-choice reasoning.** When C started to justify D again, he cut it: "no, forget about the answer choices. Just talk to me."
- **Names her wrong answer's trap as an intuition trap, not a mistake.** For D he built a parallel from her own life: "if I say to you, you should be studying two hours a day, it naturally would sound like I'm saying you're not doing that right now. But did I ever say that?" Then extended it: "when someone says that, they're usually throwing shade in an indirect way. But that doesn't actually technically mean that."
- **Tests answer choices by literal reading.** On D, "no secondary school": "take it very literally. If there's even one school that teaches this, this answer choice would be wrong. He doesn't have to be assuming zero." On B, "at least as important": "can you think it's 1% less important? And would that mean the argument completely collapses?"
- **Endorses her think-aloud habit and explains why it works.** "It's forcing you to recognize patterns out loud... you take your thoughts from in here to actualizing it outside of your brain. You actually get to see your thoughts versus when it's muddled in there, you don't actually know where the potential issues are." Compared it to journaling. C connected it to acting: "if I tell an actor to read their monologue silently, they don't get any premise of their character, but when they speak the text out loud, it's the same thing."
- **Demands specificity in objections.** "Not just saying the conclusion is wrong because they didn't prove it. That's not enough. Introduce a specific situation where it respects the bounds of the evidence but shows the conclusion is wrong."
- **One method for all question types.** "It doesn't matter the question type, fundamentally it's the same. You always want to identify what can I think of that could show the conclusion could be false. A loophole."

## Worked examples

- **Necessary assumption question, secondary school science curriculum.** Stimulus: abstract knowledge of science is seldom useful for the decisions adults typically make in daily life. But the skills taught in secondary school should be useful for making such decisions. Therefore secondary school science courses should teach students to evaluate science-based arguments regarding practical issues such as health and public policy, instead of or perhaps in addition to teaching more abstract aspects of science.
  - C answered D cold, reasoning that "therefore this should be happening" implies it is not currently happening.
  - Structural pass: line 1 premise, line 2 an opinion (flagged by "but" and "should"), line 3 the conclusion (flagged by "therefore").
  - Gap located by checking what appears in the conclusion but never in the evidence: evaluating science-based arguments regarding practical issues. "We have nothing about teaching students to evaluate science-based arguments. It's not even mentioned in our evidence. They just slipped it in there."
  - Assumption articulated: evaluating science-based arguments is a useful everyday skill. Joshua tested it against ordinary life: "how often do you every day have to evaluate science-based arguments?" C: "when my partner's mad at me, that's the only time."
  - Correct answer E: the ability to evaluate science-based arguments regarding practical issues is sometimes useful in making the decisions adults typically make in their daily lives. C selected E after the necessary-assumption framework was taught, and justified it herself: the other choices compare two modes of learning, E addresses the ability, which is what is missing.
  - A eliminated on "the most": too strong for a necessary assumption.
  - B eliminated ("at least as important"): a 1% difference would not collapse the argument.
  - D eliminated on descriptive vs prescriptive plus the literal reading of "no secondary school."
