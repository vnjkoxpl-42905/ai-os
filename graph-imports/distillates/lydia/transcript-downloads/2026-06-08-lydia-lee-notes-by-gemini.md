# Lydia Lee, 2026-06-08

The session where Joshua taught claim strength as a formal system: four indicator groups (must / cannot / could / not necessarily) mapped to percentage ranges and burdens of proof. Applied first to a question she got wrong on the objectivism passage, then across a fresh timed passage on cultural imperialism (PT132 S3 P3).

## LSAT concepts

- **The four indicator groups.** Two certainty terms and two possibility terms:
  - **Must** = 100%. Synonyms: necessary, requires, has to, always, forever. To disprove, show one exception. "if I wanted to disprove this claim, all you have to demonstrate is that it's possible."
  - **Cannot** = 0%. Synonyms: never, ever, will not. To disprove, show a 1% chance. "All you have to do is show that it's possible."
  - **Could / may / can / possible** = 1% to 100%. To disprove, "you have to show it's impossible." Very hard to disprove, very easy to support.
  - **Not necessarily** = 0% to 99%. Simply the negation of must. "not necessarily means not must... anything that's not 100% could work."
- **The family structure.** Must and cannot are "mom and dad"; could and not necessarily are the weaker children. "could is a weaker version of must because 100% falls under both must and could." The two possibility terms differ only at the extremes: "could includes 100%, not necessarily does not; not necessarily includes 0%, but could will never include 0%."
- **The warranty/lawsuit frame for qualifiers.** "if you're a lawyer, when you write a commercial... think of these words as the words that will prevent someone from filing a lawsuit against you. This product may have XYZ side effects — that's to give yourself a covering in the event something goes wrong." The LSAT test-taker's job is the reverse: "what is the burden of evidence... where's the bounds of the warranty?"
- **No indicator word means certainty by default.** "whenever there's none, the default is certainty. You want to read it as though it says must." Demonstrated on "Jackie is planning to go to law school" — reading it as "Jackie must be planning to go to law school" is an equivalent statement, "it would have made no difference."
- **The action term is not the qualifier.** "planning" is an action, not a strength indicator. "all I have to prove is that she had the plan to go to law school... I'm not proving that she's going to law school. But I do have to prove that with 100% certainty. Two different claims. Very different claims."
- **"Unreservedly" is a must-category word.** "unreserved means it's a technical term. It's like saying 0%. Not even one reservation." So "unreservedly optimistic" is a far stronger claim than "strongly supportive," despite intuition running the other way. "if I were to hear both of those statements in a normal day-to-day conversation, I'd [agree with you]... but unreserved is very very, it's what makes [it] biggest."
- **Soft language does not downgrade a recommendation.** "the LSAT writers like to use soft language at the climax of the passage because people get baited... the strength of it doesn't affect whether it's a recommendation or not. They'll never go all the way into one side. They'll give you their opinion tempered with some sort of qualification, but it does still point towards that one direction."
- **Relatively true, not objectively true.** "we're looking for the one that most accurately describes it. It doesn't necessarily have to be objectively true. It just has to be relatively true — relative to the other answers."
- **Evaluation is descriptive, recommendation is prescriptive.** "think of recommendation as a prescription for something, and evaluation is more of a description... a doctor can tell you what's wrong with you, and a doctor can also tell you what you should do to get better." "Should" and "ought" are the recommendation markers; "flawed" and "wrong" are the evaluation markers.
- **Purpose answers must mirror the passage's move.** "whenever you see a purpose question you want it to reflect the language of whatever type of claim it is. So if it's a recommendation, we would want to see should, ought, arguing for. But if we see an evaluation, we'll expect it to say a certain viewpoint is flawed."
- **The half-right trap.** "the biggest danger for RC questions... one of their most notorious tricks. They will usually have the first half be correct because they want you to read the first half and kind of turn off the engines. And then the second half will have a detail that's wrong. Expect that every time you see an answer is attractive. That last half you want to really tune in."
- **Run the move analysis at sentence level too.** "first, what is the function of the paragraph, and then what is the function of the sentence in the paragraph... do the same analysis for the sentence in relation to the paragraph. We're basically doubling down on it."
- **Quantity has its own strength scale.** "some" means one or more, so it is trivially easy to prove. Contrast a choice that would require all viewers to watch for the same reasons.
- **A question stem's constraint can defeat a true answer.** "C is not technically false — based on the passage it's actually the entire point of the author's recommendation — but it specifically fails the narrow constraints of the question stem." Distinguish the ultimate goal from the precondition: "Answer C is a finish line. Answer B is a starting block."
- **The tiebreak rule.** "if you're stuck, go with the answer that's part of the could or not necessarily camp, not ever the must... you're by default playing it safer. This also applies in LR, except LR has a nuance where you want stronger answers for weakening and strengthening questions."
- **Blind review must be blind.** "don't let yourself see the score before you blind review... you want to be able to go back to the questions and still say I would have chosen the exact same answer the second time around, not because you know that you got a high score."

## Teaching moves

- **Catches a process error in the first two minutes.** Lydia mentioned in passing that she views her score, then blind reviews. He interrupted to stop it, and explained the bias in both directions: a high score makes you defend wrong answers, a low score makes you change right ones. "there's going to be a bias there."
- **Frames the qualifier skill as the single most underrated thing on the test.** "this is like a really really really really underrated skill. Very underrated... there's certain skills that seem obvious when you say them, but if you don't really register it, it doesn't sink in, because they go under the radar." And later: "these are the most important indicator words, all indicator words... the house that you're building on is going to be based off the level of strength. The fridge is cool, the microwave is cool, but it's not as important as how strong your foundation is."
- **Teaches the framework with a running silly example rather than an LSAT stimulus.** "AJ may eat Russian food" / "AJ cannot eat Russian food" / "AJ must eat Russian food" / "AJ not necessarily eats Russian food," walking her through the burden for supporting and for disproving each. Keeps the logic clean of content.
- **Uses negation of "cold" to teach not-must.** "if I say cold, how do I negate cold? Not cold. Right. So does that mean hot? Could be. Exactly. But it doesn't necessarily mean that."
- **Plants a deliberate trap to teach the action-vs-qualifier distinction.** He wrote "Jackie is planning to go to law school" knowingly: "I purposely wrote that... because when you see planning it seems like oh, planning means it's weak."
- **Installs a standing rule mid-session.** "from now on when we go through every answer choice, whenever you choose an answer choice, I'm going to force you to tell me which category of the four does it fall under. And then you'll notice instantly how insane some of the answers are without even realizing how strong they are."
- **Then immediately enforces it.** On the "some viewers" question: "remind me based off the four categories, what's the nature of the answer choice that we would want? What indicator words would we want?"
- **Separates a nuance miss from a method failure.** After the objectivism question: "your issue wasn't necessarily that you got the wrong answer because of a misunderstanding. You knew what you were looking for. It just came down to interpreting what optimistic means versus strongly supportive... that's not so much of an issue, I wouldn't be stressed about that."
- **Admits the counterintuitiveness rather than pretending it's obvious.** "Even I wouldn't have expected this to be D."
- **Makes her produce the smoking-gun line for a categorization.** After she called the passage a recommendation: "where's the quotes for your understanding for why it's recommendation? What sentences? If you underline the sentences that indicate the recommendation, which lines would be..."
- **Pushes back on double-labeling a paragraph.** "when you see evaluate, you don't want to say both... stick with one of them ideally. You can do two max, but avoid two if you can."
- **Asks for the four moves back-to-back, stripped of content.** "just tell me the four moves back to back. Without even telling me the content of the matter."
- **Declares a question type not worth the time.** "these questions are a little out of the norm... I'm even reluctant to want to do this question because it's such a rare type of question. It's almost never going to be presented. It might be better to just go on to the next question because it's not a good use of your time."
- **The sinking-ship argument for 100% accuracy before leveling up.** "there's no value in leaving holes in the ship. If you take off and you know there's just a couple holes, you're going to pay the price eventually. You're going to sink eventually... especially because you want to get into the 170s range. There's no room for letting things go. Every point counts."
- **Deflects the comparative-passage request to a dedicated session** rather than cramming it in, and commits to it as next session's focus.
- **Refuses to let his own preparation shortcut the diagnosis.** She asked whether he'd read the passage; he read it live in three minutes rather than skipping the shared reading. "if you don't, the question type is probably not the reason as to what the issue is."

## Worked examples

**Passage 1: objectivism in legal discourse** (brought by Lydia from her own untimed practice, a question she had gotten wrong).

Structure as she categorized it, endorsed by Joshua:
- P1: **explaining** objectivism and its flaw — the objectivist standard is hard to reach.
- P2: **evaluating** why the flaw arises — those fluent in legal language are treated as objective, and those without that literacy are rejected as false.
- P3: **recommendation** — reformers propose replacing abstract legal discourse with personal narrative, which can generate empathy and "shatter the complacency of the legal establishment," and thus "the engaging power of narrative might play a crucial positive role."

The attitude question. Lydia hesitated over "might" and picked based on softness. Joshua's route: the last few sentences of P3 are not attributed to the reformers ("they argue that" attaches earlier), so they are the author's own voice, and they are positive. That eliminates ambivalent, somewhat skeptical, and strongly opposed. The final pair was **strongly supportive** (correct) versus **unreservedly optimistic**. "Unreservedly" is a must-category term meaning zero reservations, which the passage's hedged "might" cannot sustain. "Strongly" leaves room; "unreservedly is zero."

Lydia's own analogy for her intuition, before being corrected: "running might make you lose 50 lbs, opposed to running will make you lose 50 pounds, or I strongly advise you run to lose 50 pounds."

**Passage 2: cultural imperialism** (PT132, section 3, passage 3 — chosen deliberately because Joshua had not seen it either, after she had already done two of his other candidates).

Structure:
- P1: **evaluating**, negatively. Explains cultural imperialism (dominance of some countries' culture over others) and criticizes the assertion that it harms domestic culture as abstract and based on little research.
- P2: **explaining**, via a study/analogy. Anthropological findings that in cities where imported programs were available, domestic dramas were popular, and domestic TV serves "an analogous function to oral poetry."
- P3 and P4: **recommending**. Two questions researchers must address; marker word "needed."

Lydia's own way in: "I like to think of it like exporting and importing things and how there's tariffs sometimes to protect the inner part."

**Q: Purpose of the passage.** Correct because it contains "should" and "argues." Joshua: "What's the word that gave it away? Should. Should is a prescription."

**Q: Organization of the passage.** Lydia first chose **D** — "takes issue with an assertion, presents examples that run counter to the assertion, [then] discusses two ways in which a new approach will benefit proponents." The first two thirds are right; the last clause is not. The passage gives **two things that must be considered or addressed**, not two benefits. Correct answer **B**. Lydia diagnosed her own miss: "I kind of overlooked the first question. They must investigate. And then here says another question that is imported. So I just kind of glazed over that." This is the half-right trap in its pure form.

**Q: Function of the anthropology study sentence in P2.** Correct: it cites evidence that contradicts claims made by international communication specialists. Route: P2's function is to explain P1; the sentence's function within P2 is premise/evidence. Lydia landed it: "So it's a premise to this conclusion." Joshua: "the premise — another word for that is evidence."

**Q: What can be concluded about the TV viewers studied.** Correct answer uses **"some"** and mirrors the oral-poetry analogy: some viewers watch domestic TV for at least one of the reasons they enjoy oral poetry. Burden: "At least one reason. Just one reason. You're good to go." Eliminations Lydia produced herself: "they would gradually come to prefer" (unsupported), "give it up for watching TV exclusively" (unsupported), "would likely watch more TV if they did not have oral poetry" (unsupported), and "enjoyed domestic TV mainly because they had little access to imported ones" (the passage says imported programs *were* available).

**Q: Empirical study of external cultural influence must begin by identifying...** Lydia chose **C** and was wrong; correct is **B**. C describes the ultimate goal — understanding the impact of external cultural influences on people's lives — which is genuinely the author's point, but the stem asks what the study must **begin by** identifying. The next sentence in the passage answers it: "the first question they must investigate is what model best represents the true relationship." Joshua also flagged that C carries no qualifier, so it defaults to a 100% claim, and that "must" in the stem demands a certainty-level answer. "Answer C is a finish line. Answer B is a starting block."
