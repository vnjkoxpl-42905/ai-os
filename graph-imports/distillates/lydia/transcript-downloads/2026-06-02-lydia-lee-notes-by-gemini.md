## LSAT concepts

- **CEER as memory compression, not classification for its own sake.** "when you can categorize information as you read it you remember it better because you put things in buckets, your brain will be able to recall those buckets versus trying to remember all of the individual things in each bucket." The NASA analogy: remembering "National Aeronautics and Space Administration" word for word is hard; "spacecraft, NASA" is unforgettable.
- **Explaining**: "the goal with this sentence or this paragraph is to explain a concept, a problem or a theory."
- **Comparing**: track what is being set against what.
- **Evaluating**: "taking the author's opinion and making a judgment call... I'm evaluating the situation as a judge. I'm saying as a judge of the situation, this was good, this is bad, this is very bad."
- **Recommending**: "advocate for a specific position or a course of action... It's one thing for me to criticize. It's another thing for me to assert a position." Explicitly **not** a matter of strength: "it's not so much about strength. That's not the way you want to think about it... he can be really strong and still be critically assessing something."
- **Main-point priority ladder**: recommendation beats evaluation beats explanation/comparison. "If there's recommendation, the main point will be the recommendation. If there's an evaluation and no recommendation, then the main point will be the evaluation. If they have both, then the main point will be the recommendation." Priority runs bottom-up through the four moves.
- **Every RC answer is a Must Be True answer.** "every single question you get you want to think from the perspective of it has to be explicitly supported based off a specific line in the stimulus. You want to get away from the generalities of vagueness. You want to focus on specificity."
- **Question taxonomy: defined / semi-defined / undefined.** Defined means the stem localizes the answer ("what is the purpose of this underlined word"); undefined means an open stem where "you can't really prephrase what the answer is going to look like prior to going to the answers," so the choices themselves drive the search back into the passage; semi-defined narrows to a region (e.g. "legal theorists" lives in paragraphs 1-2). Lydia's refinement, which he confirmed: even on an undefined question, a specific supporting line can still be found, and "it'd be way easier to find. These are easier to find."
- **One answer has support; four have none.** "only one of the answers will have any support for it at all. So four answers will have zero support and one of the answers will have explicit support."
- **Specificity raises the evidentiary bar.** "the more specific the claim is, the more evidence is required for that answer choice to be correct because it's limiting the amount of situations where it could be true. The more general this stays, the simpler it is to prove."
- **Choose the weakest, safest claim.** A choice containing "compatible" is nearly free to support: "it's an amazing setup because we have an incredibly low bar." Contrast "most," "only if," "sufficiently," "the only" — all raise the burden.
- **Main-point answers stay broad.** A choice that pins the flaw to one specific argument fails: "for this to be the correct answer it would require for it to be specifically the problem is that one argument, nothing else, which is very particular. That's not general."
- **On an evaluation passage the main point must carry the valence.** "we always need to find that negative idea like oh this is flawed, this is wrong, this is misguided. Those are answers we like, especially with evaluation passages." Neutral answers are eliminated before reading further.
- **"Only if" flags a necessary condition** and is a strength warning. Lydia caught it herself twice.
- **Typically retained implies sometimes transferred.** "the passage says the right for profit is typically retained by the original owner. If something is typically retained then we know it means it's sometimes not retained, so it can absolutely be transferred."
- **Hypothetical-scenario questions map onto a parallel the text already gave you.** "we just need to find the exact parallel scenario the author handed to us in the text."
- **Least-worst, not best.** "you're not looking for the good answer, you're looking for the best one of the bunch, the least worst answer, just like the way you do for LR... the least worst answer is not going to be a good answer always."
- **Getting it right for the wrong reason is the worst outcome.** "if you get the answer right and you get there the wrong way, that's the worst thing that could happen to you because you're teaching yourself really bad habits."

## Teaching moves

- **"Show me the paperwork."** The session's signature line and the homework in one sentence: "I always tell my students, show me the paperwork. You're my lawyer. Show me where it says in the contract. If you cannot show me where it says in the contract, I don't want to hear about it."
- **Underline for every answer, right or wrong.** "Even if you get it wrong or right, still do it because it forces you to be very clear about why you chose an answer, why you did not, or why you wouldn't have chosen the answer. When you just leave it to like getting your feels out... when you get it right, you don't know exactly why you got it right."
- **Underlining kills the "I misread it" excuse.** "when you get it wrong, you can look back and say, I looked at this line of support. Why was that line not supporting the answer that I chose? I can't leave it to 'oh, I just misread the passage.' No, no, no, you didn't misread the passage because you underlined it... I literally chose a sentence. So you force yourself to face the noise."
- **The snowball argument for why underlining pays compound interest.** "your overall understanding of the passage increases as you do more questions. So when you get to the last few questions, which are typically harder, you have a larger database of information that you may have missed when you're reading."
- **Also teaches you why wrong answers are wrong.** "it informs you with two sides of the coin: why the other answers are wrong, and what the passage says about those other ideas, which makes it so you can be faster at the other answers when we go to other questions."
- **Attack mushy passages harder, not less.** Lydia asked why bother underlining on a passage this convoluted. "instead of running away from the passage, get into it even more aggressively with underlining it... it forces you to see through everything that's fluff and getting to the meat and the potatoes."
- **Teaches evaluate-vs-recommend with a Canadian political example, then a personal one.** Residential schools: "saying why the Canadian government has a very ugly history with the indigenous folk, that's me evaluating what's happened... but for me to say we should recompensate for their troubles, that's me offering solutions, that's me giving a recommendation." Then closer to home: "let's say I was your dad and I said, Lydia, you are not working hard enough — that's one thing. But if I were to say Lydia, you should go to law school, that's giving you a recommendation. It's one thing to criticize you. It's another thing to assert what you should do."
- **Quizzes the framework back before applying it.** Opens by asking her to define CEER and each category rather than re-lecturing.
- **Prefilters main-point choices on a single criterion before reading them properly.** "remember what was our golden rule here? What was the number one thing that has to be in the answer no matter what?" Then a rapid A/B/C/D pass on valence alone.
- **Makes her articulate answer strength as a separate step.** "how strong is answer choice C? Does it take a lot to support this, does it take very little to support?"
- **Refuses to let her paper over a claim with a plausible-sounding word.** On "existing statutes": "I can't underline anywhere where it says statutes. I'm not allowed to just pontificate about the idea of statutes because it's not there... the passage is about philosophical debate, theoretical justifications for copyright. It never actually analyzes real world legal statutes."
- **Accuracy strictly before speed.** "don't do it with any time. Take away time... students worry about time, and what ends up happening is they forget that you can do it as fast as you want, but if you don't get the answers right, you might as well not do it at all... get every question individually right and then let the speed follow you."
- **One passage at a time, with an explicit level-ladder.** "only throw in multiple passages when you can do one passage perfectly consistently... I've got level three passages good, I can do them comfortably. Can I do level four passages now, getting them consistently right? Then go to level five, and then worry about adding volume."
- **The review-latency argument for single passages.** "when you get a question wrong, you can address it right there and then versus waiting for it 20 questions later when you've forgotten the details of your thought process."
- **Is transparent about his own platform's bugs** rather than deflecting (see Logistics).

## Worked examples

**Passage: PT130, section 2, passage 3 — the tangible object theory (TOT) of intellectual property.**

CEER breakdown as Lydia produced it:
- P1: **explaining** the tangible object theory and its proponents.
- P2: **explaining** the perceived support for the theory — a seller of land retains certain rights, and theorists apply that notion of retained rights to the domain of intellectual property (music scores, right to copy for profit, right to use as a guide to produce other things). Last sentence: "among the rights typically retained would be the right to copy the object for profit."
- P3: **evaluating**. The author enters and points out the theory's inadequacies — works consisting of ideas, live broadcasting, and the poet case. Closing line: under the TOT "there seem to be no grounds for the poet claiming copyright unless the poet conveys all the ideas expressing the work."

No comparison, no recommendation. Therefore the main point centers on the evaluation, and the evaluation is negative. "our main point question has to say that explicitly in one way or another... there's no negotiating with that component, we need to see something say it's negative."

**Q: Main point.** Correct answer **B**, which calls the theory misguided. A and C eliminated instantly for being neutral. D eliminated for over-specificity — it pins the failure on ignoring that "ideas should be allowed to circulate freely," a notion that appears nowhere in the passage. Lydia: "nothing really suggests the assumption of ideas should be allowed to circulate freely within TOT. I don't know where they put that."

**Q: Which question does the passage most directly answer.** Lydia chose **B**, correct: whether proponents hold that ownership of things other than real estate can involve retained rights. Support underlined in P2 — the seller of land retains rights, and theorists explicitly apply that notion to music scores. Joshua's framing: "when you get any of these questions you should be able to say yes or no directly in the passage."

**Q: Hypothetical — an inventor describes an innovative idea to an engineer, who builds it from their own materials.** Correct answer **A**: only the engineer is entitled. The mapping Joshua drew out loud: "the inventor with the new idea equals a poet with a poem, and the engineer with their own materials equals a friend with their own paper. So who gets the copyright? The friend, because the friend provided the physical paper and created the physical object." The parallel is the poet-dictating-to-a-friend case at the end of P3. Lydia eliminated D on "only if": the passage never establishes a necessary condition, and P2 says retained rights arise when an original owner transfers a physical object they already own.

**Q: What legal theorists would most likely agree with (semi-defined, P1-P2).** Lydia chose **E**-labeled reasoning but the discussion settles on the choice supported by "the chief advantage is that it justifies intellectual property rights without recourse to the widely accepted but problematic supposition that one can own abstract, intangible things such as ideas." So patent protection can be afforded without invoking inventor ownership of abstract ideas. She got there but flagged low confidence.

**Q: Most strongly supported (undefined).** Lydia chose **C**, correct: the idea that ownership of the right to copy an item for profit can be transferred is compatible with the theory. Support: the last sentence of P2, "typically retained," which implies sometimes not retained. "Compatible" makes the bar trivially low.
- **A** — "most" rights retained by the seller. Unquantified in the text.
- **B** — retained rights apply only where intellectual property is not involved. Backwards; the whole of P2 applies them to IP.
- **D** — creators are sufficiently protected under many legal systems. The author's thesis is the opposite: the theory is insufficient and fails people like the poet.
- **E** — "only if" plus computer programs, neither supported.

**Q: Final, undefined — what would an adequate theory do.** Correct answer **D**: an adequate theory must acknowledge that the work's creator owns the abstract ideas. Support: the author's closing critique about the poet having no grounds to claim copyright. Lydia was tempted by C but rejected it on "generally" ("we don't know how often that happens"). Joshua added the stronger reason: C's "existing statutes" has zero textual basis — the passage is philosophical, never statutory.
