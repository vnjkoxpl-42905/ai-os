# Lydia Lee, 2026-07-13

## LSAT concepts

- **Reading comprehension, passage structure (SEIR)**: Joshua tested Lydia's grasp of the SEIR framework, categorizing each paragraph as Compare, Evaluate, Explain, or Recommend. He pushed her off "explain" as a default: "you can avoid using explain, it's the one you want to use the least because it's the most generic."
- **Evaluation vs recommendation**: a sharp distinction Joshua drew when Lydia's answer was fuzzy. Evaluation is when the author "critiques or affirms a position, an idea, a concept" (something is right, wrong, flawed, good). Recommendation goes "one step further and gives a prescription," what you ought to do about it. His example: "me saying your job today, the work you did today was good, that would be giving you an evaluation."
- **Main point**: reframed as topic plus purpose. Joshua: "another way to think of main point is what's the topic and what's the purpose." He also stressed its leverage across the section: every question "in some way will not exactly test your main point but it's round off" it.
- **Passage type, critique/debate**: for a critique-debate passage with the author present, the main point is nearly always the author's side and why the person critiqued is wrong. If no author perspective surfaces, "the fact that there is a debate is the main point" (Lydia's own formulation, which Joshua endorsed).
- **Q&A passage structure**: an alternative lens Joshua offered. When the author poses a question and answers it, read the passage through that frame. He noted this passage was ambiguous, not cleanly in one box.
- **Author attitude/tone**: categorize into three "moods" first, neutral, positive, or negative, then "find an answer that does that job with the least amount of force." Avoid extremes on either side.
- **Extreme language as a disqualifier**: absolutes like "always" and "has always assumed" attributed to a broad group are near-automatic knockouts. Joshua called it the "always knockout function."
- **Inference/detail, attribution errors**: an answer that attributes a view to the wrong party (passage vs author) is out. Joshua called this "strike two" when Lydia's choice ascribed a belief to mainstream economists that the passage assigned elsewhere.

## Teaching moves

- **The core reframe of the session: disqualification, not selection.** Joshua returned to this at least five times and closed on it. "You're not looking for the right answer. Your goal is to look for all the wrong answers until you can't tell me why one's wrong." And: "It's not because it's not good enough. You might say it's not good enough, but not good enough is not the same thing as saying that's actually wrong."
- **"Which one do I hate the least."** His replacement mentality for finding an answer you like. He framed disqualifying as a disposition rather than a technique: "it's not just like a simple thing to say okay I'll do it, it's like a mind state to take on."
- **Missing vs objectively wrong.** The session's most repeated diagnostic. When Lydia eliminated B because it "doesn't talk about" something, Joshua refused the elimination: "I want you to tell me what exactly is wrong, not what's missing. What's missing is what would make it correct." He built an analogy: "if I tell you something is neutral that means there's nothing good or bad about it, but I want you to tell me what's the negative about it." And: "the deprivation of good doesn't necessarily mean wrong." An answer is disqualified only on a contradiction.
- **Stubbornness as a standard.** "You will never allow the sales to choose an answer that has something wrong with it. Even if you just choose no answer, that's how stubborn you want to be."
- **Holding her to what she noticed.** Lydia flagged "always" herself, then picked the answer anyway. Joshua escalated rather than consoled: "I didn't even say it to you, you said it first, so that tells you noticed it, which is dangerous, because if you noticed that means it didn't get past you. If you got past it, I would feel less, I'll have more mercy on you. I'm holding you to the fire because you saw it."
- **Naming a correct answer as bad.** To break her hunt for a satisfying answer, he conceded the credited answer's weakness outright: "this answer is a terrible answer, I'll lie to you, it's a horrendous answer. This is literally best available doesn't mean good." Lesson drawn: "we're not going crazy if we end up having to pick an answer that feels incomplete. Incomplete's okay compared to a detail that's wrong."
- **The "best two" drill.** Mid-session he changed the protocol: for each remaining question, Lydia must name her two best answers, then quote the exact phrase or word that is off in the loser. Introduced explicitly as a fix for her pattern of getting to the final two and choosing wrong.
- **Demand for granularity.** His diagnosis of her error pattern: "you're avoiding, you want to get into the details." Saying "it's not doing this thing" broadly is insufficient; "when you leave it open-ended, you give yourself the feeling of confidence, or you leave some empty space." Target: "this is wrong because it's contradicting this other idea," with both contradicting things named.
- **Rapid-fire concept check before applying.** He opened by having her explain SEIR from memory, then quizzed the evaluation/recommendation distinction cold, before opening any passage. "I want to see you do it," rather than talking about method abstractly.
- **Socratic chain to the right answer.** On the railroads question he did not supply the answer; he asked "what is an example of," then "what are they referring to when they talk about increasing returns," walking her to "the pin factory" herself.
- **Homework as written self-correction.** He assigned bullet points explaining what is wrong with each rejected choice, to stop the same mistakes recurring.

## Worked examples

**PT-138, Section 1, Passage 3** (pin factory vs invisible hand; Joshua called it a level five passage). Lydia read cold and mapped paragraphs aloud using SEIR.

- **Paragraph mapping.** Lydia gave explain / evaluate-explain / explain / explain. Joshua's revision: P1 is closer to a comparison (old versus new) than an explain; P2 explains the pin factory and invisible hand; P3 explains the contradiction, why the two ideas clash; P4 covers increasing returns in real industries. She categorized the whole passage as critique/debate, which Joshua held her to for the rest of the session.
- **Main point question.** Lydia chose A, anchored on the last sentence of P3 ("the idea that free markets always get it right depends on the assumption that returns to scale are diminishing, not increasing"). Two disqualifiers Joshua drew out: the extreme "mainstream economists have always assumed," and a misattribution, since P3 says the assumption of diminishing returns dominated mainstream theory, the opposite of the answer's claim. Correct answer was E, which she had held as second best and disliked for feeling incomplete. Real main point: Marshall's book highlights the opposing forces of invisible hand and pin factory, which explains why the latter was deemphasized for two centuries, because "the mathematical modeling was too difficult a problem, that they managed to solve in the late 1970s."
- **Author attitude question.** Lydia read the author as neutral, possibly "curious." Joshua: "curiosity displays a neutral emotion, but the author is not neutral about this." Evidence, the first sentence of the last paragraph: economists "tried repeatedly to bring the pin factory to the mainstream to reflect the fact that increasing returns obviously characterize many enterprises." The word "obviously" is the tell. "He steps out of the neutral zone and he's reporting his voice here to drop his own opinion."
- **Main purpose of paragraph question.** Lydia narrowed to B and D, chose correctly. Her rejection of the "tension between two economic theories" framing was right; Joshua sharpened the reason: a tension requires "two sides pushing against each other," and here one side was simply ignored for two hundred years. B correct.
- **Scientific discipline / "underground river" question.** Lydia narrowed to A and C and picked C correctly, knocking out A on "had always been a discipline." Joshua confirmed "underground river is a synonym for not being mainstream."
- **Railroads in the final paragraph.** Lydia's hardest question. She started on C ("illustrates the shortcomings"), wavered to D ("call attention to the increasing prevalence of industry"), and rejected D correctly, then had to be walked back to why C also fails. Joshua's reasoning: railroads are an example of increasing returns, which is the pin factory phenomenon, used to show economists could not mathematically demonstrate something people plainly observed. The actual shortcoming of the two-hundred-year emphasis on the invisible hand "was that it forced them to completely ignore obvious real world industry." C mischaracterizes: the passage never says increasing returns are on the rise. He noted the credited answer "ropes in some unexpected wording for sure, but that's often the case." Lydia's stated reason for avoiding B was that it mentioned the invisible hand and she "didn't make the reference."
- **Increasing scales, paragraph 2.** Lydia picked E over B because B "doesn't talk about how it produces far more than if each worked independently." Joshua rejected that as a missing-not-wrong elimination, then drew the real distinction out of her: E describes dividing something to be produced rather than specializing on narrow tasks and producing more, "describing it inaccurately, not that it's just not doing the job you want it to do." B correct.
