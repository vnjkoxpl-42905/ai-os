# Lydia Lee, 2026-05-28

The unbilled follow-up promised at the end of the 2026-05-27 session. A full RC passage (Dutch tulip bubble, McKay vs Garber) worked end to end: CEER categorization, then the sentence-by-sentence rephrasing drill, then four questions with every elimination justified against underlined text.

## LSAT concepts

- **The four moves (CEER)**: comparing, explaining, evaluating, recommending. Applied per paragraph, with a hard cap: "we can go up into maximum two moves a paragraph. I don't ever want you to go more than two moves."
- **Explain is the default; prefer any other move.** "explain is the most default generic one. If you can choose any of the other moves when you have that option, go with the other three because it's more useful."
- **Evaluation or recommendation locates the main point.** "whenever you see an evaluation or you see recommendation, that specific thing is going to be the main point of the passage."
- **Surrogate author rule**: when the author never voices a position in a comparison passage, default to whichever side is discussed more. "You want to your default always is which one did he speak about more. That's the default. That's a rule of thumb... You want to pretend like he is that side speaking." He puts a number on it: "in every 90% of times 95% of times unless the author says his own side."
- **But do not confuse the surrogate with the author.** "you don't want to make the mistake of thinking that was author speaking because it's just bad practice." Correct in effect, wrong in method.
- **Every paragraph is an LR stimulus.** "You want to think of every paragraph as a stimulus of the LR section... This paragraph is an intermediate conclusion. This paragraph is a main conclusion. This paragraph is like a premise... And then you want to look at the whole passage and say what is the main point?"
- **Agreeing with the premise, rejecting the conclusion.** Lydia's own formulation of Garber's move, which Joshua called "phenomenal": "he agrees with the premise. He doesn't agree with the conclusion."
- **Rational-actor rule**: "You need a framework to understand every passage you read is based off a real person... these are educated intelligent people... they should always sound reasonable. You have to ask yourself when you read an answer choice would this be something a reasonable person would agree with." Any choice attributing an unreasonable belief is disqualified. Lydia pushed back ("let's say it's an unreasonable person"), and he refused the exception: "It will never be that. It will never do that... The passages that the LSAT chooses will always be intelligent person."
- **All of RC is Must Be True.** "RC is just a bunch of most strongly supported questions or must be true questions... every answer in RC you want to default to the weakest answer. That's like one of the safest bets you'll ever make. Not only words wise, but even conceptually."
- **Conditional language in an RC answer is a red flag.** "if I ever see a conditional indicator word like if, instantly it's a very strong answer no matter what the answer is... if it doesn't get triggered the whole conditional is a waste of your time. So any conditionals, if, unless, any of these terms we should look at them as very strong answers, very bad signs, it's a red flag."
- **Unqualified means universal.** "if they don't give a qualification, it means universally default."
- **Recognizable wording in an answer is a warning, not a match.** "If you ever see recognizable terms, it's almost certainly not going to be the correct answer... they know that people are going to try to do almost like a keyword search. So they want to prevent that... it's going to have to be a meaning match. They want to filter out people who are just reading for words versus meaning."
- **Synonym, not verbatim, is the good sign.** "Whenever the synonymous term that's not verbatim the same, that's a very good sign. So standard and regular sounds the same... but it's not the same words."
- **Double negatives get rephrased immediately.** "not irrational... they did that purposely. They could have just said the prices are rational. You would have it would have been more intuitive, but they purposely said not irrational."
- **Indicator words in the passage**: "however, but, in, yet" mark a pivot — "the authors change directions." "For" introduces a premise. "Even" marks a concession: "it's when the author is conceding or granting... doesn't mean my point is all wrong."
- **Inference vs basis**: "inference is the other side's conclusion... An inference is when you take premises and you deduce another fact, another belief which should be a conclusion." Disagreeing with an inference is not disagreeing with the basis.
- **Paragraph-function questions are relational.** "It's not just generally what is a paragraph doing? It's what is the purpose it's serving to delivering the main point... the author took the time to write the passage for the main point, that's the only reason why he wrote it."
- **Word-in-context questions**: strip a phrase to its parts and define each. "Standard" has two senses — typical, versus the benchmark others are measured against — and the wrong answer plays on the second.
- **Two rules that together do the work.** "If you can rephrase every claim, and you also for every answer you choose you force yourself to underline the part where you get support from... when you're forced to underline, you couldn't find it because it's not there."

## Teaching moves

- **Fixes the highlighting system before the reading.** Lydia over-highlights whole ideas; he cuts it back to voice boundaries only: "highlight one of them in yellow whenever you see someone's voice jump in... when you see somebody else shift, the first word of that new shift." The reason is mechanical: "when you have too much highlighted... your brain will naturally not take highlighting as serious because it just sees a bunch of. But if it's only one thing highlighted, your eyes will catch it." Underline for importance, keep one color reserved for the author.
- **Reads the whole passage first, categorizing silently.** "We're not going to start and stop just so we can give you the ability to flow and think about it and get familiar with the content of the passage first." Five minutes.
- **Demands an analogous real-world concept.** "when you can think of an analogous concept it really reinforces a connection in your brain. Do you have anything that you could think of as an example of this in the real world?" She offered roller coasters and speculative stocks; both accepted.
- **The rephrasing drill, introduced with an honest cost warning.** "this is going to be very exhausting, but this is a surefire way to guarantee if you're like I want to get a higher score no matter what... It's by rephrasing each claim or each statement as you read it. So you want to read the sentence and then look away and rephrase, next paragraph and then rephrase, and as you're rephrasing you also want to link to the information you read before." Sentence first, then paragraph. "That's how you'll get mastery. Everyone that does it gets way better instantly."
- **Uses the video pause button to enforce it.** He has her literally pause the passage timer/screen after each line and restate from memory. "We're going to do that after every line. We're going to literally do that. It's going to be, I told you it's going to be exhausting, but it will be really powerful."
- **Refuses a half-right paraphrase and sends her back to the exact words.** On "standard pricing pattern occurs for new varieties of flowers," she said "tulips," then "the expensive ones and the common ones," then "the rare ones." He kept asking "Which varieties?" until she found "new." Then: "they didn't say that by accident. That's a very important thing to remember."
- **Brings the economics to life with a livestock analogy.** "think of it in parallel like a cow... if you think about like a chicken and laying eggs, it's like yes, the original chicken might be expensive, but the eggs, they might all sell individually for cheap. But what's the cost for you to make those eggs? Don't cost you anything outside of just watering or taking care of the chicken." Then numbers: "if I were to buy the first original flower for 100 bucks, I can sell the new individual ones for a dollar each... how much does it cost to make the new ones? None." When she said "they broke even," he corrected: "No, not broke even... they're not incurring new costs, but they can sell each individual one."
- **Two cavemen framing for viewpoint questions.** "Before you go into the answers, you want to have like two cavemen thinking of them as arguing. So, like me good, me bad... you want to know what the author's general vibe is. So when you go through the answers you can filter a couple of the answers based off that, then you can do the research."
- **Makes extreme answers absurd by instantiation.** On choice C: "if we take this very literally, in every market ever to exist, this is what Garber would have to agree with. In Japan in the 16th century... the shoes that are being sold at Walmart, those are reasonable. Would that sound reasonable?" And on D: "we're talking about potentially millions of people, all of their investments. It's like no, that's way too radical."
- **"Underline that for me."** Every claim about a speaker's view must be pointed at. When she cited "they could not be sold at 10% of the peak value" as evidence McKay thought prices irrational: "That's a descriptive statement... does that tell you about his belief if it's rational or not?" When she then answered with Garber's words: "I agree. But I don't see Garber's name in the answer."
- **Localize the paragraph before reading choices.** "If the information does not come from the second paragraph it's not going to be true. So you don't want to wrestle with all of the floating ideas in the passage because that can give you brain fog."
- **Skips a question type on strategic grounds.** On the analogy question: "we can skip that for now because it's not going to be a common question. So usually just want to pass."
- **Names understanding over memorizing as the whole game.** "if you understand the passage completely and understand the English and the answer choice as well, the questions are very easy... it's not a hard test, it's just very challenging to turn it into natural English where you can resonate with it. That's the difficult part." And: "if you have a loose bolt here, loose bolt here, what ends up happening is your ship will crash."
- **Reminds her the passage does not disappear.** "the passage is not going anywhere... I have to always remind my students of that. It's like people forget the passage, they're not going to take it away from me as you start doing the questions."
- **Owns the discomfort of being blunt.** When she thanked him for being strict, he said: "I hate doing it. I feel like I'm being annoying, but it's that cool details."
- **Won't decide session frequency for her, but frames the tradeoff.** "the two sessions are good because you have the ability to fill in any gaps of anything that I didn't get the last time... but one week apart can be tricky because what ends up happening is you forget the little details I might throw at you that I sent to you seven days ago."

## Worked examples

**Passage: Dutch tulip mania, McKay vs Garber.** Structure as categorized:
- P1: **explaining** (defines a speculative bubble) + **comparing** (McKay says the tulip episode was one; Garber says it was not). Cap of two moves reached.
- P2: **explaining** McKay's side, by example — prices rose 1636-37 then collapsed to under 10% of peak.
- P3: **explaining** Garber's side, and by the surrogate-author rule, **evaluating** — since Garber gets more space and the author never speaks, Garber is treated as the author's position, which upgrades the paragraph from explain to evaluate and makes Garber's conclusion the passage's main point.

Speculative bubble defined by Lydia: "a price of something goes all the way up and then immediately it goes down," because of "loss in confidence and speculation."

Garber's argument reconstructed line by line via the rephrasing drill:
1. Garber concedes prices rose dramatically 1636-37 and fell — same premise as McKay, different conclusion.
2. The rise and fall "can be explained in terms of fundamentals."
3. A **standard pricing pattern** occurs for **new** varieties of flowers. Unpacked as "standard" = typical + "pattern" = something that recurs.
4. When a prized new variety is developed, its original bulb sells high.
5. **However**, as bulbs become more available through reproduction of the original, price falls rapidly; within under 30 years bulbs sell at reproduction cost.
6. **But** this does not mean the high original prices were irrational, **for** earnings derivable from the millions of descendant bulbs can be very high **even if** each individual descendant commands a very low price.

Main point: the rapid rise and eventual fall of tulip bulb prices does not indicate a speculative bubble, because the price behavior is standard fundamentals, not hype.

**Question 1 (Garber's position).** Lydia answered in 20 seconds, correct. Support: the last sentence of P1, "arguing that there's no evidence" the episode involved a bubble. She also flagged a tempting wrong choice: it attributed the view to "many economists" when the passage only gives Garber — and the passage said "widely mistakenly believed," which tells us nothing about how many economists hold it.

**Question 3 (what McKay's account supports).** Lydia chose **B**, correct.
- **A** (prices were not due to the flowers being fashionable) — fashionability appears only in Garber's paragraph, not McKay's. Also inverted: McKay's account is that the tulips were hyped and then fell, so "not fashionable" is roughly the opposite of his position. Joshua separates the two ideas: "speculation is saying what the future price could be... fashionability is like how trendy, how much are they in right now. That's not speculation."
- **C** (the Netherlands was the only center of cultivation in the 17th century) — "the only is the strongest indicator to tell you that's a very powerful claim and we don't like powerful claims." Read literally it claims nowhere else on Earth grew new tulip varieties.
- **D** (most investors were rational in all their investments) — quantifier stack of "most" plus "all," about millions of people's entire portfolios. "That's way too radical."
- **E** (McKay mistakenly inferred the low eventual prices were irrational) — McKay's paragraph never characterizes prices as rational or irrational at all. It only describes. The rationality language belongs to Garber. This is where the underline rule did the work.
- **B** is correct because it is the weakest, most easily provable claim: McKay believed the prices were speculative.

**Question 4 (function of the second paragraph).** Correct answer explains the basis on which a scholar makes an inference the author disagrees with. Lydia had resisted "basis" because Garber accepts McKay's premise. Joshua separated the terms: the choice says the author disagrees with the **inference**, not the basis, which is exactly right. Wrong choices: the one saying the paragraph shows a mistake (that is the main point, not this paragraph's function) and the one saying the paragraph undermines the case (P2 only states the other side; the undermining happens later).

**Question 5 ("standard price pattern" most nearly means).** Correct answer: a pricing pattern that regularly occurs in certain types of cases. Eliminations:
- The choice containing "pricing pattern" verbatim — disqualified on the recognizable-wording rule.
- The choice about a criterion ("commonly... criteria") — nothing in the passage sets criteria.
- The choice about a standard against which other patterns are measured — plays the wrong sense of "standard." "there's two definitions of standard: could be this is typical, and standard could be this is the metric we measure other things by. That's not the appropriate definition here."
