# Lydia Lee, 2026-05-27

## LSAT concepts

- **Reading comprehension passage types**: two families, problem passages and clash passages. Identifying the type early fixes where everything else will sit. "once you're able to identify the passage type, your understanding of the structure of where everything will lie will be very quick."
- **Problem passages**: focus on an ongoing problem, not a historical one. Three subtypes: (1) author's solution, the author states a problem and gives their own fix; (2) someone else's solution, also called viewpoint solution, the author reports a third party's fix; (3) exploring a problem, the author elaborates on the nature of the problem and offers no solution.
- **Clash passages**: also called critique/debate. Joshua names this "the dominant type of passage type" and says it is getting more common. Subtypes: author criticizes a viewpoint; author presents a viewpoint then labels it problematic; author criticizes a viewpoint and offers their own position; author presents multiple viewpoints and takes a side; author juxtaposes two views without attacking either.
- **Main point follows from passage type**: author's solution passage, the main point is the solution, not the problem description. Someone else's solution, the main point is that solution. Exploring a problem, the main point is a boiled-down summary of the problem. Author criticizes a viewpoint, the main point is that the viewpoint is wrong or flawed. Author takes a side in a clash, the main point is the author's side only. "It's not your side and my side and my side's better. No, it's just my side."
- **The "why" framework**: ask why the author wrote this sentence, this paragraph, this passage. Read for structure, not content retention. "we're not looking to memorize content. We're trying to read for structure."
- **Hypothesis and validation**: form a hypothesis about the author's purpose within the first few sentences, then modify, confirm, or replace it as new information arrives. "you want to think of RC as science experiment."
- **The CEER method**: the author is always doing one of four things: comparing, explaining, evaluating, or recommending. Applied per paragraph at minimum, per sentence ideally. Evaluating states merits or flaws without asserting a position; recommending asserts a position or says how things ought to be. "It's one thing to suggest, criticize a position, and another thing is to actually affirm a position saying this is what you should do."
- **Comparison implies more**: "whenever we see a compare passage... that's usually brought with a recommendation or an evaluation."
- **Voice tracking**: identifying who is speaking when, and each speaker's main point, carries over from LR. "being able to identify who's talking when and their main point, just like we do for LR, is really monumentally important."
- **Prephrasing main point**: commit to a one-sentence main point before reading answer choices. "you want to not let the answers guide you. You want to be able to have a premeditated answer."
- **Underline-or-eliminate rule**: "we should never ever choose an answer unless you can explicitly tell me... if you cannot underline and point towards something in the passage that gives you direct support then you shouldn't choose it."
- **The sentence before and after rule**: for function and meaning questions, the answer lives in the adjacent sentences, not the target sentence. "the answers to these questions are always going to be in a sentence right after, at maximum going to be two sentences after... it'll never be about the information that's being stated in the actual sentence for the most part."
- **Obscure vocabulary is always defined in place**: "if they use a weird word they will always define it in the exact same sentence always. Or the very next one using some sort of synonym." Test makers use hard words to intimidate, not to test vocabulary. "test makers are not going to test you whether you swallowed a dictionary."
- **Context mapping and placeholder labeling**: do not define the word, determine its function. Give it a name: "call it thing X."
- **Surface-word matching is inverted**: recognizing an answer choice's wording in the passage is a warning sign, not confirmation. "if there's something that you recognize wording wise in the answer choice that was stated in the passage that's actually a bad sign because they want you to find a meaning match... more so conceptual overlap. They're going to disguise it behind abstract words."
- **Example means illustration**: "taking a general idea and giving you an individualized instantiation of that one idea."
- **Conceding is not agreeing**: an author can grant that an opposing argument is well reasoned while still disagreeing. "I can disagree with you and still concede that your point makes sense."

- **Credits his sources.** Attributes the problem-passage framework to his own teacher and to RC Hero, notes 7Sage calls clash passages critique/debate, and credits PowerScore for voice identification. He then argues critique/debate "is too generic."
- **Times tables analogy for annotation.** She dislikes writing, so he reframes annotation as temporary scaffolding: "it's like when you're doing times tables when you're learning multiplication. You write it out and it gives you the ability to not have to write it out." Practice untimed, purely to identify the passage type.
- **Annotation is art, not science, but must be consistent.** "You can enter it how you feel comfortable, but it has to be consistent. Choose one and stick with one."
- **Quizzes the framework back before applying it.** He asks her to state the expected main point for every passage subtype before touching a passage. She does, mostly correctly, and he corrects one drift where she called the clash resolution a "solution."
- **The annoying younger sister as the "why" framework.** "I have a younger sister, so she can be really annoying. She would often bother me and just for everything you could think of, she'd ask me, But why? But why? But why? That's the kind of framework."
- **Why asking why works, mechanically.** "when you're forced to ask yourself why, what ends up happening is indirectly you end up really digesting the information because you're like, Oh, I got it wrong. It was not about this. It was about this. That's good. Because you can only know what's wrong if you know what's right."
- **Names the cardinal sin.** "the cardinal sin I find for most people for RC is they're scanning through a passage without thinking. They're getting bogged down in trying to understand the details without actively thinking."
- **Attacks the uselessness of "active reading" advice, again.** "The advice to actively read doesn't really help anybody, I find... when it comes to challenging passages, it's hard to suddenly magically begin to just perfectly connect the dots." CEER is offered as the operationalization.
- **Reads first, silently, five minutes, no interruption.** "Pretend like as though I'm not here." Then opens with "how did you find the passage" before any analysis.
- **Prefers she share her screen so she can annotate.** "it's honestly better because you can actually engage with it better."
- **Get ahead of the passage.** "You do not want to think of the passage in the moment. You want to ask yourself where do I think the author is going to go and you would make a prediction."
- **Presses on precision of "refute."** She says the author refuted the critics in paragraph 2; he asks three times where, escalating to "did he explicitly refute it?" until she concludes he did not, only foreshadowed. This distinction is exactly what decides a later answer choice.
- **Retroactively reveals why an earlier question mattered.** "when I asked you what the purpose of second paragraph is, you said this, the author criticizing. The reason why I asked that was very intentional. The author isn't saying they're wrong in that paragraph. He's giving you the other side, but he's not actually saying they're wrong. He's saying they're reasonable." Her mischaracterization of the paragraph is traced directly to her wrong answer.
- **Plain-language recap of the whole passage.** "critics will often say law has authority because the system enforces it. Period. Done. The author is saying not so fast. He's really just pointing out that judges throw out their own bad rulings and that's not raw power. He's saying it's a force of better reasoning that wins out."
- **Demands justification for eliminations, not just eliminations.** "when you eliminate any answer, I want you to tell me why you think that answer is wrong." Then: "Give me the part of the passage where you can define that. Underline the support for that claim you just made."
- **Diagnoses picking by exclusion.** She chose D saying "I don't like the other answers, but I don't know what D is really saying," and later "I didn't think D was correct. I just didn't think A or C was correct." He treats this as the core failure mode rather than a lucky-guess issue.
- **Normalizes not knowing a word.** "any word that you would find that might be foreign, you know that means there's a million other people who haven't as well. The passage authors know that you won't know that... nobody really. Maybe a few people might know it."
- **Word difficulty as an inverse signal.** "if the language is simple in that same sentence then we have to be worried because we know that it means something more convoluted, but when the word is convoluted it usually means the opposite, which means that word is actually not too complicated."
- **Admits his own dislike of the question type.** "these questions are, I hate these, actually my least favorite questions to do. I hate them so much, but they're important to know."
- **Offers an unbilled follow-up rather than end mid-topic.** "I hate leaving an awkward note... we can do this in a way that you can have the follow-up for this. You don't have to book this. You don't have to worry about anything."
- **Withholds homework to finish the arc.** "I'm going to give you the homework tomorrow because I want you to add one more thing to it... I threw so much, like different pieces, that when I bring it all together you'll see it."

## Worked examples

**Passage: PT114, section 3, passage 2, on judicial authority.** Intellectual authority versus institutional authority.

CEER walkthrough:
- Paragraph 1: comparison. Two competing concepts introduced, intellectual authority versus institutional authority. Intellectual authority defined as arguments that prevail by virtue of good reasoning.
- Paragraph 2: comparison continues, now between the critics' view and the author's. Critics hold judicial authority is exclusively institutional. The author responds without explicit refutation, noting that intellectual authority and institutional consensus are not the same thing, and that some well-reasoned arguments never receive institutional imprimatur.
- Later paragraph: back to the critics' argument, with an example that intellectual authority is not recognized as such.
- Final paragraph: the author's own position. Decisions based on institutional authority can be overturned when badly reasoned or no longer applicable.

Main point as Lydia and Joshua settled it, largely in her words: legal systems default to institutional authority, but intellectual authority is introduced when a decision is badly reasoned or simply no longer applies. Joshua's addition: the author takes his own position rather than siding with either camp.

**Question 1, main point.** Lydia chose D, correctly, by eliminating any choice framing the passage as purely intellectual since the passage mainly rebuts the strictly institutional view. She narrowed to B and D, choosing D for its reference to reconsidering badly reasoned or socially inappropriate decisions. Joshua confirms the main point must capture both the blend and the reconsideration.

**Question 2, function of an underlined portion in paragraph 2.** Lydia chose D and got it wrong; correct answer is A.
- D ("failed to convince by virtue of good reasoning") fails because the passage calls these "some well-reasoned arguments." The author concedes their reasoning is good. It also collides with paragraph 1's definition of intellectual authority as arguments prevailing by virtue of good reasoning, which Lydia quotes back when asked.
- B ("fail to challenge institutional beliefs") fails because the very next sentence says the reasonable argument goes unrecognized precisely because it challenges institutional beliefs. Direct contradiction. Found by applying the sentence-after rule.
- C fails because it describes an example, and "example" means illustration of a general principle, which this is not.
- E ("fails to gain acceptance except by coercion") fails because the passage says some well-reasoned arguments never receive institutional imprimatur. Never, so there is no coercion path to acceptance. Lydia parses the "except" as a negated sufficient condition.
- A is correct. "Failed to gain institutional consensus" matches, because the passage substitutes "institutional consensus" for "institutional imprimatur" at the end of the same sentence. Lydia reconstructs it: "if it's failing to gain this, then it's failing to gain this exclusive institutionalness."

**Vocabulary case study, "imprimatur."** The parallel structure that defines it: "some well-reasoned arguments never receive institutional imprimatur" sits beside "the reasonable argument that goes unrecognized." Well-reasoned equals reasonable; never receives institutional imprimatur equals goes unrecognized. Lydia's own restatement of the strategy afterward: don't get flustered, brush past the word, relate it to the main statement, "this could be called apple and this could be called pear."
