## LSAT concepts

- **Avoid "explain" when another move fits.** "we want to refrain from going to explain when there's other options, because explain is more general and it's less easy to work with, less pointed."
- **The MVP sentence.** "the most valuable sentence — you can think of it as the summary at the end." Here it was the closing line proposing that some clearings are purely social phenomena. Locating it fixes the main point.
- **The structural pivot carries the main point.** "the resource procurement model is widely accepted, then he goes and tells you the alternative theory... it captures the exact structural pivot, which the pivot always introduces the main point, without getting bogged down in the minor details. And we always want that to stay general."
- **"According to" always signals an inference/must-be-true question.** "whenever you see 'according to' anything, it always is going to be an inference question. It's going to be almost a must be true question. So you know that you are not ever going to rely on memory or just vibes. You want to locate the exact concept in the actual passage."
- **Attribution is the discipline.** The core lesson of the session: "the LSAT — it's really important to pay attention to the exact words, the exact groups they're referring to, because they'll bait us." A named model introduced in paragraph 2 may be nothing more than an academic label for the common view already stated in paragraph 1. "they're not introducing a new theory in paragraph 2. They're just giving an academic name to the common view of hunting and gathering that they already established in the very first part."
- **Subject and predicate as an RC tool.** "understanding what is the subject and predicate is another very valuable thing for RC. What is the noun and what is being attributed to that noun, and that goes for every sentence."
- **Control-F is a tool, not a method.** "we do want to use control F, but we want to be very careful with just not obviously getting triggered by it." Keyword proximity is exactly what the trap exploits: the phrase "resource procurement model" and the phrase "increase grazing areas" sit near each other in paragraph 2, but they describe different populations.
- **Population mismatch as an eliminator.** Prehistoric/Mesolithic populations are not the same as recent or premodern populations cited in ethnographic evidence. This killed one answer here and another on the support question.
- **Necessary assumption logic in RC.** "in order to have a fear of something, you have to possess a concept of that thing."
- **Strength language matters most on assumption/inference questions.** "these ones require special attention to how strong the language is, more than any other RC question type." Superlatives like "the first" are near-automatic eliminations: "nothing in this passage signals the humans as the first to do anything."
- **Ritual implies fear.** The reasoning that made the support answer work: "why would you ever perform a ritual for the sake of protection unless you needed something to be protected from?"
- **Weak support beats strong support.** A choice about heightened anxiety in modern people was weaker than one about premodern people performing protective rituals — both because of the population match and because ritual is stronger behavioral evidence of fear than reported anxiety.
- **Word-in-context questions are read from the surrounding sentences.** Lydia's own method, endorsed: "I read the first sentence or a couple sentences before that, and then I read the sentence back... and I try to get a good understanding of why the author used this specific word."
- **Purpose over phrasing.** On an awkwardly worded correct answer: "it's correct because it says non-economic. When the author says purely social, he was intending it as a rebuttal to the common view." Ugly wording is not a disqualifier if the function matches.
- **Parallel-reasoning method in RC.** "we first have to abstract the logical structure of the author's argument in the target paragraph." Here: theory relies on evidence type X; however, other evidence of type X supports an opposing theory.
- **"Same evidence" vs "similar evidence."** The trap answer said the same evidence supported both conclusions. The passage says *other* ethnographic evidence points the other way. Lydia caught it herself.
- **Endorsement questions have one job.** "what would make the author more likely to endorse a flawed theory? You only have to do one job, which is find exactly where the author complained about the theory missing evidence."

## Worked examples

**Passage: prehistoric woodland clearings.** Structure as Lydia read it aloud, move by move:
- P1: explaining the phenomenon (Mesolithic human populations and woodland clearings) plus the common view that they had an economic use, specifically hunting — and noting this lacks evidence.
- P2: comparison. Names the **resource procurement model**; most evidence for it "comes from ethnography rather than archaeology," including the recognition that some recent/premodern populations used fire to increase grazing areas. Then flags that a non-economic explanation is also available.
- P3: introduces **Tuan's** argument — that human relationships to landscape are driven by fear of the wilderness.
- P4: the author's own proposal, building on Tuan: "I propose that one of the primary motivators" was fear of harm from wildlife or spirits, or simply of getting lost.
- P5: extension — clearings become hubs of activity and social meeting spots. The MVP line: clearings may be "purely social phenomena, since where paths meet, wider clearings emerge as corners are cut and intersections become convenient spots for resting."

**Q1, main conclusion.** Lydia chose **B**, correct: the available evidence gives comparable support to an alternative non-economic model. Her eliminations: A and C did not mention a proposed alternative; D attached support to the wrong hypothesis; E was neutral. Joshua confirmed the reasoning as the structural pivot.

**Q2, "according to the resource procurement model, Mesolithic populations engaged in which practice."** **The one she got wrong.** She chose a choice about grazing domesticated animals, reasoning from "used fire to increase grazing areas" in paragraph 2. Two failures compounded:
1. That sentence describes **recent/premodern populations** cited as ethnographic evidence, not Mesolithic populations. Different group.
2. She had mentally filed "the resource procurement model" as something introduced in paragraph 2, separate from the "common view" of paragraph 1. Her own account: "I did a mental search for a model, and I knew this talked about this, but I just never made that tie to this whole entire section, because I just thought it was just a common view as opposed to specifically this model."

The correct answer had to restate paragraph 1's view: humans removed trees to attract grazing animals, or used natural clearings for opportunistic hunting. Her stated frustration: "it was just frustrating because I felt like that wasn't in direct relation to this little section. I had to make that bridge across."

**Q3, evidence supporting the author's proposal (P4).** She initially chose **C** (a map of pathways used by premodern populations) and switched to **E** at the last minute. E is correct: premodern populations performing rituals for protection in the forest. C fails because a map shows routes, not psychology — "it needs to at least point towards the psychological component, the fear." D fails on population: it discussed modern people. She caught that herself: "it talks about modern and we're not talking about modern... I was getting premodern and modern mixed up, and that's why I got rid of everything and had to relook."

**Q4, necessary assumption.** **D**, correct: the humans possessed a concept of wilderness. "for the author to claim humans were afraid of the wilderness, those humans must have possessed a concept of that wilderness." She eliminated a tempting **B** on the word "first": "I never heard anything about being first or being the inventor of something, so I knew those are pretty strong."

**Q5, why the author mentions Tuan's argument.** **D**, correct: to build on it. Her prephrase was right before she read the choices: "I kind of knew that it wasn't like he was bashing this, because he was kind of siding with it." She eliminated the critique/challenge options and the comparison option, and rejected C on "the scholar who developed" — Tuan is not said to have invented anything.

**Q6, what would make the author endorse the first theory.** **A**, correct, a near one-for-one match to the author's stated complaint at the end of P1 that the specific evidence was lacking. She located and underlined it directly.

**Q7, "purely social phenomenon" in context.** Correct answer is the non-economic reading, awkwardly worded. Joshua acknowledged the phrasing is bad and that most students miss it: "this is a tricky tricky question... most students I've done this passage with usually find this one challenging because of the ridiculously worded answer choice." Lydia got it, largely because "all the other answer choices were pretty bad."

**Q8, parallel structure of the ethnographic-evidence argument.** Correct answer matches: an established theory rests on evidence type X, while other evidence of the same type supports an opposing theory. The circumstantial-evidence analogy in the correct choice maps onto ethnographic evidence. She rejected **B** because it said the **same** evidence yields both conclusions, where the passage says *other* ethnographic evidence does. "Evidence A is providing this conclusion, but evidence A could also provide this other conclusion. And that was slightly different."
