# Lydia Lee, 2026-05-06

## LSAT concepts

- **Main conclusion**: two requirements. It must express the author's opinion, and it must be explicitly supported by at least one other statement. Joshua stresses this is a strict technical definition, not the everyday sense: "on the outset has a very precise meaning. It's not flexible."
- **Facts cannot be conclusions**: "a fact can never be a conclusion. Has to be a statement of opinion of some sort." What matters is not real-world truth but presentation: "It has to be a fact as the author presented as a fact. That's all we care about."
- **Not every stimulus is an argument**: a conclusion requires an argument, so a must-be-true stimulus has no conclusion. The conclusion is one full claim, not part of a sentence, and not the whole stimulus.
- **Statements of motive are opinions**: a claim about why someone acted cannot be objectively known. Joshua adds a distinction Lydia surfaced: a stated intention is a fact about what was said, but the actual intention is an inference. "if they said that was their stated intention that would be a factual statement but if I said that was actually their intention there's a difference between those two statements."
- **Referential phrasing**: the LSAT compresses earlier content into "this," "they," "that," and the fix is substituting the referent back in. "they write really badly intentionally. You're never going to see somewhere else where they write really badly with like their intention to write badly."
- **Active reading as anticipation**: "being able to hypothesize where the car is going." Signal words like "normally" introduce a status quo the author will then counter.
- **Indicator words as a trap**: "but" usually introduces a conclusion, which is exactly what makes it dangerous. Relying on it gets students "baited" into the wrong line.

## Teaching moves

- **Fly on the wall.** Same setup he uses elsewhere: "I'll let you take as long as you need... I'm a fly on the wall." Lydia volunteers to think aloud.
- **Diagnostic before instruction.** He asks her to restate the stimulus "in the simplest terms possible" before teaching anything. Her answer is purely structural ("one statement was made about a context and then another statement was made and the authors using premises to support that statement"), which exposes that she has form without content.
- **Reframes question-type difficulty as comprehension failure.** "a lot of reasons why students think they're making a mistake on certain question types... is because they think there's something that's particularly challenging about the question type... but it's actually almost always going to be an issue of just like truly comprehending what was said in the stimulus."
- **Say it in your own language.** Recurring demand: "be able to say it in a language that you speak, not just in like repeating what they said."
- **Read aloud with warned interruption.** "I'm going to interrupt you quite a bit, so like forgive me in advance."
- **The airplane metaphor for early-stimulus pacing.** "my students hate me for this at this point because I say it so often I feel like an old man... think of it as like a plane like when a plane gets trajectory once it takes off you'll be able to go fast. In the beginning you want to collect your thoughts and say what did I just read."

- **Credits her when she outruns the question.** She answers the follow-up before he asks it: "you were two steps ahead. I asked you just for the first step and you were you hit it out of the park."
- **Names rarity of her insight.** On her threshold analysis of "too few households": "Very sharp. Most people don't catch that."
- **Question chosen as a trap, revealed afterward.** "So I chose this question intentionally. - **Platform dogmas are symmetric failures.** LSAT Demon under-structures ("a focus on not having a structural focus... that hinders your ability to have like a guard rail"), 7Sage over-structures ("there's almost an inability to be flexible"). ## Worked examples

Main conclusion question, the four-sentence Epson political-ad stimulus. Lydia's initial read identified sentence 2 ("but the recent ads for Epson's campaign were sent to too few households to serve this purpose effectively") as the conclusion, with everything below as support and the first sentence as context. This is the trap answer.

Path taken:
1. "Normally political candidates send out campaign material in order to influence popular opinion." She predicts the pattern unprompted: "a common statement that potentially the author would rebuttal." Established as status quo plus expected reversal. Paraphrase pushed down to "People usually do this one thing to get an outcome," then to politicians sending flyers so people vote for them.
2. "But the recent ads for Epson's campaign were sent to too few households to serve this purpose effectively." Referent substituted: "this purpose" = influencing popular opinion. Asked whether fact or opinion, she answers fact and supplies the reasoning herself: "let's say there's a threshold within 50 households... that is too few then." She then wavers and defends it as the conclusion anyway; Joshua points out the contradiction rather than resolving it.
3. "The ads were evidently sent out to test their potential to influence popular opinion." He drills the gap between influencing opinion and testing potential to influence. Her first attempt is vague ("one has more of a deeper effect"), then lands on "just to see if it's feasible or not." He supplies the metaphor: "he's dipping his feet in the water seeing do I have what it takes to be the big man in office." Then "evidently," which she reads correctly as "based on the evidence," leading to the motive argument: the author cannot objectively know Epson's intent, so this is opinion.
4. "They covered a wide variety of topics and Epson's campaign has been spending heavily on follow-up to gauge their effect on recipients." She initially calls it "just more context." Asked why they would spend heavily on follow-up, she connects it: "to test the potential." Confirms sentence 4 supports sentence 3.

Resolution: sentence 3 is the conclusion. She names it, then matches it to the answer choice.
