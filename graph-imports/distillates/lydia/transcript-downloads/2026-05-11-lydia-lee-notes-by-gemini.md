# Lydia Lee, 2026-05-11

## LSAT concepts

- **Argument**: premise plus conclusion, nothing more. "What is an argument? Premise conclusion. That's it. the relationship between the two." Premise is the evidence; conclusion is the statement supported by the evidence.
- **Validity**: a conclusion guaranteed by the evidence. Binary, never a matter of degree. "if there's even a 1% chance" the evidence does not guarantee the conclusion, it is invalid. "There's no like closer to valid. This is just 100% binary, one or the other."
- **LSAT conclusions are never valid**: framed as a structural given. "the conclusions will never be valid. Okay, they're always going to be invalid spoiler."
- **Sufficient assumption**: supplies the equal sign between two concepts the argument uses interchangeably. "All I'm doing really is taking the idea that they're trying to interchangeably use and I'm adding an equal sign." Example built from Lydia's own biography: from "Lydia is from Nova Scotia" to "Lydia is in Canada" requires "anyone from Nova Scotia is in Nova Scotia."
- **Necessary assumption**: what must be true for the argument to hold. Distinguished by counterexample: "Lydia has never left her province" is not necessary (you could have left Canada and still be in Canada now), so it is a sufficient assumption offered as a trap.
- **Conditional logic, "unless"**: negate the sufficient. Lydia applies this fluently on two questions without prompting.
- **Only if**: signals the necessary condition. Lydia self-corrects mid-analysis: "only if so that's sufficient. Uh no that's necessary."
- **Main conclusion, two-step test**: (1) the author's opinion, specifically the author's and not the opposing side's; (2) receiving explicit support from at least one other statement. "Opinion by itself doesn't do anything. It needs to have a statement that's also holding it up."
- **A conclusion can never be a fact**: "the evidence is likely to be a fact... they're interpreting the evidence with the lens of the worldview and coming to a conclusion. That is where you are assessing a difference. But it can't just be taking a fact and saying another fact because if that was the case there would be no argument." Framed as "things as they are versus things as they ought to be."
- **The four statement categories**: conclusion, support (premise or intermediate/sub-conclusion), opposing info (opposing viewpoint or concession), neutral info (background). Everything in a stimulus is one of these.
- **Neutral/background is not a premise**: it tells you why the topic is being discussed but does not support the conclusion. "It's just information fluff to be honest."
- **Concession**: a form of opposing viewpoint where the author grants the other side a point. Critically, "do not let it fool you into believing that the author is agreeing with the other side."
- **Pivot words**: but, yet, however, although. They follow a concession or opposing viewpoint and are immediately followed by either premise-then-conclusion or conclusion-then-premise.
- **Premise indicators, FABS**: for, after all, because, since. Joshua rates these above conclusion indicators because "It's impossible for a premise indicator word to not have a premise following it. Impossible," whereas a conclusion indicator might mark an intermediate conclusion. They function indirectly as conclusion indicators too, since a premise must attach to a conclusion.
- **Rephrasing referential conclusions**: when the conclusion is "they are wrong," restate it explicitly. "Take away the they, replace it with the concept that's being spoken of and understand where does the author stand on this."
- **Role/argument part questions**: the answer must be identified by category relative to the main conclusion, then matched against the answer choice's precise wording.
- **Illustration means example**: "By definition, it just means example. There's no difference. You can say those words interchangeably."

## Teaching moves

- **The hostile reader stance.** "we are reading the argument with hostility. We're reading the argument with aggression. We're reading it like you're lying to me. What are you doing? How are you trying to play? Um, like what are you trying to essentially trick me with? You're not reading with the rosy lens thinking that it's going to be given to you as a genuinely good faith argument."
- **The structural engineer framing, aimed at her background.** He asks her undergrad major (economics) before deploying it: liberal arts training teaches interpretive reading, "The LSAT's almost forcing you to do the opposite... we're looking from the perspective of an engineer, an architectural engineer... does this statement with this statement guarantee one or the other?" Content is irrelevant, structure is everything.
- **The archaeologist analogy for categorization.** "we are looking to pick up the bones, the fossils, and we're trying to understand what are these fossils that we have. What could this fossil have been used for? Is this the Tyrannosaurus Rex claws? Is it their arm? Is it their hind leg?... We're looking at each portion and we're trying to categorize and say, Oh, this could have been the leg. This could be the arm." Payoff: "once you get mastery of categorizing, the denser information becomes a joke."
- **Flags his own style.** "I use a lot of analogies, by the way, heads up."
- **Ambiguity vs specificity as the growth lever.** His central diagnostic principle this session: "ambiguity is where there's stagnation and growth. Specificity is the way you can progress." He models the drill-down: "people say I'm not good at questions to do with science... it's not science passages, what precisely is it about science passages? It's the content. What about the content? It's the long words that intimidate me when I see them and when I have to read them under pressure."
- **Validates the intuition, then demands a procedure.** After she describes her conclusion-finding as saying "therefore" in her head: "what you said is completely sensible and like I amen to all what you said it completely makes sense. The thing is though... you want to have a step-by-step process." And he reassures her the procedure is already latent: "you're probably going to be doing this without realizing it."
- **"Why should I believe this?" as an every-statement habit.** "You want to read it as you go. Why should I believe this? Why should I believe this? Why should I believe this? You want to be aggressive. I don't want a nice lawyer. I want a lawyer that's willing to press push."
- **The dishes-on-the-counter analogy for rebuttal vs concession.** Direct rebuttal: "I told you last time you don't leave the dishes on the counter. You always do this." Concession: "listen babe, look babe, I get it, you work so hard, you're the man of the house, rub your shoulder, but you need to put the dishes away." Both do the same work; "one is saying hey like don't do it, the other one's acknowledging, meeting you middle ground, then saying not to do it."
- **Smoke and fire framing for concessions.** "whenever there's smoke, there's fire. If I see concession, I know the author is about to go straight into their argument. So the most important part is about to come up. So it's like a flag." The concession matters less than what it predicts.
- **Builds examples from her life.** The Nova Scotia assumption example uses her own biography; the Ottawa vs Vancouver concession example checks first whether she has been to Ottawa. He notes he is from Ottawa and self-deprecates: "there's not much to say about Ottawa, but it has a beautiful canal."
- **Concedes to the student when she is right.** When she argues that "they are wrong" does not commit the author to Ottawa being better or equal: "That's valid. Actually, I'll give you that... Very very astute." He then folds her point into the lesson.
- **Three-step reading protocol, named and repeated.** Read, recall/rephrase into plain English, categorize. Reinforced at the start of each worked question: "the same thing as last time. We want to read it, rephrase it, and then categorize as we read."
- **Comma as a stopping point.** "when we see a comma we want to use that similar to a period and just say okay let's rephrase from that point."
- **Pushes back on paraphrase that drops content.** When she abstracts to "something depends to achieve a goal": "we want to stick to the content within the scope of the content... Just explain it to me as a story."
- **Credits a student for the FABS mnemonic.** "the ones that I remember a student of mine brought to me that I've always used since he exposed me to them."
- **Names the next session's topic and why it is separate.** "we did the stimulus focus today which is evidence, conclusion, all that, but then there's another entirely different skill which is how to analyze answer choices and how to de-stractify them and be able to map them onto the arguments so you can ideally never get them wrong once you know what you're looking for."
- **Removes deadline pressure once he learns the cost is zero.** On the June vs August test date, after learning her fee waiver lets her switch up to the day of: "don't hold on to it because what's going to end up happening, it'll give you a sense of urgency... plan as though you're going to take it, but be understanding if there's a world where you take it in August."

## Worked examples

**1. Ottawa/Vancouver concession example (constructed by Joshua).** "Ottawa is a great city and has a beautiful canal, but since it is a boring city, it is far inferior to Vancouver." Structure: concession, pivot word, premise, conclusion. Lydia identifies the conclusion as "Ottawa is far inferior to Vancouver." He then adds a referential layer: "People think Ottawa is far inferior to Vancouver since it is a boring city, but they are wrong." Lydia unpacks "they are wrong" into the author's position and correctly notes the author is not thereby claiming Ottawa is superior or equal. Restated conclusion: the author believes Ottawa is not far inferior to Vancouver.

**2. Argument-part question, societal stability.** Stimulus: "The stable functioning of a society depends upon the relatively long-term stability of the goals of its citizens. This is clear from the fact that unless the majority of individuals have a predictable and enduring set of aspirations, it will be impossible for a legislature to craft laws that will augment the satisfaction of the citizenry. And it should be obvious that a society is stable only if its laws tend to increase the happiness of its citizens." The referenced claim is the final one. Lydia works it live: identifies "only if" as necessary, rules out C (not a consequence of the argument), rules out D, deliberates between B and E, and selects B. Joshua confirms and walks it back through: sentence 1 states the thesis up front, "this is clear from the fact that" is a support indicator, and the last claim is a support statement.

**3. Argument-part question, deregulation and medicines (Joshua calls it "a very tricky question... actually a hard question").** Stimulus structure as reconstructed: (1) conclusion, relaxing medicine regulations should not be accompanied by lifting all industrial regulations; (2) unless strict environmental regulations are maintained, endangered plants and animals go extinct; (3) since a large majority of new medicines derive from plants and animals, general deregulation would undermine the original intent. The referenced statement is "since a large majority of new medicines are derived from plants and animals."

Lydia's first pass eliminated C and E, then D, and settled on B. Joshua walks the eliminations:
- B fails because it describes the statement as evidence for a point of view the argument undermines, which would make it opposing info; it is not.
- A fails on the word "not," which she catches herself: the argument is about restricting, not not-restricting.
- C fails because "illustration" means example and this is not an example.
- D is correct. Joshua's framing: "this is exactly what the statement does... It's literally the bridge that connects step one and step three. Without this statement, the extinction of plants and animals wouldn't have anything to do with the pharmaceutical industry. The author is using this fact strictly to link the environmental disaster to the medical disaster."

Lydia's own stated trip point on D: reasoning that undermining an intent to increase availability amounts to decreased availability.
