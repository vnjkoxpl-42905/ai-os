# Bella Lorenzo, 2026-06-17

## LSAT concepts

- **Main conclusion**: not the summary of the argument the way "conclusion" means in English class. On the LSAT it is "literally one line of the argument" that must pass two tests: (1) it is the author's opinion, (2) it receives explicit support from at least one other statement. "If one of these two things are missing, it will never be the right answer for the conclusion."
- **Statement of fact vs opinion**: a statement the author presents as empirical and measurable can never be the conclusion. It can only be background information. "It might not be a fact in the real world, but if the LSAT treats it like a fact, we just accept it as to be."
- **Not every line is a premise or conclusion**: some lines are background information, some are opposing viewpoints, some are concessions.
- **Referential language**: the LSAT deliberately chains reference words ("this," "they," "their") to obscure structure. Resolve each reference and substitute the antecedent back into the sentence before moving on.
- **Reading comprehension and logical reasoning are not separate skills**: "RC and LR are not that different. There's some differences of course, but a lot of it is superficial." Getting better at LR improves RC.

## Teaching moves

- **Diagnostic-first consultation.** Joshua interrupted his own backstory to run a live question: "Do you mind if we go through a question together?" He gave it cold, camera off, no time pressure, explicitly de-risking it: "No pressure, by the way. I don't care if you get the question right."
- **Recall check before analysis.** Before touching the answer choices he asked her to restate the stimulus from memory "in like the simplest possible fashion." He then praised a merely adequate recall to build footing: "you'd be surprised, usually students will be like, 'uh, something about politics.'"
- **The shopping list analogy for active reading.** "If you have a shopping list and you go to the shopping mall and you have the list of things you want, when you go and the salesman gets his hands on you, you can say, 'Sorry sir, I'm good. I know what I want. It's right there.'" The answers live on the left side of the screen; the right side "is just finishing up the job you did in the stimulus."
- **Names the discipline "active reading" / "logical hostility"**: reading "with some sort of aggression," not passively.
- **Read-pause-rephrase loop.** The prescribed procedure: read, pause after a complete thought, rephrase without looking, in your own words "as though you're talking to your sister and telling her a story. Turn it into a language that you speak." He enforced it every line, cutting her off mid-sentence with "Pause."
- **Predict then validate.** On the word "normally" he stopped everything: "you want to pause and you want to foresee where the stimulus is going. You want to hypothesize where it's going to go and you want to validate with the new information." He led her to derive the exception herself rather than telling her.
- **Substituting antecedents out loud.** He made her identify what "this," "they," and "their" each pointed to, then re-read the sentence with the referent plugged in: "Now it's organized. We're not letting it just get chaotic. We're keeping it under our own palm."
- **Pushes from vagueness to procedure.** Repeated framing: "we want to get away from the vagueness and get into the specificity. That's where growth happens." Asks for "the steps," "the methodology," "one, two, three."
- **Softens being put on the spot.** "I'm putting you on the spot here, I know it's really hard to come up with an answer on the spot." When she had no answer he did not fill the silence with the answer; he reframed the question smaller: "I guess the question is, what is a conclusion?"
- **Diagnoses temperament, not just skill.** He named her second-guessing as the actual bottleneck and reframed it as a strength paired with a missing system: "you're actually mostly almost always right but you always have this hesitancy." The prescription: "having an operationalized system so you have guard rails... because you have the horsepower naturally." Confidence is an output of a repeatable process, not an input.
- **Calibrates aspiration upward.** She self-limited to 165; he pushed back with the median mechanic: "medians are not averages... you're either above, at, or below. If you get to that 167 mark, it's going to make a massive difference for your ability to get scholarships."
- **Uses his own low starting point as credential.** He started in the 130s, went 169 then 173, and volunteered that his obsessive platform-hopping was "actually very counterproductive. I would not recommend you do it. It's pretty embarrassing to be honest."

## Worked examples

- A main conclusion question about a political candidate (Epson) whose campaign ads were sent to too few households to influence popular opinion effectively. Bella answered C cold and correctly, but could not justify it.
  - Line 1 ("Normally political candidates send out campaign material to influence popular opinion"): flagged as the status quo setup, signaling an incoming exception.
  - Line 2 ("the recent ads for Epson's campaign were sent out to too few households to serve this purpose effectively"): "this purpose" resolved to influencing popular opinion. Ruled out as conclusion because it is presented as fact.
  - Line 3 ("The ads were evidently sent out to test their potential to influence popular opinion"): Bella independently spotted this as a hypothesis, which impressed him. "It's an experiment. He's dipping his feet in the water... He says the potential, trial run, right? Experimenting." Not empirically knowable (nobody can know Epson's intentions), so it is opinion.
  - Line 4 ("They covered a wide variety of topics and Epson's campaign has been spending heavily on follow-up to gauge their effect on recipients"): identified as support for line 3, via the question "why would they cover a wide variety of topics?"
  - Conclusion confirmed as line 3 by both tests: opinion, and explicitly supported. Joshua rated it roughly a level 4 or 5 difficulty question and noted "very few students will get this question right."
