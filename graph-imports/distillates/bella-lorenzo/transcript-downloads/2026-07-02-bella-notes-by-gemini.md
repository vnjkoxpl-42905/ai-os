## LSAT concepts

- **Argument**: defined as the relationship between premises and a conclusion. Nothing more.
- **Categorization of every line**: each line of a stimulus is premise, conclusion, opposing viewpoint, or neutral/background. The student must become a "categorizing machine" and ask of every word why the author included it.
- **Opposing viewpoint**: someone else's perspective, which the author typically introduces only in order to rebut.
- **Concession**: a nuance within an opposing viewpoint where the author grants the opposition a valid point before pushing back.
- **Neutral / background information**: provides context and relevance but does not support or oppose the argument.
- **Intermediate conclusion (sub-conclusion)**: a dual-function claim that receives support from a premise and supplies support to the main conclusion.
- **Premise indicators**: taught via the "FAPS" acronym, For / After all / Because / Since.
- **Conditional logic**: sufficient guarantees, necessary is required. Statements have a "head and tail"; if the sufficient condition (left of the arrow) is true, the necessary condition must be true.
- **Sufficient assumption**: framed as building the bridge between premise and conclusion.
- **Validity**: an argument is valid only when the evidence provides a 100% guarantee of the conclusion. Anything less is technically invalid.
- **Sufficient vs necessary flaw**: taught via the New York / United States analogy. The error is treating one route to an outcome as the only route.
- **Strengthen and weaken**: not separate methods. Both reduce to locating the gap between evidence and conclusion.
- **Pre-phrasing**: form the answer before reading the choices.
