# Ethan Trie, 2026-04-21

Keyword mapping and answer-choice discipline. The session where Ethan says he had been "approaching this test completely wrong."

## LSAT concepts

- **Categorizing statements.** The carryover skill from last session, restated as an identity: "You want to be a categorizing machine." Label each statement (generalization, opinion, fact, principle) and its function. Explicitly a both/and, not either/or, with content.
- **Four buckets for role questions.** Simplification of the full taxonomy: every statement is main conclusion, support (premise or intermediate), opposing (opposing viewpoint or concession), or neutral (background). All assessed in relation to the main conclusion.
- **Role / method questions.** Method: underline the statement named in the question stem, find the conclusion, locate the statement, then assess the statement's function *in relation to the conclusion*, never in isolation.
- **Keyword mapping.** The session's core technique. Abstract answer choices in flaw, role, method, and conclusion questions get decoded by extracting the abstract terms and substituting concrete stimulus content. "The stimulus should be a proof text for your answer choice."
- **Parts of speech as verification checklist.** "If we see a noun, we should be able to identify that thing in the stimulus. If we see an adverb, we should see the author doing that action in the stimulus. If we see an adjective, it should be descriptively accurate of the stimulus."
- **illustrate = example.** "Instantly replace it." Requires both an idea and an instance of the idea being exemplified.
- **general principle = a general rule, a rule of thumb.**
- **presuppose = a necessary condition,** "something the author is taking to be true prior to the argument starting."
- **explanation = causation.** "It means strictly causation on the LSAT. So explain means cause." Operational consequence: "If I see an answer choice that says explanation, I should right away see, did I identify a cause and effect in the stimulus? If I didn't, if I saw conditionals, that has nothing to do with this choice, just by that one word."
- **hypothesis = a proposed explanation,** therefore a proposed causation. Same cause-and-effect test applies.
- **indirect support = an intermediate conclusion.** "Whenever they say indirect support, they mean there's an intermediate conclusion." Test: if no intermediate conclusion exists in the argument, the answer cannot be correct.
- **Least wrong, not most right.** "We are always looking for the answer that is the least wrong. We're not looking for the answer that is the most correct... You're looking for an answer that cannot be disproven, not the answer that you can prove is right."

## Teaching moves

- **Pre-empted the score-fluctuation spiral before Ethan could ask about it.** Ethan opened by reporting he got more practice questions wrong than usual. Joshua's frame: "You don't ever want to allow yourself to be reflective of where you are based off of one day... be careful not to allow it to inflate how you're doing," and noted he gives the identical warning on good days. Ethan supplied the synthesis: "it's more like a fluctuation, but it should be fluctuating upwards rather than downward." Joshua: "the trend should be towards a direction."
- **Categorize instead of re-reading, his RC diagnosis.** A teaching insight he says he only got from teaching, not from taking the test: "students will read something four times, they don't understand it... after the third time you think you understand it, but what ends up happening is they didn't actually improve their understanding of what they read. If you didn't understand it the second time, you're probably not going to understand it the fifth time. But students get into a loop." The replacement move: "when you don't understand something, categorize it. Label it as something." Payoff: "You'll be astonished how little the content can matter at times, especially with RC." Cost framing: "they spent more time, more energy to spin their wheels... less return on your investment."
- **The geology example for content-independence.** "I'm not really into rocks, so when I read something about rocks I find it hard to work with. But when I'm able to categorize statements for their function, I can get past that."
- **Inspection mode with a written list.** For two competitive answers: "Get into inspection mode... make a list of all the potential downsides for each answer. Most students try to say this one does the job a bit better. Don't think of it from that perspective. Look for why both of them are bad, and write out a list." The rule that falls out: "when you are forced to write down a list, you'll never get the question wrong... the one with the shorter list of problems, that's the answer." Recommends a sticky note.
- **Named the specific texture of the trap.** "There'll be two answers. One answer will be kind of dull and general and vague, almost like it's not really saying much. And there'll be an answer that does address what we want, but the one that addresses what we want is wrong. That's the funny thing." Self-disclosure: "if I told you, I ripped my hair out at times. I'm like, this is just not a good answer, the right answer is not a good answer."
- **Ethan articulated the trap himself and Joshua stopped to mark it.** Ethan: "the one I vibe with I chose, and then it ended up being the other one... this one just seemed too broad to be true. But then it's the broadness that made it true." Joshua: "that exact explanation how you described it tells me you understand exactly what I'm referring to."
- **Every word counts, argued from legal liability.** Contrasted "eight out of ten people have benefited from product X" with "eight out of ten people are likely to have benefited." "There's only one word different. But this can get you a lawsuit." Tied to the TV-ad disclaimer read out fast at the end of a commercial. Punchline: "they are testing your ability to differentiate between what's basically right and what's right."
- **Never answer on vibes; skipping is allowed.** "I never ever want you to choose an answer because it's the best one... If you don't know the answer, just don't answer it. I'd rather you not answer the question, because I don't want you to have a false positive." His own habit as a student: "I would leave it for a day and say I'm just not going to choose an answer unless I can say with full confidence."
- **The accountability argument, the deepest move in the session.** "The biggest thing that I find students have issues with is accountability. Not intentionally, but if they don't allow themselves to say I chose this answer with full confidence, what ends up happening is you give yourself a way out... you trick yourself into believing that now you understand." The value of the alternative: "there is something monumentally important when you can say I was certain this is right and I got it wrong. That means there's a data point that is definitive." And: "it might help you in a moment to feel better about yourself, but nobody sees you. It's just you doing this practice by yourself, so who cares?" He calls it "ruthless self accountability," and told Ethan directly he can hold him to it "because you're a student that really has high expectations for themselves."
- **Mantras written ten times.** Mentioned as his practice: students write a mantra on paper ten times after repeating a mistake, as reinforcement.
- **Made Ethan invent the stimulus from the answer choice.** Rather than explaining "illustrate the general principle the argument presupposes," he asked Ethan to construct a stimulus in which that answer would be correct, then acknowledged the difficulty honestly: "it's hard to do what I'm asking you. It's really hard actually."
- **Worked answer choices before the stimulus, deliberately, on the second question.** "I know you're probably eager to jump into the questions, but I'm going to hold you off on the stimulus side. We're going to work backwards." Purpose: define what each answer would require to be correct before looking for evidence.
- **The lawyer-as-translator frame for why abstraction matters.** "A common man that comes to you is not going to understand stat 3.47, mens rea, these terms. Your job is to take the language that's very obtuse and make it obvious to the layman. That's why they would hire you, because they can't do it. So you need to translate the abstract language, turn it into concrete. So someone says, okay what does that mean? You're going to prison, buddy."
- **Told him the timeline honestly rather than encouraging speed.** "If you think you're going to need to bleed, this is going to be something you're not going to learn in a day. This is going to require you banging your head on a laptop ten times." And: "It's really challenging what I'm asking you to do. If you could do this, you get a 180."
- **Explicitly deprioritized the clock.** "Don't worry about time. Don't look at the clock. Don't even allow that to enter your mind for now. It's about perfecting the process first." Justification: "repeated conscious practice will make it subconscious. That's where you're training your intuition."
- **The trained-vibe resolution.** When Ethan noticed he had gone from vibes to deconstruction and wanted to return to vibes, Joshua validated the arc: "your first gut instinct before you train, you don't want to rely on that. But once you've trained your instinct, then you can say my vibe will direct me to the right answer because of the hundreds of hours of practice." Analogy: "you don't want to practice a free throw before you know the form."
- **Set an absolute standard for a question family.** "Flaw questions, method questions, role questions, you will never get them wrong... I will hold you to a standard." Ethan agreed on his own terms: "I cannot, I don't have the room for that." Joshua: "I almost say you don't have the right."
- **Told him the answer was correct before analyzing it, to remove suspense as a distraction.** "I don't want to discourage you, it's not D. It is D. I'm just pointing that out so we can go into this." Later, on the second question, the inverse: "I'm not saying you're right. Whenever I do this, it's not because I say you're right or wrong. I just always do this to be meticulous."

## Worked examples

**Constructed example for "used to illustrate the general principle the argument presupposes."** Joshua built it live: "Despite having a high-paying job, John was extremely stressed out and depressed" (the illustration/example), supporting the presupposed general principle that money is not everything, in service of the conclusion that "there are immaterial things in life that we must pursue to find true happiness." All three requirements must be met simultaneously: an example, a principle, and the principle being required for the conclusion to stand. Ethan then correctly reconstructed which sentence the question stem would be pointing at.

Supporting mini-examples: "all swans are white" as a generalization with "I went to the lake and saw a white swan" as its example; "punishment should be proportional to the crime committed" as a principle. Ethan offered Belgian Malinois dogs as his own attempt.

**Role question, psychological theories (level 5).** Stimulus: many popular psychological theories are poor theories, inelegant, and do not dispel the mystery around the psyche. However, therapeutically they tend to have greater successes than their more scientific rivals. Thus their inelegance is unimportant. Question stem: the role of the statement about relative therapeutic successes.

- Structure walked live: sentence 1 is opposing information, specifically a concession ("I'm conceding some ground, I'm saying yeah this is true... but that doesn't matter"). The therapeutic-success claim is support for the main conclusion, used to combat the concession.
- Ethan chose D: "an example of what the critic takes to be the most important aspect of scientific theories." Correct answer B: it is used to counter a consideration.
- The diagnosis, which Ethan produced himself: "I skipped over in D was 'most.' I read just 'important.'" Joshua confirmed the word alone kills it: "If the answer took away the word 'most,' that would have been okay... Does that mean it's the number one priority? Not necessarily. He never said that. We added to what the stimulus said."
- Joshua conceded D's pull: "I looked at it like, whoa, that looks very close. This is way better than B... but just one issue. The answer that's least wrong."

**Role question, gratitude (level 5).** Stimulus: the obligation to express gratitude cannot be fulfilled anonymously. However much society has changed over the centuries, human psychology is still driven by personal interaction. Thus the consequences for others can be served only if the benefactor knows the source of the gratitude. Question stem: the role of the claim that the obligation cannot be fulfilled anonymously.

- Answer choices were deconstructed first, blind to the stimulus:
  - (A) "an illustration of a premise": requires a premise, plus a separate statement giving a concrete instance of that premise, and the premise must support the main conclusion directly, not an intermediate one. Joshua's mini-example: "eating healthy is good for you, because it benefits your immune system" (premise), then an example of that premise in action. Ethan's phrasing, which Joshua adopted: "the premise being manifested into a real situation."
  - (B) "used to counter a consideration": Ethan recognized it as the correct answer to the previous question. "So they see how the language is, it's the abstract. When you de-abstractify, you're able to see it for what it is."
  - (C) indirect support: requires an intermediate conclusion to exist.
  - (D) identifies a social benefit: requires a social benefit to be identified and addressed by the argument.
  - (E) the main conclusion.
- Ethan then mapped the stimulus: the "however" sentence supports the "thus" sentence (an intermediate conclusion), which in turn supports the first sentence. Answer E, main conclusion.
- The trap Joshua flagged: "the bait a lot of students fall into is they see the word 'thus' and instantly think this last part is the conclusion, because you expect the conclusion to come after the evidence. But here they give us evidence and conclusion" in reverse order.
