# Ethan Trie, 2026-04-25

Practice-heavy session on two level 5 role questions. Ethan's breakthrough session: "I feel like a new person right now."

## LSAT concepts

- **Concession.** The session's central concept. Ethan's own definition, which Joshua adopted and reused: "It's giving somebody their flowers and saying you're wrong." Joshua's expansion: "I'm trying to meet the middle ground and say I get where you're coming from, then giving you my pushback and saying this is where we disagree." Two hard rules established: **a concession is never a premise and never a conclusion**, and "a concession and opposing viewpoint are always together."
- **Indicator words.** "Indicator words refer to argument parts." `although` introduces a **concession**, not a premise. Premise indicators memorized as an acronym: **FABS** = for, after all, because, since. "These are the most important indicator words you'll ever have. If you remember that acronym FABS, you'll be good to go."
- **Commas as structure markers.** "A comma tells you there's a complete thought." Two commas bracketing complete thoughts signal two separate premises. "Commas are your best friend."
- **but / yet / however as pivots.** "That 'but' always tells you he's clearing his throat." A but "almost always introduces a premise then conclusion, or conclusion then premise."
- **Least wrong, restated as falsification.** "We just need to look for where we can falsify each answer... you're looking for ways to poke holes in answers, and one of them you won't be able to." Ethan's formulation, which Joshua endorsed: "If you can't find anything wrong with the answer choice, that is the answer choice."
- **Disqualify, do not qualify.** "It's more like you're just looking to disqualify. You're not looking to qualify." One inaccurate word kills an answer regardless of the rest: "if there's even one word that's inaccurate, eliminate it."
- **"either" in answer choices means both.** Joshua's test case: "if I said to you, to get into this party you can't have a problem with either Jack or Mike," you have to be fine with both. Ethan had been reading it as exclusive or, which is why he had crossed out the correct answer.
- **especially.** Signals extra credit, not exclusivity. "Especially virtuous" does not mean the other group is less virtuous. The single word that cost Ethan the second question.
- **sufficient = enough.** Used to unpack "insufficient to justify."
- **indirect / justification language.** "A claim for which the argument attempts to provide justification" means the claim is receiving support, therefore is a conclusion.

## Teaching moves

- **"Never go to the next line."** Enforced on the first line of the first question: "We're never going to go to the next line. Even at the first comma, it's good to stop and rephrase every statement."
- **Bite-sized reading with the landmine metaphor,** his most developed version of the chunking rule: "Don't try to absorb the information at once. You want to see where the landmine is. When you step on the landmine, that answer choice gets eliminated. But if you just try to jump on it all at once, you won't see all the nuances. Read up to this point, stop, test it there. Read to the next point, reading it in relation to the last point." Rationale: "once you have other information, you'll try to qualify something that might not be true." Endstate: "you'll be like, I see, test, test. You're basically stepping on landmines, and once the landmine goes off you know the answer can't be right."
- **When you don't understand, find the exact point where comprehension stopped.** "Whenever you read something that's not tracking, you want to find precisely up until what point you do know, and work from there, taking piece by piece. Even if I only understand the first word of the stimulus, start there."
- **His single most reusable diagnostic: the problem is always in the stimulus.** "If you're having issues with the question, it's very likely that the stimulus is the problem... Once you know the stimulus like the back of your hand, the answer will be very easy." And: "when you don't see the answer, you should instantly say, I missed something when I was reading. Because the answers are always stupid... the questions are not hard questions. They're only hard because of something we missed. And 'basically the same' is the enemy of the LSAT." Then: "people worry about, oh, did I not do my little trick properly? No. It's almost always going to be a reading thing."
- **Named the answer's texture before Ethan could dismiss it.** On the correct but unglamorous answer C: "it's a weird one because it doesn't stand out, it doesn't even seem like it means much. But when you're looking at answer choices, you're looking to disqualify. If you can't tell me why an answer choice is wrong, then you cannot eliminate it." Ethan had crossed C out precisely because it seemed to say nothing.
- **Set the day's contract explicitly.** "Every answer choice that you choose, I should not be able to disqualify it. And if you don't know the answer, I actually don't mind if you say I don't know the answer." He announced the questions would all be hard: "I'm going to be really hard on you today."
- **Demanded written reasons, not feelings.** "I want you to write out why it's bad. I don't want you to tell me your feelings. I want you to tell me for this answer choice, what exactly is wrong." Notepad or sticky note.
- **Rhetorical-move narration per line.** Modeled it on the homelessness stimulus: line 1 is "the politician protecting himself, admitting a problem," line 3 is "using the newspaper as evidence to destroy the other side's belief." Purpose: "you don't need to get as lost in details."
- **Used a recovery/addiction analogy to unlock the virtue argument.** "If I can actually defeat the substance abuse disorder, I should not be diminished because I beat it... a recovering addict versus a person who's beat an addiction, they shouldn't be treated as though one is more virtuous than the other. We shouldn't say, oh, you already beat it, so you don't deserve any credit." Later reached for Buddhist and Hindu practices of extinguishing desire: "those people shouldn't get less credit because they've been able to eliminate the issue at the root."
- **Admitted the question was hard for him too.** "This actually required me to really pause, because usually I can just turn my head off and just do it... these are like the hardest questions you'll ever get." He worked the answer choices without having seen them first, deliberately: "I actually don't even know the answer. I'm going to look at the answers with you. I wanted to see it fresh with you."
- **Criticized the writing openly rather than defending the test.** "I'm getting anxiety just looking at it. This is so badly written. How can this be a full sentence?" Then converted it into the lesson: "this is why the LSAT is tricky, because it's so intentionally written badly."
- **The 170 barrier, framed as a knowable skill rather than talent.** "The tricky part is, it's one thing to develop that skill, but it's another to not even know that that's the skill in the first place. That's the difference maker." On students who quit: "I've had students say I've tried everything, I don't think it's for me. It's unfortunate. It's reasonable for you to think that, because there's no way you would have got there by guessing. You can do vibes up to a certain point, but to get to that higher barrier, this is the detail you want to look for."
- **Speed is a byproduct, and the gym analogy.** "Don't try to shortcut the machine to make the process fast. That's never the move." Training frame: "you grind your gears, you go to sleep, you wake up, you do it again, your brain will make neural networks and connections, and then you'll see it faster without even trying." The counterexample: "if you're just trying to get your reps really heavy, you're going to injure yourself and you're not going to get the returns on your investment." Then: "Once you know the system, we can lubricate the machine."
- **Explained his own first-session diagnostic.** "I remember the first question, I asked you, what did the stimulus say? The reason why I always test students the first time I meet them with that exact approach, where I surprise them: it gives me information, because I know where I need to meet you."
- **Praised the articulation, not the answer.** After Ethan distinguished countering "they are virtuous" from countering "they are the only ones who are virtuous": "You got it, my man. Beautiful. I love the way you explained that. That was perfectly articulated."
- **Noted Ethan's memory as a signal.** When Joshua accidentally opened last week's set, Ethan recognized the question instantly. "You have a good memory though. Holy. That's very impressive."

## Worked examples

**Role question, homelessness (level 5).** Stimulus: homelessness is a serious social problem, but throwing more government money at it is not the cure. The most cursory glance at the real estate section shows housing is readily available. Therefore anyone blaming this crisis on a lack of housing is wrong. Question stem: the role of "homelessness is a serious social problem."

- Structure: sentence 1 is a concession. "Government money is not the cure" is the intermediate conclusion. The real-estate observation is support. The final sentence is the main conclusion.
- Ethan chose B. Elimination worked answer by answer:
  - (B) "sets out a problem the argument is designed to resolve": the argument never offers a solution. Ethan: "He's just like an academic who talks about problems but never gives a solution."
  - (A) "suggests an alternative perspective to the one adopted in the argument": fails because the author *agrees* with the statement. It is not an alternative perspective at all.
  - (C) correct: "it is compatible with either accepting the conclusion that people are homeless because of a lack of housing is wrong, or with denying it." Ethan initially eliminated C by misreading "either" as exclusive. Once corrected: "Oh yes, this is describing a concession, because it's compatible with both arguments."
  - (D) does not summarize any position. (E) is not required to establish the conclusion; the conclusion needed support, and that statement is not it.
- Joshua's negation test for whether it was a premise: "If I told you that the lack of housing is not the reason why people don't have housing, and the evidence for that is because homelessness is a serious problem, that doesn't even make sense."
- Ethan's own retrospective: "I could have been eliminating way faster by doing that, rather than going, is it A or B, is it A or B. I should have just been looking at the weird words."

**Role question, virtue / ethicist (level 5).** Stimulus, one long sentence: it would be a mistake to say that just because someone is not inclined to do otherwise, he or she does not deserve praise for doing what is right, for although we do consider people especially virtuous if they successfully resist a desire to do wrong, they are certainly no less virtuous if they have succeeded in extinguishing all such desires. Question stem: the role of the claim about people being especially virtuous if they resist.

- Ethan was genuinely stuck. The blocker was one word: "especially," which he read as establishing a hierarchy in which the resisters rank above the extinguishers.
- Structure once unpacked: the first clause is the conclusion, "for" introduces the premise side, "although" introduces a concession, and the last clause is the actual premise. The commas mark two complete thoughts.
- The reading that unlocked it, in Ethan's words: "they accept that claim to be true, but then they are introducing a new fact that this is also true. But that doesn't prove that this isn't true."
- Answer B, correct: an observation that, according to the argument, is insufficient to justify a claim the argument concludes is false. Joshua's paraphrase: "you can't use that as a form of evidence to say that they don't deserve credit."
- (A) "a claim for which the argument attempts to provide justification": would make it a conclusion. It is not.
- (C) "primary obstacle": rejected because no primary obstacle is identified, and the author actually accepts that people can still be good in that sense.
- (D) says the commonly held belief is nevertheless false. Ethan's clean distinction: "We don't give a counter to the fact that they are virtuous. We give a counter to the fact that they are the only ones who are virtuous." Joshua noted this is exactly the trap an entry-level student falls into.
- (E) "an observation that according to the argument serves as evidence for the truth of the conclusion": Ethan killed it structurally rather than by feel. "That's already wrong. That's not what a concession is." Joshua: "Concessions are not premises. That's why, when we do the questions, categorizing makes your job on the other side way easier."
