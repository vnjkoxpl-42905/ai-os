Then straight into four level-four and level-five LR questions with no theory preamble: a Flaw (unproven vs proven false), a correlation/causation Flaw, a Strengthen, and a hard Must Be True / Flaw about Antarctic fossils that took roughly thirty minutes and needed an analogy to crack.

## LSAT concepts

- **Unproven versus proven false** — the session's named fallacy. Attacking a premise does not refute a conclusion. Ethan's articulation, which Joshua called perfect: "they haven't proved the conclusion to be wrong. They've proved the premise to be wrong, but not the conclusion." Joshua: "you can't use my inability to prove something to say that therefore it's false... my ability to argue something properly has nothing to do with the truth or falsity of a conclusion." Compressed rule: **"you can't assume bad evidence means a false conclusion."**
- **Structural requirements of an abstract answer choice** — an answer saying an argument "concludes that a person's statement is false" imposes checkable conditions on the stimulus. "If the author is concluding a person's [statement is] false, that means by definition there has to be somebody that they're disputing. There has to be two people." Then run a **two-part check**: is the conclusion accurately described, and are the required parties actually present?
- **"Merely on the grounds that"** — a load-bearing phrase. "Whenever they say merely on the grounds, that means whatever follows that has to adequately describe the evidence, the premise." So you read the answer, then look back at the stimulus and ask whether the description actually fits.
- **Correlation versus causation** — "a textbook correlation versus causation fallacy." Recognition cue: comparative-quantity phrasing. "When we see statements that say tend to, or the higher the blah blah blah, the higher that is," you are looking at a correlation, not a cause.
- **Answers written to look causal** — Ethan's own point: correlational statements are "written in a way that makes them seem causal. And I can't sit here and be like that is causal, because then I just get the question wrong."
- **Strengthen does not mean guarantee** — Ethan's formulation on the tornado question: an answer that "interacts with it in a way that just strengthens it but doesn't necessarily guarantee it" is exactly what a Strengthen question wants. Joshua: "it doesn't guarantee it by any means, but the likelihood increases if C is true."
- **Precision of scope in answer choices** — "the average tornado" is not "tornadoes." Ethan cut A on this: "if the statement had said the physical damage caused by tornadoes has remained roughly constant... I could see that being right. But the fact that they point to the average tornado is too specific of wording."
- **Conventional wisdom as a setup** — "whenever they tell us something was generally thought, we know they're probably going to push back." A "however, recently" line is the counter that puts the conventional claim in question.
- **"Must" as absolute language** — "that means that there can't be any possibility. If there's even 1% chance... if there's a world where you can conceive [otherwise], this argument's conclusion [fails] entirely." The author "came to a definitive, absolute explanation to solve a mystery."
- **The fabs (premise indicators)** — for, after all, because, since. "If we see any of these four words, it has to be a premise attached to it, with a conclusion that was said prior to it." Ethan misidentified "after all" as a conclusion indicator and was corrected: "so close, it's a premise indicator."
- **Could-to-must is never valid** — the golden rule of the session. "We know that we can never ever take a could statement and get to a must statement. That will never be possible... remember that as a golden rule." Joshua pairs it with the is/ought rule: "you can never take a statement of fact and come to an ought statement. You can't get from an is to an ought."
- **More than one possible solution treated as though only one is possible** — the flaw name that answer E carried, and the shape of the whole Antarctic argument.

## Teaching moves

- **Opens with a question, not theory, based on a read of this specific student.** "I was going to start off just straight up with a question... because I find that you especially, I noticed that whenever we do questions, something turns on more than when I just do theory. But like using that to get you to engage better, to get you to really turn those dials on."
- **Starts at level four and five deliberately, on day one back.** "We're going to start off like straight out the gates running."
- **Asks for the process and the confidence level, not just the answer.** "Walk me through this argument. Tell me exactly how you approached it, what you thought about it, and your level of confidence overall too."
- **Withholds right/wrong until the student has committed to reasoning.** "Before I tell you yes or no, what did you notice about the stimulus?"
- **Names the fallacy after the student has described it, not before.** Ethan articulated the unproven/proven-false flaw in his own words; only then did Joshua ask "do you know the name of that?" and supply it. The sequence is: student describes, then gets the label.
- **Distinguishes knowing a fallacy from understanding it.** "A lot of the fallacies people kind of know them, but they don't understand them at a rational level, intuitively understanding, which is [what] you described perfectly."
- **The brunettes-and-Canada example for unproven vs proven false.** "If I said Canada is the greatest country in the world because we have a lot of brunettes... my argument could be bad, but that doesn't mean my conclusion is wrong." Ethan then extended it himself, and Joshua escalated to the theological version: "if I can't prove God exists, that's not an argument to say that God doesn't. It just means that you can't prove it. Those are two different claims."
- **Frames deep analysis as efficiency training, not answer-getting.** "It's not like you have to be able to notice this every time you get the answer right or wrong, but it trains you to be able to see it. So whenever you get answers you know are wrong or right, you know exactly how to pick it apart, which gives you the ability to do it efficiently."
- **Escalates and says so.** "I'm going to up the ante here. We're going to go to level five questions." And when Ethan asked whether a question was a three: "no, this is a four. You're making it look like a three, though."
- **Praises the diagnosis, not the score.** "Not just getting it right, because sometimes you can get answers correct through process of elimination... but you knew why it was right, which is a very good sign."
- **Walks a lost student claim by claim rather than re-explaining the whole stimulus.** When Ethan said "I'm not sure if I'm understanding the premise correctly," Joshua's response was "we're going to go claim by claim here. The first claim, give me an understanding [of] what it's saying."
- **Asks why the author included a fact, rather than what the fact says.** Repeatedly: "they're saying it's under the ice sheets. Why are they telling us that?" This is what finally unlocked the question. He kept returning to it through Ethan's confusion and an interruption, without changing the question.
- **Lets the student produce the key inference.** "If it's buried under the ice, what does that mean about the fossils and their age?" Ethan: "It's older than the ice." That single exchange resolved a ten-minute impasse.
- **Refuses to accept a right answer reached by elimination.** After Ethan landed on E, Joshua checked: "I really hope that, do you understand specifically why the argument is problematic?" Ethan: "No, I kind of just did it by process of elimination." Joshua then taught the analogy rather than moving on. This is the pivotal move of the session.
- **Teaches analogy as a transferable skill, explicitly.** "Another valuable skill is using an analogy. So when you don't understand the content, replacing the content with something more intuitive is a very useful skill... it can force you to take yourself away from focusing on Antarctica ice, which is just super out there."
- **The sprained-ankle analogy.** "If I told you the star runner just finished dead last, and I said therefore he must have severely sprained his ankle during the race, and my support was: after all, a severe ankle sprain could have definitely caused someone to run that slowly. What would you tell me about that argument?" Ethan got it instantly, then Joshua drove it home: "Did he have to sprain his ankle? Of course not. What if his shoe fell off?"
- **Compresses the lesson into a portable rule at the end**, so the analogy does not have to be re-derived: could never yields must, paired with is/ought.
- **Protects the student's non-LSAT priorities without dropping the thread.** "I've been waiting for you to give me the green light because I know you have your school stuff... I'm obviously understanding that's priority, so I'm not going to push there, but to get into grind mode with regards to the LSAT it's going to be really important to tone that up soon."
- **Closes on the human note.** "Enjoy your graduation week. These moments are important, and we'll recap it from there."
- **Confidence language that cites a track record rather than flattering.** "I've yet to see someone I've worked with that was determined to get a certain score and they didn't get it. If they're determined, they'll get it."

## Worked examples

- **LR, Flaw (level 4) — Kramer's book and the coal companies.** Kramer's main contention is that coal companies are to blame for the region's economic difficulties, argued on the grounds that coal companies do not make significant contributions to other local industries. The editorial responds that coal companies do invest heavily and indirectly, particularly in roads and manufacturing, and concludes Kramer's contention is false. Ethan answered **E** immediately and correctly: the editorial "concludes that a person's statement is false [merely] on the inadequacy of an argument." Structural check they ran together: E requires two opposing parties (present: the editorial and Kramer), requires the argument to conclude the other party is *false* rather than *unproven* (it does, which is the error), and the text after "merely on the grounds" must accurately describe the evidence. Rejected choices were killed by the same method: one described concluding that "one party is not to blame for a particular outcome," which fails because there is no second party in that relationship; another said "concludes that a statement is false merely on the ground that, if accepted, it was true," which Ethan cut on the spot: "why would we care if we accept that as true? That's not relevant to our argument."
- **LR, Flaw (level 4) — protein, blood pressure, disease X.** Higher levels of a protein are correlated with higher blood pressure; disease X causes a rise in that protein; therefore disease X must cause higher blood pressure. Ethan answered **C** in well under the allotted time: the argument "implicitly infers, solely on the basis of two phenomena being correlated, that one causally contributes to the other." He identified the correlation from the comparative phrasing and produced the **ice cream sales and shark attacks** analogy unprompted.
- **LR, Strengthen (level 5) — reported tornadoes.** The number of tornadoes reported annually has roughly doubled since the 1950s, but the actual number has not increased; the increase in reports is due to improved ability to detect them. Ethan answered **C** correctly and fast. His reasoning: large and medium-sized tornadoes are the ones we could always see, so if their reported number has stayed constant, the extra reports must be smaller or previously-missed storms, which is exactly the author's story. He explicitly noted it does not guarantee the conclusion (the small ones could genuinely have increased) but does raise its likelihood. Eliminations: **A** ("the average tornado") is about damage, not detection, and is scoped to the average rather than the total; **B** (tornadoes hitting major population centers annually has doubled) actively weakens, "quite literally telling us the opposite thing we want to hear"; **D** (annual deaths) irrelevant; **E** (geographic range) not what the argument is about.
- **LR, Flaw (level 5) — Antarctic ice sheets and ocean-floor fossils.** Antarctica was conventionally thought to have been covered by ice for 14 million years. However, 3-million-year-old fossils of a kind previously found only in ocean-floor sediment were discovered under the ice sheet covering central Antarctica. Therefore the Antarctic ice sheets must temporarily have melted about 3 million years ago; after all, either severe climatic warming or volcanic activity in Antarctica's mountains could have melted the ice sheets, raising sea levels and submerging the continent. Ethan first answered **B**, was genuinely lost, and reached **E** (correct) by elimination before understanding it.
  - The blocker was not the logic but the setup: he could not see why the fossils' location mattered. Resolved by Joshua's question about burial depth and Ethan's own inference that the fossils must be older than the ice, which is what puts the 14-million-year claim in question.
  - The flaw: the last line offers two things that **could** have melted the ice, and the conclusion says the melting **must** have happened. Ethan's final statement of it: "we made this seem like it's guaranteed, and then afterward he's like, oh this could have happened, this could have happened."
  - Alternative explanations he generated once he saw it: "what if somebody just dug a hole in there really deep and then just put it there?" and "what if that animal just kind of buried itself in there? Maybe that's why it's one of a kind." Joshua's version: "the author is assuming there's obviously no other way an ocean fossil could end up under the ice."
  - **B** (that two things could independently produce an effect is taken to show they could not have operated in conjunction) — rejected because the argument says nothing at all about the two causes acting together: "they don't disprove that, nor are they in support of that. They just never said a single thing about B."
  - **C** (that a position is widely believed is taken as showing it is the only position) — the only widely-believed claim is the first sentence, and that is not what the argument does with it.
  - **D** (establishing that an event occurred is confused with having established its cause) — Ethan tested it against the text and rejected it, though he could not fully articulate why: "that's just kind of not the flaw. I don't know how to describe it."
  - Another choice was cut for being about general applicability, which is not at issue.
  - **E** (an inconsistency: more than one possible solution is treated as though only one solution is possible) — correct.
  - Ethan's own closing compression: "I could win the lottery tomorrow, therefore I must win the lottery tomorrow. Not true."
  - His retrospective on his own error is worth keeping: "had I just stopped reading at the conclusion, I would have gotten E immediately. But the last bit, I was thinking about their relationship, and I was like, oh, these two actually do end up causing this together."
