# Ethan Trie, 2026-06-28

A two-hour session that opens on LSAT accommodations (Ethan has none; the August deadline had passed three days earlier) and then works four LR questions. The last one, a sufficient assumption about ecological problems, consumed roughly an hour and exposed the real finding: Ethan had never been taught that "few A are B" entails "most A are not B," which made the question unsolvable for him. Joshua's diagnosis, in his own words: "it's not the question being challenging, it's actually just one specific thing."

## LSAT concepts

- **Quantifier ranges** — the session's core content. **Some** = 1 to 100% ("some just means at least one"). **Most** = 51 to 100%. **Few** = 1 to 50% ("technically 50% is not allowed" for most). Joshua flags that "some" is the counterintuitive one: "if I were to tell you you can have some of the cookies in the cookie jar and there's five cookies, technically that means you can have five of them, which is very bizarre. I know it's counterintuitive, but it's just the way that it is."
- **Few A are B = most A are not B** — the rule that unlocked the whole question. "Few A's are B's means the exact same thing as saying most A's are not B's. They're interchangeable ideas." Illustration: "if I said few people will be successful, that's the same thing as saying most people will not be successful."
- **Few and most are distinct categories, not overlapping** — Ethan's misconception was that few might be *included* in most. Joshua: for LSAT purposes they must be treated as distinct. Ethan's own summary: "now that I know most and few are to be distinct from each other in terms of this conditional, that just changed the whole chain of ideas for me."
- **Negations of quantifiers** — negating **some** gives **all are not**. "Some are not" means at least one is not, and is compatible with *all* not being: "if I told you some people are not going to make it into law school, that could also mean that all people are not going to make it to law school, which is very unintuitive."
- **Few is harder to prove than some** — a scoring consequence for answer-choice selection. "Some includes anything between 1 to 100, and few doesn't include anything above 51%. So few is substantially more hard to prove than some." Students treat them as similar and mis-rank answer strength as a result.
- **"Only" introduces a necessary condition; "the only" introduces a sufficient condition.** "Every time you see only outside of the word 'the only' it's necessary. If you see 'the only' it's sufficient." Ethan had been burned by this before: "that's what I got tripped up on the other day."
- **"As a result" is a conclusion indicator, almost always.**
- **Repeating concepts across two conditionals mean a chain.** "If you see repeating concepts in the evidence, that means you can chain the evidence." Joshua: "you should be a shark for repeating concepts."
- **Head and tail of the dragon** — the point of a chain is the endpoints, never the middle. "They want to see if you identified the beginning of the chain and the end of the chain. It's never going to be anything to do with the middle of the chain." They never build chains for fun.
- **Necessary is not guarantee** — the second big correction of the session. "If I said to you most of your problems will not be solved unless this one thing happens, that doesn't mean if that one thing happens your problems will be solved. It just tells you that's a requirement. It's not a guarantee."
- **Flaw-question stems are interchangeable formulas.** "Ignores the possibility," "fails to address," "fails to consider" all mean the same thing: if this alternative were true, would it destroy the argument? Likewise **"takes for granted"** and **"presumes without providing justification"** both mean **assumption**, and specifically a **necessary assumption**.
- **An assumption is an unstated premise** — "something the author is using to be true in the argument, taking that piece of information to be true without explicitly saying it. So it's almost a premise that's not stated."
- **The negation test** — how to attack a necessary assumption. Ethan supplied it: "see if this argument still makes sense if that thing wasn't true. So, negating it."
- **Asserting versus establishing** — the distinction Ethan drew unaided on the ACME question. "He's assuming it true. He's asserting it. He's not establishing it." An author who reports what experts "have thus clearly recognized" has not shown the experts said any such thing.
- **Contrapositive** — Ethan worked this live: economically enticing is necessary for non-government problems, so not economically enticing guarantees it is a government issue.
- **Sufficient assumption** — "we just need to guarantee the outcome that they're saying" (Ethan's own definition, endorsed).

## Worked examples

- **LR, Flaw (level 5) — ACME retirement plan.** An advertisement notes that for the past 25 years, winners of the nation's most prestigious award for economists have used the ACME retirement plan, and that these economists have thus clearly recognized that ACME offers a financially secure future; therefore it is probably a good plan for anyone with retirement needs similar to the prize winners'. Ethan answered **D** in time and correctly. D is a "takes for granted" choice, so it is a necessary assumption, tested by negation: the argument rests on the economists having *chosen* ACME because it is good. Joshua: "if the universities just forced them to use it, then the whole idea that they clearly recognized it kind of falls apart." Ethan's instinct at first read was exactly that: "did they choose it or did the ACME thing choose it for them?"
  - **A and B** — "ignores the possibility" choices. Negated, neither destroys the argument: "it could still be a good plan had that been true."
  - **C** (appeals to the fact that supposed experts have endorsed the argument's main conclusion, rather than appealing to direct evidence) — the popular wrong answer (Ethan checked the platform stats: "C was very popular"). Ethan's rejection, which he worked out live and struggled to articulate: the prize winners never endorsed the *conclusion*. "The author makes the assumption for them, thus clearly recognized, but the economist never says that... they're treating it like a fact rather than the fact that he's just making an argument." He then found 7Sage's phrasing which matched: "the author does not state or assume the prize winners endorsed that specific conclusion."
  - **E** — the tempting one. It hinged on **identical** versus **similar** needs. Ethan: "what threw me off was the fact that the plans are identical. Does it matter that their needs are identical? No. It just needs to be a good plan. A good plan does not entail being identical to the need of the consumer." Joshua flagged this as the whole difficulty of the question: "the conclusion specifically says the plan is good for people with similar needs, not identical needs, which is such a small nuance."
  - Joshua's assessment: "this is one of the hardest flaw questions I think I've seen, and one I've done many times. I'm surprised you got it with such ease."
- **LR, Paradox / math (level unstated) — grocery store sales.** Shoppers who bought sale items spent far more than shoppers who bought comparable numbers of non-sale items. Ethan had seen this question before and remembered getting it wrong, but wanted to redo it. Correct answer **C**: only expensive items go on sale. "If it was $200 and they bought the same amount of items and it gets reduced to 100, boom. Compared to green onions, which is maybe $2." Joshua's version: "if the only things on sale are $100 steaks, shoppers are paying $50 an item. If the full-price shoppers are buying $2 loaves of bread, they pay $2 per item." Eliminations: an answer about shoppers using a list versus a cart (irrelevant), an answer about how many items were on sale (irrelevant), and an answer about the study's stores carrying expensive items that other stores do not (irrelevant, because "we just need to analyze it inside that grocery store"). Ethan identified his original error on re-reading: "what I missed on this question was **comparable numbers of items**." Joshua ran this specifically as a probe: "I just wanted to see how you felt about the mathy style questions... you seem like you did that flawlessly." Ethan: "I feel like I rarely get the math ones wrong."
- **LR, Sufficient Assumption (the hour-long one) — ecological problems, consumer habits, government mismanagement.** Stimulus: the solution to any environmental problem that is not the result of government mismanagement can only lie in major changes in consumer habits; but major changes in consumer habits will occur only if such changes are economically enticing; as a result, few serious ecological problems will be solved unless the solutions are made economically enticing. Correct answer **A**: few serious ecological problems are the result of government mismanagement.
  - **Structure work**: two "only" conditionals plus "as a result" tells you the shape before reading for content. Repeating concept = consumer habits, so the premises chain: not-government-mismanagement → major consumer habit changes → economically enticing. Head and tail: non-government environmental problems → economically enticing.
  - **The gap**: the evidence is about environmental problems *not caused by government mismanagement*; the conclusion is about *serious ecological problems*. Those two groups must be bridged.
  - **The bridge**: the author must assume most serious ecological problems fall into the non-government group. Answer A says few serious ecological problems are the result of government mismanagement, which by the few/most rule means most serious ecological problems are *not* the result of government mismanagement, which puts them on the non-government train that leads to economically enticing.
  - **Why Ethan could not get there**: he narrowed correctly to A and E ("I was right on the dot when I was saying it's between A or E, because it needs to be about serious ecological problems") and then eliminated A because he could not license the inference. "I was not sure if few of these things could overlap with most things... do you maybe include it in most? That was what was mathematically making sense to me in my head." He stayed on E throughout.
  - **The conclusion itself was the second blocker.** He said flatly, "I don't think I'm understanding this at all. The chain I'm fine doing. It's just the conclusion makes no sense to me. I don't understand what it's trying to say at all." Two things resolved it: translating few→most ("most serious ecological problems need economic incentives if they are to be solved"), and separating requirement from guarantee, which Ethan himself surfaced as a question: "I'm confused as to, if the solutions are made enticing, how does that guarantee that most serious [problems get solved]?" Joshua: "it doesn't. That's the problem... it's required, not a guarantee."
  - **He derived the contrapositive correctly on his own** midway through: "not economically enticing guarantees that something is a government issue," and self-corrected a sufficient/necessary flip in real time ("I am flipping the sufficient necessary right now, but I am understanding it right now").
  - **The moment it landed**: "if few of the problems that are ecological are due to government, what does that mean about the majority of the ecological problems?" Ethan: "They are not government issues. Therefore it guarantees enticing." Joshua: "Yes. And that's the answer."
  - Ethan's own timing estimate for a version of himself who knew the rule: "it would have been 30 seconds I took on that question, but we took an hour."
