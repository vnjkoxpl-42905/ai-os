# Ethan Trie, 2026-04-11

Foundations session. Joshua laid out his full LR framework before working a single question. High density of reusable framings.

## LSAT concepts

- **The five habits/skills of LR.** Joshua's organizing claim: "These are the five habits, the five skills that you want to develop with proficiency. And if you ever make a mistake on a question, it's because one of these five things you don't have a strong grasp for." Covered explicitly this session: structure detection, method of reasoning, assumption.
- **Argument structure.** Step one on every question: identify the author's premises and conclusion. Premise = starting point / checkpoint, intermediate conclusion = "the layover," main conclusion = "the end destination."
- **Method of reasoning.** Step two, and distinct from structure: "The method of reasoning is asking you what form of transportation you're taking from each checkpoint. Are you taking a car, a flight, a train? You can get to each checkpoint theoretically different ways. I could advance the same argument and the same conclusion appealing to different forms of reasoning."
- **Conditional, causation, comparison/analogy.** The three most common forms. "More than 50% will fall into the first two." Joshua flagged the terminology equivalence Ethan had already discovered on his own: "argument by analogy is another way of saying argument by comparison." Joshua admitted this took him a long time to see himself.
- **Assumption.** "An assumption ultimately is a premise. Unsaid premise. Unstated, implicit premise." Key corollary: "Not every argument has an assumption. Any valid argument has zero assumptions."
- **Necessary vs. sufficient assumption.** Necessary: "the author must have been thinking of this when he was making the argument, but he didn't say it out loud... it needs to be there or else that argument cannot exist." Sufficient: "what missing premise could I add to make this argument perfect?" Intuition anchor: "when someone uses the word assumption in everyday language, they're more so thinking of necessary assumption."
- **Validity.** "The evidence should be an absolute guarantee. So long as the evidence is true, the conclusion has to be true." Not likelihood, not probability. Validity is binary: "It's either 100% valid or 0%. There's no such thing as 99% valid. 99% is equal to zero."
- **Unjustified is not false.** "The argument is unjustified doesn't mean the other side is wrong. It just hasn't been given enough evidence to say one way or the other." Joshua called this the nuance students most often lack, and told Ethan directly that most struggle with it.
- **New concepts in the conclusion.** "Any valid conclusion is just a baby of the evidence, a restatement of the evidence." Any idea, concept, or especially wording in the conclusion that is absent from the evidence marks where the assumption lives. Stated formally: "the land of validity lives upon the intersection of the premises."
- **Main conclusion.** Two tests given: it must be supported by other claims, and it "has to be an opinionated statement. Cannot be a fact." Also: correct answers are usually not verbatim but "a meaning match that the author is trying to convey."
- **Statement types: facts, opinions, generalizations, principles.** Facts "describe the world as it is, not as it ought to be" and "can only be premise or background information... never arguments by themselves." Opinions and decisions (marked by should/ought) are the likely conclusions but can also serve as premises.

## Teaching moves

- **The house metaphor for statement types.** "There's argument parts, so these are like the pieces in the house. Premise and conclusion would be like the walls of your house, the roof would be like a conclusion. But the substance of the wall is not going to be made out of feathers." Payoff: substance predicts role. "If I see a piece of wood, I know that's not going to be your pillow. By process of elimination it could only be maybe your wall, could be your table."
- **The Toronto/Mississauga flight, his signature gap metaphor.** Drew LA to NYC to Toronto as checkpoints with a visible space before Toronto. "The argument will present it as though the flight from NYC will take you to Toronto. But there's a space. That means the flight will take us to this point." Then made it visceral: "I guarantee you if I dropped you off in Mississauga today, it's only one hour out, and I said hey, it's basically the same. You'd be in a very bad place... you're not from Canada, so you'd have nowhere to go." Punchline: **"'Basically the same' is the enemy of the LSAT."**
- **The Canadian courtroom, for why premises are granted.** "If you came to Canada as an international lawyer and told the court, no no, in California that's not the law, what do you think they would say?" Two options: "you could come on horseback and take Canada," or "play by the rules that they give you and beat them at their own game." Formalized: "think of every stimulus as its own municipality, its own code of law, but the conclusion is the case we're fighting for."
- **"Full-time validity checkers."** His name for the tester's job. Followed by the spoiler: "none of the conclusions are valid on the LSAT. None of them. Spoiler alert, all of them are invalid."
- **"Logical hostility."** The reading posture he wants: "we are reading every stimulus with aggression, we're reading it with hostility... you want to read it understanding, why are you lying? What are you lying about? What are you hiding here?" The payoff skill: "When you can identify why the conclusion is not guaranteed, you can think of a hypothetical situation, a what if, that is the most valuable asset on the test."
- **Structural engineer, not philosopher.** "We want to take away your mindset from being like a social science student or a philosopher. You want to think architectural engineer, structural engineer." Reinforced later mid-question: "Structural engineer. Remember that."
- **Validity as verbal mathematics.** "It's essentially a verbal expression of mathematics. If I added the evidence together, does that equal the conclusion?"
- **Held the student accountable to his own earlier definition.** When Ethan offered "the sky is blue" as an argument, Joshua did not correct it directly: "I'm just going to hold you accountable to what you said earlier. I asked you what is an argument. What did you tell me it was?" Ethan: "premise and conclusion." Joshua: "So what you gave me is one of the two."
- **Named the difference between intuition and mastery.** "You have a good feel toward these ideas, I guarantee you do. The only thing is these types of concepts get exposed at the highest level questions. You can get away with just an intuitive feel, but when you get tested at the highest level, what ends up happening is..." This is his recurring justification for drilling definitions a strong student already half-knows.
- **"Distill it to me" / the handyman client.** Rephrasing rule enforced live: "We're never going to read a claim without rephrasing it as simple as possible. We need to turn it into natural English. I'm hiring you as my lawyer, I'm a handyman, and I don't want to know the fancy legalese. So teach it to me as you read it."
- **Conclusion as manhunt.** "We're on a manhunt for the conclusion. The conclusion is a north star. Every claim we should ask ourselves, is this a contender for the conclusion?" Applied from the first sentence onward.
- **Attack every argument regardless of question type.** "Whether it's a strengthening question, a weakening question, a main conclusion question, you should be attacking every argument. Why does the evidence not lead to the conclusion? That alone will solve most of your questions."
- **Deliberately picked a level 5 question early, then pre-empted discouragement.** "You know what, screw it, let's do it, there's no reason not to." After: "I won't lie to you, I chose a very difficult question. So don't feel discouraged... I threw you in the fire here, but it's good to feel it."
- **Diagnosed why the hard question was hard.** "It wasn't because I brought a new piece of information that was a new argument part we never heard. It's just the way that they relate to each other... when we stick to our fundamentals you can work through it, because we need to not just read it through vibes."
- **Refused to advance until structure is automatic.** "We're not going to budge until we get this mastered, because once you're able to do this, every other skill on top of this becomes way easier... it should be impossible for you to get it wrong. We want to make it impossible for you to fail."
- **"The LSAT wants you to play dumb."** On autofilling: "if somebody said that in conversation, you would think, okay, that means the doctor told me to do that. But the LSAT wants you to play dumb. You didn't say it. That's the game... you have to break that habit of connecting the dots for them. It's a habit more than anything."
- **Deferred deliberately and said so.** Repeatedly capped scope rather than chasing tangents: "I'll be careful not to open this too much, that's going to be on its own a huge thing," and on Ethan's correct-but-early necessary-assumption point, "you got it right, but be careful with that, that's a can of worms."
- **Praised precision specifically, not generally.** On Ethan catching from vs. in: "Very sharp. Very few people catch that argument. I use this a lot of times. They'll fall for it, because the game is being diligent, and you caught it very quickly."

## Worked examples

**Toy arguments used to build validity intuition:**
- "All dogs are fluffy. All fluffy things are cute. Therefore all dogs are cute." Ethan's own construction, used to probe what an assumption is.
- "Jack is from NYC. NYC is in the USA. Therefore Jack is in the USA." Ethan caught it immediately: from and in "are two very different verbiage." Joshua: "You can be from somewhere but not in there." Ethan supplied the disproof: "Jack could be in Canada for all we know." Joshua used this as the model of the target skill: attacking the conclusion without disputing the evidence.
- Ethan then inverted it himself: premise "Jack is in the USA," conclusion "Jack is from the USA," and identified that the assumption would be that NYC is in the USA. Joshua confirmed while flagging the necessary/sufficient distinction as premature.
- Sun arguments, Ethan's construction: "the sun has always risen, therefore it will rise tomorrow" is invalid; "the sun will always rise every day, therefore the sun will rise tomorrow" is valid even though the conclusion may not be true in fact. Later refined to the should/will distinction: "if I say the sun should rise tomorrow, it does not mean the sun will rise tomorrow."
- Doctor argument: "we should do what the doctor tells us, therefore you should take your medicine tomorrow." Ethan identified two separable necessary assumptions: that a doctor (not a family member) prescribed it, and that tomorrow is when it was prescribed. Joshua confirmed either could be the credited answer.
- Ethan's own probe about disconnected truth: "I like cheese, therefore 1 plus 1 is two." Joshua: "you tapped into something very big there," and used it to separate validity from actual truth.

**Main conclusion question, Mars (Joshua's rating: level 5 of 5).** Stimulus: it is probably within reach of human technology to make the climate of Mars inhabitable; it might be several centuries before people could live there; some of the world's great temples and cathedrals took centuries to build; research efforts now are justified if there is even a chance of making another planet inhabitable; besides the intellectual exercise, understanding how the Martian atmosphere might be changed could help in understanding atmospheric change inadvertently triggered by human activity on Earth.

- Ethan answered in well under the 2.5 minutes allotted, then was made to reconstruct it aloud.
- Sentence-by-sentence: (1) human technology can probably make Mars livable, an opinion, therefore a conclusion contender. (2) it might take a long time, a separate point rather than support. (3) the cathedrals line answers the delay objection: "that's not a problem, because even the cathedrals took forever." (4) the "if" clause, which Joshua unpacked: "Think of 'if' as introducing a hypothetical. It's not saying it is, they're not saying it isn't." (5) the Earth-atmosphere benefit, which also supports the research.
- Ethan initially treated the first and last sentences as the conclusion. Working the relations produced the correct read: statements 1 and 5 both support "research efforts aiming at making Mars inhabitable are justified," which is the main conclusion.
- Correct answer D, which Ethan noted "is not the same wording and it's not even the same statement." That became the lesson on meaning match over verbatim quote.
