# David Salgado, 2026-05-17

A randomized high-pressure LR drill session at 1:45 per question, built around a diagnosis that David's LR has slipped while his RC has climbed to minus two. Two questions worked (a Flaw about speed limits, a Sufficient Assumption about skilled artists), plus a short RC vocabulary question about "reflects." ## LSAT concepts

- **Reduce / increase as causal flags** — the session's headline shortcut. "When you read a stimulus and you see the word reducing... it's causation. Having an effect, to be able to reduce something is always going to do with causation, because that something has an influence on something else." Then: "every time you see reduce, instantly know that they're flirting with causation."
- **Denying causation is as flawed as asserting it** — "it's interesting, they're not saying that something causes something. They're saying something equally is bad, saying that something does not cause something, which is equally invalid. Saying no causation is as bad as saying there is causation."
- **Sufficient assumption** — "we are looking to just find an answer choice that would guarantee this outcome." Joshua's diagnostic question, asked before touching the choices: "was there anything that was included in the conclusion but not in the evidence?"
- **Concept, not word** — the refinement David reached under prompting. The gap is not always a new *word*; here every word appeared in the stimulus and the gap was a missing *link* between concepts. Joshua: "not word. We don't care about. You're right. You pointed out something important... conceptual concept."
- **Negative-to-positive translation (quantity statements)** — the extended teaching point. "Not all A's are B's" does **not** imply "some A's are B's." "If I say not all skilled artists are famous, that means that some skilled artists are not famous. But whenever we see a negative formulation we want to perform a negative to positive translation." And: "at least one isn't doesn't mean there's at least one that is. We don't want to take the inverse of that." The evidence therefore says nothing at all about famous people, only about not-famous people.
- **The missing-term cheat code** — since the evidence never mentions famous people at all, "the answer choice has to include famous." Joshua explicitly frames this as an efficiency shortcut, not a requirement: "you will get the right answer, but you pointed out E's the only one that has that famous in it."
- **Chain shape versus term tracking** — two valid routes to the same answer. David solved by shape ("you just make a triangle. Just say most A's are C's"); Joshua endorsed it fully ("that's beautiful work... I would never say not to do that") while adding term-tracking as a second angle.
- **Flaw versus Weaken** — David self-corrects mid-analysis: "this is not a flaw. We have to connect the flaw, not a weaken per se." Answer choices that merely attack the argument without naming its reasoning error are out.
- **Never pick an answer that helps the argument** — on the speed-limit question, choice A (motorists ignore speed limits) actually *strengthens*: "if drivers are just going to ignore this new speed limit anyways, then reducing the speed limit definitely isn't going to save lives or the environment... you never want to pick an answer that would benefit the argument."
- **"Only if" as over-strong flaw language** — David rejected E on the grounds that the author never claimed collision risk arises *only* from time on the road: "the only if is a bit strong... he doesn't agree that drivers run significant risk only if they spend a lot of time. We don't have that, and I feel like that's stretching that flaw."
- **Two-pronged conclusions and where the answer hides** — David's own observation, elevated by Joshua. When a conclusion has two prongs (here: saves lives / protects the environment), the tested flaw tends to sit in the prong you would *not* naturally attack. "The one that you naturally probably wouldn't naturally come to is probably going to be the one they'll lead on to more... they know where people are going to be looking, because they tested this a million times prior to giving it out."
- **"Reflects" is context-dependent, not inherently causal** — "the word reflect doesn't actually have any sort of necessity for it to be regarding anything to do with causation... it completely depends on the context." Contrast: "your score reflects your hard work" (causal) versus a mirror reflection (not causal).
- **The minus-two arithmetic for a 170** — see Teaching moves; this is the scoring model driving the whole session.

## Teaching moves

- **Names the exact score arithmetic instead of a vague goal.** "If you get minus two on all three sections, you just get a minus two, that brings you right about a 170 range." And the failure mode: "if you get minus four on RC or minus four on one of the LRs, you've ruined everything... even if you get a perfect RC section, you're banking on it." Standard set: "you should never ever get over minus two."
- **Randomized, timed pressure as a diagnostic rather than a test.** "I'm going to completely randomize it for you today. I'm not going to tell you what type of question types we're going to do. It's going to be completely randomized. You're not going to have an expectation." The rationale: "when you have unlimited time you can use all your mental energy and all your mental faculties towards one question and you'll be good, but you can't do that for 25 questions."
- **Reframes the point of the drill as exposing process, not producing errors.** "It's not like to get it wrong. You might not even get it wrong, but it'll at least give you the understanding of where you have shakiness when you apply heat."
- **Answers the "but I don't want to feel rushed" objection with a standard rather than reassurance.** David worried that a perfect section should feel calm and controlled. Joshua: "you still don't want to feel rushed as you're doing these questions. That's where it tests whether you would feel rushed... if you're feeling pressured or rushed or you're not feeling comfortable as you do it, that tells you that there's something that's inefficient in your system." And: "under the 1 minute 45 seconds for the questions, nothing should change. You should feel as comfortable as you are. But now it's just actually putting stakes on it."
- **Explicitly declines to teach content at this level.** Repeatedly: "this is not going to make it so you get the question right or wrong. It's just about being hyper-efficient, finding cheap cuts, shortcuts." And "you already know everything, so you're not going to learn new content." Also self-monitoring aloud: "I don't want to tell you stuff you already understand. I'm trying to be careful not to go too lost in it."
- **The "developer instinct" framing for pattern recognition** — "if we're going to have a developer instinct, what do we want to think of when we see the word reducing?"
- **Names the meta-benefit of seeing the pattern**: "oh, I've seen this game before. I know what you guys are doing. I don't have to play this BS with pretending I don't know where it's going to go."
- **Makes David state his answer before his reasoning, for accountability.** David has adopted this himself: "before going into reasons why, I want to make sure that I'm accountable. For this one, I chose answer choice D."
- **Withholds the correct answer until the full analysis is done.** He confirmed only after the entire walkthrough: "we got the answer choice correct, by the way, just to give you a heads up."
- **Teaches an abstract quantity rule through an absurd concrete case, then hands the student the pen.** The NFL sequence: "if I said all NFL players are female... then it would be true to say not all NFL players are female. That would be problematic." Then Chinese-speakers in the NFL, then finally the two of them on the call: "at least one of us is not Chinese. Does that mean that one of us is Chinese?" Joshua explicitly credits David's version as better: "you have a better example actually."
- **Admits when his own explanation sounds absurd.** "This is where it's so sneaky, because the language is so misleading when I say it. It sounds ridiculous as I hear myself." This lowers the cost of David not following immediately.
- **Checks his own comprehension before correcting.** "I think if I sorry, I might be wrong, I might have heard you wrong or I might have misunderstood you. So I want to be careful." Used twice.
- **Endorses the student's method rather than substituting his own.** After David explained the triangle-shape approach: "that's literally what you want to do. I would never say not to do that... that's exactly it, you got it right." The term-tracking tip was framed as an addition: "just a little cool little tip there."
- **Elevates a student observation into a durable rule.** David's two-prong insight got "that's a very good observation to make" plus the test-maker rationale.
- **Refuses to let a news cycle become a study distraction.** On the ABA/DEI change: "that's already been. That's kind of old news... nothing's changed. This cycle was the same. Even if you apply this cycle it would have been the same thing as next cycle. There's nothing that would have changed. This is not new. This is already a conversation I've had with many students." Closing advice: "all you can do is put your best foot forward. If you try to play the prediction game of Trump and all that stuff, you're going to go crazy."
- **Prescribes volume with a stated diagnostic purpose, not just more work.** "I want to get you out of the one section a week or practice test a week... so we can take an average of where you are... it's more like, where does your average hover around, so we know what you're dealing with, versus trying to do individual question types, which you already have a good handle on."
- **Extends the session rather than cutting the student off.** With five minutes left and David asking for a look at an RC passage, Joshua added time: "I'll add a couple more minutes because I have somebody after, but we have 10 minutes just to be comfortable."
- **Volunteers enthusiasm for the next session's format.** "We can dissect, get as many passages as you can... we can just bang them out and I have no issue. I enjoy that actually."
- **Engages the personal conversation seriously without steering it.** He asked genuine follow-ups about David's agnosticism and Catholic practice ("so you don't really believe in the spiritual part, but you believe more in the practical component, is that what it sounds like?"), offered his own position ("I used to have a lot of negative things to say about the Catholic Church, but as I got older... the Catholics are basically the same faith in many ways"), and closed it cleanly ("these are complicated topics and there's so much nuance that goes into them. But as for the LSAT stuff, how has that been going for you?").

## Worked examples

- **LR, Flaw — reducing speed limits.** Conclusion (first sentence): reducing speed limits will neither save lives nor protect the environment. Premises: the more slowly a car is driven, the more time it spends on the road spewing exhaust into the air and running the risk of colliding with other vehicles. David worked it in 1:45 and chose **D**, correctly.
  - His path: noted the conclusion is two-pronged (lives *and* environment), so a flaw could attack either. Found the lives-prong flaw easily and intuitively ("higher risk of colliding doesn't mean you're going to die... what if it's 10 mph crashes"), then noticed **D is the only choice touching the environment** and that the environment link (more time on road → more emissions) was the untested assumption.
  - **D (presumes without justification that total emissions for a given trip are determined mainly by the amount of time the trip takes)** — correct. The argument never establishes that trip duration, rather than something else, drives total emissions.
  - **E** — attractive, and closest to the lives-prong flaw David had prephrased, but killed by "only if": the author never claimed collision risk arises only from time on the road. Joshua confirmed this exact reasoning: "the author didn't actually assume you can only crash if you spend a long time."
  - **A (neglects that some motorists completely ignore speed limits)** — the sneaky one. It *strengthens*. David's first cut was that "most people will still follow the rule, so the chain still goes on"; Joshua pushed past that to the real reason: even granting it, drivers ignoring the limit means reducing it changes nothing, which supports the conclusion.
  - **B (ignores benefits from lower speeds other than environmental)** — out; the conclusion is scoped to those two variables.
  - **C (fails to consider that increased driving time will increase the number of cars on the road at any given time)** — out; if anything it supports the collision premise.
  - Joshua's efficiency retrospective: spotting "reducing" as a causal flag up front "gives you the ability to be even more efficient than you already probably are now."
  - A side thread David raised and Joshua declined to chase: whether the argument assumes drivers actually slow down when the limit drops. Joshua granted it is a genuine gap ("we don't want to assume that's going to be the effect... that's a valid point") but redirected to what the question actually tests: "it's a lot even simpler. They're trying to build a very simple cause and effect chain... does that necessarily entail that it'll be less lives? We don't know that."
- **LR, Sufficient Assumption — skilled artists, creativity, abstract reasoning, fame.** Chain structure: most skilled artists are very creative; all creative people are good at abstract reasoning; not all skilled artists are famous. Conclusion: some people who are good at abstract reasoning are famous. David locked **E** (most skilled artists are famous) on gut inside the time, then verified.
  - His reasoning was shape-first: the two chains are disjointed, so "you just make a triangle" by asserting most A's are C's, which then flows through the existing links to deliver the "some" conclusion.
  - Joshua's added angle: the evidence never mentions famous people **at all**. "Not all skilled artists are famous" tells us only that at least one is not famous; it is entirely consistent with zero famous artists. So the correct answer must import "famous," and E is the only choice that does.
  - David's own competing filter, which he explained afterward: he was tracking the quantifier rather than the term, reasoning that "some skilled artists are famous" would not bridge (those could be the wrong subset) and that "most" plus a conditional was needed. He rejected D because it ran up the chain in the wrong direction.
- **RC, historians / property / women's rights (brought by David, ~3 minutes).** The passage discusses whether a change represented "a gain, one that reflects changing views about democracy and property," and then argues it did not. David was between B and D and asked two questions: whether the historians named late in the passage are the same group as those named earlier (he assumed yes, since both hold the same view and the author would not switch groups silently), and whether **"reflects" implies cause and effect**. Joshua answered the vocabulary question (no inherent causal content; context decides) and deferred the full passage to next session. B was rejected by David on the word "significant."
