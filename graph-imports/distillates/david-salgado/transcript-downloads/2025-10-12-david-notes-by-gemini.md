# David Salgado, 2025-10-12

A two-hour late-night session, three weeks out from David's November LSAT, that split three ways: a long personal disclosure about grief and therapy that was affecting his study week, an RC diagnostic on referential phrasing that produced the session's central technique (the underlining rule), and admissions work on his diversity statement.

## LSAT concepts
- **Referential phrasing** — David's own name for his failure mode: answer choices that require connecting an idea in one paragraph to a reference in a distant paragraph. Covers both single-word-in-context questions and inference questions, with the inference variety costing him more.
- **Defined / semi-defined / undefined targets** — Joshua's taxonomy for RC question types and how to attack each. *Defined*: an underlined word or line, so "you want to be able to prephrase that." *Semi-defined*: "the author suggests that he agrees with which one of these ideas" — not localized to one place, answered from the general theme. *Undefined*: "the passage states all of the following except" — "you can't prephrase these, because the answers are what speak to you. You have to just jump in." For undefined targets he recommends **Control-F**, "because it has to be found in the actual passage."
- **The underlining rule** — the session's core technique. "You're not allowed to choose the answer unless you can underline the exact sentence that you get support from... not the paragraph, the line, the sentence, from beginning to end or half the sentence."
- **Every RC answer must be must-be-true and passage-sourced** — "four of the answers will have zero support. One of them will be supported. If not 99%, it's going to be damn near 99%." And: "all of the answers are almost like a rewording of something that was said in the passage. It's never going to come from outside the passage."
- **Person passages have a road map in paragraph one** — "whenever you see a passage about a person and why they're special, the first paragraph is monumental... they'll say he's special because of one, two, three reasons, and then the rest of the passage is going to follow the road map that they set in the first paragraph." Usually three factors, one per paragraph.
- **A passage as an LR argument** — the person's distinction is the conclusion; each paragraph supplies a premise. "Sam Gilliam was one of the foremost painters in the Washington Color School — that's my conclusion. What are the premises for that? Because one, he did this thing; two, he did this thing."
- **Question-and-answer passage structure** — first paragraph establishes a mystery, later paragraphs hunt explanations. "The answer is the main point. Whatever the explanation is... The main point of the whole passage is that's the reason why. It's not going to be the question."
- **"Best" does not mean good** — "the LSAT wrote 'which one of the following best.' It doesn't have to be actually right. Best doesn't mean good. Best just means, of the bunch, which one's the best. So the best of the worst, you've got to take the best of the worst regardless."
- **"Some" in a comparative context** — technically "some" is compatible with "all," but a comparison forces a distinction. "There can't be a distinction amongst two things that are the exact same thing... If we're talking about which one's less prone to ethical failings and we're just talking about two groups but they're the exact same groups, that wouldn't make any sense."
- **Strength-of-language traps** — "most likely" flagged as "a big word there" in an except question; "exclusively" flagged as the risky half of an otherwise well-supported answer.
- **Practice tests as the least efficient study unit** — see Teaching moves.

## Worked examples
- **Sam Gilliam / Washington Color School passage (an "all of the following EXCEPT" question).** David's main point: Gilliam represents a view distinct from other African-American artists and the public on what art should represent — specifically experimental and abstract work, which was rare among African-American artists at the time. Joshua accepted it and added the person-passage road map structure. On choice D ("the color field style involved experimentation more than Gilliam believed the art of his African-American contemporaries did"), David could not initially see the support because it requires linking paragraph one to a later paragraph. They located it: Gilliam found his contemporaries' approach "aesthetically conservative," the message "unmistakable," with "little room for the exploration of new artistic territory through experimentation and innovation." So D is supported and therefore not the answer. Credited answer E — that everyday images are most likely to give artists great inspiration — dies on "most likely": the passage shows hanging laundry inspired Gilliam, not that he held a general view about everyday imagery. David got this right on his own.
- **Comparative passage on taxing online gaming (Passage B).** A question pointing at a line in the final paragraph about "intentionally commodified" games, with the actual support in paragraph one. Under time pressure David dismissed D ("players of some games own intellectual property") because he doubted the "some" groups were the same group, and picked E on gut feel because a nearby sentence mentioned currency. In review he found the link: paragraph one says some games *encourage* item trading and give players IP rights, and "encourages" maps onto "intentionally commodified," so the two groups are the same. E fails because the passage describes trading virtual items for *real* currency, never one virtual currency for another. Joshua's verdict on the design: brutal, and his method for it: gather every fact the passage states about the named characteristic (Control-F helps) before touching the answers. His point about the underlining rule landed here — David could not have underlined support for E.
- **Canon lawyers passage (PT 104, section 2, passage 2 — cold, unseen).** David's paragraph map, produced in one sentence each: P1 establishes the paradox (canon lawyers had organizations setting professional standards but did not enforce them); P2 offers two explanations for the lack of disciplinary action; P3 supports one of them; P4 gives further support, church authorities complaining about the system; P5 shows the criticism backfired — being pushed to professionalize made them close ranks, defend bad lawyers, and protect the profession's reputation from outsiders.
  - *Main point.* David's first answer drifted toward the paradox itself. Joshua walked him back with two questions: what was the mystery, and what was the answer? Landing on the second explanation, weak enforcement / inefficiency. Credited answer C, which both agreed was a poor answer accepted only by elimination.
  - *Detail question, "according to [the author], which of the following about the law courts in medieval England is true."* David chose correctly but was bothered by "exclusively." Joshua's argument: the passage says some English lawyers practised in both civil and church courts while others served only one, and the whole hypothesis being tested — whether church lawyers were less prone to ethical failings than civil lawyers — is nonsensical if the two groups are identical. So "some" here cannot mean all. David also walked his elimination of E (that ecclesiastical courts kept richer, more thorough records than English civil courts): the passage does describe rich medieval church records, but in a different paragraph and with no comparison to civil courts drawn — "it was really tempting, but because there's no connection I removed it."
