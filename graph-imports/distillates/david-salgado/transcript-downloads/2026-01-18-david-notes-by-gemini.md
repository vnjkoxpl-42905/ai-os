# David Salgado, 2026-01-18

## LSAT concepts
- **Prephrase calibration by question scope**: "the more narrow the scope of that question is, the more you should aim to prephrase before looking at any of the answer choices." For a broad question like the purpose of a paragraph, a general idea suffices; for a line-level question, go into the nitty gritty.
- **Function of a line**: "ask yourself what's the function of that line, that portion, that sentence, that word. What role does it play in that paragraph, and what role does that paragraph play in regards to the main conclusion?"
- **Four-step process for attributed-view inference questions**, stated by Joshua as a repeatable procedure: locate the term, identify the speaker of the term, find the rebuttal, match the fight. "The answer has to reflect this disagreement."
- **Hedged versus absolute language as an attribution signal**: CLS uses "cannot" and "without"; Marson answers with "may be viewed as a part of but not separate from the rules." The hedge is the whole answer. Joshua: "it's almost always going to be a little bit funky, like the word 'may.' That's the classic way they'll try to beat you."
- **Trap answers built from the opposing side's position**: David's wrong answer D reflected what CLS believes, not what Marson believes, and felt right because it was familiar from the passage.
- **Author as reporter versus author as advocate**: David's read, that the passage author is an observer describing a debate, implicitly sympathetic to Marson but never explicitly endorsing him. Joshua endorsed the distinction.
- **Inference answers are rarely black and white**: "it's very rarely going to be, oh yeah, he thinks he's completely a dumbass or he's completely intelligent." Joshua tied this to the test's purpose: "because they want you to be a lawyer, they don't want you to think in black and white, because the law is never that."
- **Skip and return under time pressure** as a deliberate skill. David left a hard question, banked time on questions five, six, and seven, and came back. Joshua: "that's perfect."
- **Blind review versus timed score as a diagnostic split**: a large gap means a test-taking problem (pacing, mental fatigue), not an accuracy or knowledge problem.

## Teaching moves
- Made David explain his own method before evaluating it: "what's the method, the mindset, the framework you used to approach this question type?" Then, "let's see if we can make a step-by-step process here," and had him restate the question stem in his own words first.
- Handed the student the teaching role when hazy on the material: "I'm a bit hazy on this passage. I want you to almost coach me through this as we go." Then the one-sentence-per-paragraph summary drill, paragraph by paragraph, tightening each into a slogan.
- Compressed David's sprawling summaries into crisp formulations he could reuse: "conflicting values don't make it irrational," "just because a legal course is hard doesn't mean it's arbitrary."
- Handled a real methodological objection seriously rather than overruling it. David argued that decoding every nuance costs time he may not have. Joshua conceded it was "a very good point, a fair critique," then drew the line by scope: nuance-hunting is for narrow questions specifically, and in practice you go the hardest way so it becomes automatic. "What becomes taking a very heavy notice of something will become just your default way of thinking."
- Named the altitude of the work explicitly to justify the severity: "we're really trying to pick at straws to get you to a 99th percentile score. We're not looking to make you go from decent to good, we're looking to make you perfect... we're not going to tell you you need to learn how to read English, you're already very good." And directly: "I'm being hard on you, for that reason."
- Set the aspiration frame: "we want to aim for 180 and be okay with a 17... we want 170 to be a floor." David's version: "shooting for the sun so I land on the stars."
- Reframed the coming practice test as easier than the drill: "you're going to be surprised how easy it is when you take the ankle weights off and you run."
- Deliberately did not defuse test-day pressure: "I don't want to dismiss the pressure tomorrow because I want you to feel the pressure even though it's not the end of the world. You're going to have to face the pressure in February. I'd rather you feel the pressure when it doesn't actually count."
- Delivered a bad score with the structure inverted, tough news first, then the frame: original 163, blind review 175. Immediately diagnosed rather than consoled: minus seven on LR was "super out of character," a "fluke," and the disparity itself is the evidence. "You're not lost. You know the answers when you have the time. It's more so test taking issues."
- Refused to let outliers set self-assessment in either direction: "if you got a 180 I would also say the same thing. I wouldn't use the outliers as my reflection of my ability, because that can give you a false sense of confidence or false sense of discouragement. Minus seven is not you. That's just not who you are."
- Was blunt about the stakes of LR regression without softening: "that can't happen. You can't afford a minus seven on an LR section... if you get minus three in RC but you get worse at LR, you're basically at the same point."
- Named the fix as reactivation rather than relearning: "if you have RC down, LR will be almost like waking up the dragon." Prescribed full LR sections, not individual questions, since the issue is sectional stamina and pacing.
- Pressed on communication discipline as a risk, not a scold: David had not been sending updates. "I'm not trying to point fingers, but you need to keep me in the loop, because if I come in blind with the time we have left, it's dangerous."
- Praised process over outcome where it mattered: on the one missed question, "you flagged it, which is why I'm very impressed, because it wasn't like you got the question wrong blindly."

## Worked examples
- **Legal theory passage: Marson versus Critical Legal Studies (CLS).**
  - Paragraph map produced by David under Joshua's prompting. Main conclusion: Marson argues CLS exaggerates the problems with traditional legal theory, and even where the problems are real they do not damage the theory. Paragraph: conflicting values do not amount to contradiction, since values can be ranked (example, a lawyer's duty of confidentiality against harm to others). Paragraph: having two good answers does not make a situation unreasonable or arbitrary. Final paragraph: performing under a rule does not require believing in that rule, via the rules-of-a-game analogy.
  - Question on what Marson would agree about "external considerations." David originally chose D, drawn to it because it felt familiar; D actually states the CLS position, that you must endorse the external considerations you appeal to. In blind review he eliminated A through D and took E, which says whether these considerations are part of or separate from the legal process is a matter of debate. The deciding evidence is Marson's hedge, "may be viewed as a part of but not separate from the rules," against CLS's absolute "cannot merely apply the rules without appealing." Marson only needs to introduce doubt, not to assert the opposite.
  - David's own working analogy while reading: Squid Game, where CLS claims the players must love the rules and love what the organizers impose, while Marson says that is part of the game rather than separate from it.
