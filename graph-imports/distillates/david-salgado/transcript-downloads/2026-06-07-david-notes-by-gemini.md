# David Salgado, 2026-06-07

## LSAT concepts

- **Flaw** — Joshua's frame: expect one of two things, roughly half and half. Either "a classic fallacy" with a known name, or "just an objection that you have to think of like just kind of like courtroom style." Identify the flaw before reading answers, but treat your prephrase as "a road map, not the destination" (David's phrasing, which Joshua endorsed).
- **Equivocation** — the correct answer on question 1. Joshua: the author uses "mature" as "the glue" to "force these two ideas together by using the exact same root word," then "automatically like almost magically pretend that concept A and concept B are identical." Physical/physiological maturation vs. mental responsibility. He called it "the slight of hand."
- **Circular reasoning** — the fallacy behind an answer phrased "assumes what it is trying to prove." Joshua: "it's almost never going to be the right answer." He parsed the wording literally: "what it's trying to prove" just means the conclusion, so the answer says the argument assumes its own conclusion. Distinct from an argument that gives bad evidence: "it is giving you evidence for what it's trying to prove but the evidence is just bad evidence."
- **Appeal to authority** — Joshua pushed back on David's claim that an authority must be a person. "Anything that can be authoritative would be an authority," so a discipline like science can be one. The answer still fails because the appeal here is not *inappropriate*.
- **Overgeneralization / hasty generalization** — rejected because the stimulus offers no examples to generalize from. Joshua on naming: "the name of it actually isn't the end of it, it's not that big of a deal because you described it perfectly."
- **Necessary assumption** — tested by negation. New concepts in the conclusion must be at least conceptually covered by the evidence: "You can never ever say something in a conclusion that's not mentioned in the evidence."
- **Causation** — David mapped the second stimulus as cause (people prefer natural beauty) to effect (influx of residents and businesses). Joshua flagged this read as important on its own.

## Teaching moves

- **Compressed the clock to raise difficulty.** Opened by cutting David to 1.5 minutes per question "to make it extra difficult for you." Later used the result as evidence: "when you have less time to do it, you were forced to just stick to your guns... sometimes you get more accuracy because you don't get a chance to like rethink it or like hash it out and like find ways to psych yourself out. It's better sometimes to just stick to your guns because... anything is more overthinking than underthinking is going to be your problem. You already usually have your hand on the pulse."
- **Meta-level stimulus mapping.** The session's central teaching push. Joshua asked David to restate each sentence by rhetorical function, not content: "not just the argument part, but the nature of this statement." He modeled it: line 1 is "a golden rule... this is what needs to be done," line 2 "introduces some objective scientific evidence," line 3 "is combining the information that we had prior... it's trying to shove the premise and conclusion together." Framed as zoom control: "to be able to go like zoom in zoom out, the hyperdetailed focus of what each word means, then also zooming out to knowing what the sentence means... what's the function of the sentence."
- **Guarded the caveat.** Every time he pushed the abstraction he protected against losing content: "I don't mean this at the expense of the content. I always have to be careful when I say that." Treats premise and conclusion as variables so the gap between them is visible.
- **Demanded a fallacy name per answer choice.** "As we go through the answers I want you to tell me what fallacy each answer is talking about." Rationale given explicitly: answers "are just always repackaged in different ways, but once we know them and we know their language, we should be able to do it very efficiently."
- **The "database" metaphor for variant phrasings.** When David asked whether circular reasoning gets disguised in other wordings, Joshua called it "a very good question" and answered: "now when you add it to your database... they'll have a few different ways of saying the same flaw... it becomes a part of your database of ways of saying the same thing... they can't ever trip you up by using a different variation because you've seen all the variations and you've already classified them, and your muscle memory will be able to effectively help you through answer choices."
- **Answered pushback with a concrete counterexample.** On appeal to authority: "if I were to say like, god is not real, why? because the scientists say they're not real... that's assuming that science is the authority on that matter."
- **Let the student correct himself mid-analogy.** David built a Mona Lisa analogy, caught that it used a person, and rebuilt it as "science has established that people aren't rational, therefore the legal theories are horrible." Joshua's only intervention: "Exactly. That's a good example."
- **Answer-order triage for necessary assumption.** "Run to the answer with the word 'not' in it first... you can easily test it by just taking out the not. Read it without the not. Pretend like the not doesn't exist and see if the argument falls."
- **Named "most" as a red flag.** "When we see the word 'most' for necessary assumption, that's its number one enemy. I don't think I've ever seen the correct answer with 'most' in it. It's almost like guaranteed to be wrong." Reason: if it can be 50%, the assumption is not necessary.
- **Distinguished weaken from necessary.** On the tempting trap answer: "B does weaken it though. B is very tempting... by negating it, it does seem to do something, but it's not destroying the conclusion as you would want."
- **"Disguised" as the name for non-verbatim answer language.** When David rejected the correct answer for lacking the phrase "governmentally mandated," Joshua asked "Does it have to?" then: "It's disguised. This answer disguises its reference to the new term by alluding to it as a factor harmful to some older local industries... It doesn't have to be verbatim. It just has to address the idea, at least point towards it, especially with necessary assumption."
- **Closed the loop back to the student's own work.** "The exact concepts you pointed out that were new concepts were the exact things that were in the answer choice. Both of them: one was disguised, the other one was explicit."
- **Reframed a wrong reason on a right answer as a win.** David twice said "thank god I got it wrong" about his reasoning; Joshua let that stand rather than smoothing it over, treating the corrected reasoning as the actual gain.

## Worked examples

**Question 1, flaw (level 5).** Stimulus: society grants adult privileges at the age responsibilities can be handled; science establishes physiological maturation is complete in most people by 17; since the maturing process is complete for most 17-year-olds, there is no reason to withhold adult privileges.

- David prephrased the gap between physiological development and social maturity, and picked C before reviewing choices.
- (D) inappropriate appeal to authority: rejected. Science is a valid authority to appeal to; the appeal is neither inappropriate nor "too readily" accepted.
- (E) rejected: "some people" cannot undermine a general conclusion. David's own parallel: "people like to have chocolate" is not weakened by "some people don't like chocolate."
- (B) hasty generalization: rejected because the stimulus gives no examples to generalize from. David's gas-price analogy: two neighbors reporting $1.28 does not establish the national price.
- (A) "assumes what it is trying to prove": identified as circular reasoning; almost never correct. Joshua's illustration: "the Knicks are going to win tomorrow. Why? Because the Knicks are better off at winning the game tomorrow."
- (C) equivocation: correct. Joshua noted the difficulty and the speed: "that was not an easy question by any means. I'm surprised you got it so quickly."
- Concept clarification that took ~5 minutes: David felt "assumes" was the wrong word in (A). Joshua located the real defect elsewhere: "it completely is an assumption. The part that's actually problematic is not that word. The part is actually 'what it's trying to prove.'" Assumption = unstated premise; equivocation is by definition a type of assumption.

**Question 2, necessary assumption (level 5).** Stimulus: many people prefer to live in regions of natural beauty, so there is often an influx of new residents and businesses relocating there; therefore governmentally mandated environmental protections can help a region's economy overall, even if such protections harm some older local industries.

- David selected E under time, with shaky confidence.
- Joshua ran a concept-overlap audit: what appears in both evidence and conclusion (economy, businesses, nature), then what is new in the conclusion. New: "governmentally mandated environmental protections," "older local industries," and arguably "overall" / net effect. Prediction issued before reading answers: "our answer will almost certainly deal with that information. It will almost guarantee do that."
- (B) "the economies of most regions of natural beauty are based primarily on local industries that would be harmed by governmentally mandated protections": David initially flipped to B on negation. Killed by "most" and "primarily" against a conclusion that only claims protections *can* help.
- (D) rejected as a "comparison trap" (David's term): voluntary vs. mandated protection, plus "usually." The stimulus makes no claim about voluntary protection.
- (A) rejected: conditional, and unsupported claims about residency.
- (C) rejected: addresses the mechanism of causation, irrelevant to the older-industries gap.
- (E) correct: "a factor harmful to some older local industries in the region need not discourage other businesses from relocating to the region." Covers both new concepts, one disguised. Negated: protections do discourage relocation, so they cause economic harm "without really providing an eventual economic benefit."
