# David Salgado, 2025-12-07

## LSAT concepts
- **Necessary assumption**: flagged as David's weakest LR type. Joshua's read is diagnostic, not just accuracy based: "even the ones you get right, it's taking you way longer than it should," and the time spent is the tell.
- **Reading comprehension, main point**: the main point answer must center the school of thought's core claim, not a peripheral proposal. Joshua pushed David off the last sentence toward the first sentence of paragraph two ("steady state economists believe the neoclassical model to be unrealistic and hold that the economy is dependent on nature") as the load-bearing sentence.
- **Reading comprehension, author's stance**: "the side that the author speaks about more almost always is going to be the side" the author leans toward. Used as a speed heuristic before any close reading.
- **Reading comprehension, two-viewpoint passage structure**: once two viewpoints appear in paragraph one, the expectation is support for each and the author eventually leaning. David generated this prediction himself and Joshua confirmed it.
- **Inference / defined vs undefined questions**: David's own taxonomy check, "this would be undefined, it could come from anywhere in the passage," so on a real test he would skip and come back. Defined questions have a locatable context window.
- **Context window**: Joshua's central RC tool this session. Prephrase the answer, then find the specific lines that support it, then match answer choices against those lines rather than against memory.
- **Quantifier precision**: "some steady state economists" versus "steady state economists" is a real difference, a few individuals versus the whole school of thought. Both C and D carried the flaw; D still wins as best available.

## Teaching moves
- Opened with performance data, not content: named necessary assumption as the problem type using time-per-question as the signal, then asked David to self-diagnose ("have you figured out what it is about those questions that are giving you a bit more challenge?"). David had not, and Joshua parked it rather than answering for him.
- Interrogated an outside method rather than dismissing it. David had been working with "RCT Tutor" from Reddit on translating abstract sentences into "your English frequency" and on ranking answer choices instead of eliminating. Joshua repeatedly asked what was actually *novel*: "Is there anything novel with regards to like that clicked for you potentially? Just for me to know, because for the most part that's pretty solid standard."
- Collapsed the ranking/elimination distinction while validating the student's preference: "the way you do ranking is by eliminating, the way you do elimination is like by ranking... it's kind of indirectly the same thing, but there's different ways to skin a cat." The point being that a method can be identical in substance and still "gel with you better."
- Structured the passage work paragraph by paragraph with a six-minute read timer, asking "what's the first paragraph tell us?" then "what do we expect in the passage?" before any question was touched.
- Deliberate silence during answer elimination: "I'm going to refrain from, I'm going to bite my tongue, I'm going to let you go through them, because if I influence your choice it's going to kind of ruin the chance for you to grow. But I do like that you think out loud."
- Taught the imperfect-answer posture directly. On D: "it's ridiculous for the main point answer to center around some steady state economists rather than the whole school of thought... but as we said before, it's the best available answer. You have to take the best one available, despite its downside."
- Cross-question leverage as a tactic: "using the questions against each other is a very useful tactic," because an answer already confirmed elsewhere in the set can be ruled in or out quickly.
- Resolved a vocabulary worry with textual evidence rather than assertion. When David asked whether "environment" equals "nature," Joshua pointed to the second paragraph using "nature's limited capacity" and "the cost to the environment" in the same sentence, so the author treats them interchangeably.
- Taught the "no support" answer shape at the end: on the correct answer B, "it's not that the economist would be opposed to it, it just has nothing to do with it... we can't say he's against it, we just don't have any support."

## Worked examples
- **PT 106, Section 4, Passage 3** (steady state versus neoclassical economics), read under a six-minute timer, then worked paragraph by paragraph.
  - Structure David built: paragraph one introduces the two viewpoints with no authorial stance; paragraph two explains the steady state position (economy depends on nature, must stay at optimal size) and the neoclassical counter (nature is merely one element of the economy, not a constraint); paragraph three offers conservation as an alternative to unlimited growth, with the caveat that it must not sacrifice what people want.
  - Main point question: eliminated A and B (neoclassical focused) and E (off topic, "effective way of the steady increase"). Split C versus D. Rejected C for "desirable," "should adopt," and "temporary economic stagnation," all unsupported. Chose D. Joshua confirmed D while flagging that both C and D wrongly say "some steady state economists."
  - EXCEPT question (what neoclassicals would *not* say steady state economists are wrong about): eliminated B (text says neoclassicals treat nature as an element rather than a constraint), C (neoclassicals do not fear growth), D and E (they reject the notion of optimal size). Answer A, the environment's ability to yield raw material is limited, because neoclassicals concede finiteness and answer with substitutes.
  - "Why is unlimited growth dangerous" question: located the context window in paragraph two, prephrased "cost to the environment rises faster than benefits," selected E as a near restatement. Eliminated A (replacement resources is the neoclassical line, not the danger), B (efficiency lives in paragraph three, wrong window), C (markets never discussed), D (income inequality is an issue, not the stated danger).
  - "Least likely to endorse" question: defined conservation as increasing efficiency of resource use through recycling and resource management without increasing resource amount. Eliminated A (recycling), C (quality control reduces defective output), D (greater fuel efficiency), E (reducing output to end overproduction). Answer B, using a less expensive fuel, because "less expensive" implies only saving money, not efficiency, and the passage gives no basis to connect it to steady state goals. This was the question David struggled with most.
