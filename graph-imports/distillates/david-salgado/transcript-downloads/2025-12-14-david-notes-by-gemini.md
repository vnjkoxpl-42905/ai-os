# David Salgado, 2025-12-14

## LSAT concepts
- **Reading comprehension, main point**: identified as the sentence stating that nature does provide examples that are not adaptations for reproductive success. Joshua had David underline it before touching any question.
- **Reading comprehension, author's tone / attitude**: the correct answer was "emphatic disagreement," not the safer "mild disapproval." Joshua's rule: mild disapproval implies the author largely stands beside the view with minor qualifications; when the passage's entire architecture is "they are wrong," emphatic is right.
- **Conditional logic and contrapositive** used inside RC: the passage says if the strict constructionists are right, then the persistence of every attribute must be due to adaptation. It then denies the necessary condition. "If A is true then B must be true, and B is not true, therefore A is not true." That structure is what proves the author disagrees flatly, not mildly.
- **Method of reasoning / role of a paragraph**: paragraph two is the author's main conclusion; paragraphs three and four are premises; paragraph one is background. Joshua's framework: read an RC passage as one large LR argument.
- **Main conclusion**: "when the author gives his belief on something and says they're wrong," that is the conclusion. Joshua walked David to this by question rather than telling him.
- **Context window / prephrase calibration**: "we always want to have a prephrase that matches the level of the context window. The more specific the context window is, the more precise your prephrase should be." A broad question like "which would the author agree with" cannot be prephrased tightly; a localized structure question must be.
- **Scope violations**: answer A on the paragraph-purpose question referenced "traditional evolutionary theory," never mentioned in the passage. Joshua: "there's no assume, never assume."
- **Comparative passages**: main point questions on Passage A should be answerable from Passage A alone, every time.
- **Extreme versus conservative answers**: David's default is to pick the more conservative wording, which Joshua endorsed as generally correct while showing this passage is the exception.

## Teaching moves
- Started with a three to four minute silent reread of a passage already worked, "just for us to have a solid footing, just to rehash everything," before asking any question.
- Conceded to the student when the student was right. Joshua had flagged the first paragraph's word "reduce" as an authorial tone marker; David argued the connotation belonged to Darwin, not the author. Joshua backed down explicitly: "you're correct, for sure right... I'm being a bit more overaggressive than I should be. Actually you're probably better off doing it not this way." He still named the underlying habit: "your heat seekers should be on."
- Pushed for altitude when the student answered at content level. David gave the second paragraph's content; Joshua said "let's zoom out even more though, what's the general idea of the second paragraph?" and got "to counter." David noticed his own error: "I keep on thinking about the content."
- Socratic chain to derive that paragraph two is the conclusion: "what's the function of the second paragraph?" then "if the author is going against it, what does that technically mean?" then "if it was an argument, what would you call that to be?" David: "the conclusion." Joshua: "Bingo."
- Made David find the evidence himself rather than accepting a tone claim: "the best thing to do is even to underline what he says exactly... let's find the scope window, the target window."
- Restated the passage in two words to settle the tone question: "if you were to put it in two words, it's saying they are wrong." Then listed the repetitions: "one reason why they're wrong, another reason why they're wrong, another reason why they're wrong. He's not saying they're partially wrong, he's saying their viewpoint is completely terrible... he stands on business."
- Preserved the general rule while overriding it for this case: "usually you're correct, you want to go with the more conservative answer... but I would let that get in the way of being wrong. Emphatic is usually not an attractive choice to go with, typically. This one is a rare one."
- Etymology as a vocabulary tool for "enumerates": "you could look at the root word and the etymology... enumerates, when you think of it, sounds most similar to the word number. Would that sound reasonable?" David derived "lists" himself.
- Delivered the session's signature transferable framework, explicitly leveraging David's stronger section: "always turn it into an LR argument... which paragraph is the main conclusion, which paragraph is the premise, which is background information." Then a level deeper: "turn each paragraph into an LR stimulus, and underline what sentence would be the main conclusion." Rationale: "it's useful to have different dynamic frameworks that can accommodate to which paragraph or passage you're working on, because sometimes certain frameworks don't work as well as other ones."
- Used a real-world parallel to teach the difference between a framework and a prescription: if we were to rectify what happened to Black Americans, saying we should consider what was done to them and what followed "is not me telling you what to do." Applied it to the passage's "what if anything ought to be done": "it's not saying something ought to be done, it's saying *what if*. That's the difference."
- Reframed a diagnostic use of the comparative-passage rule: "if you cannot get the main point answer based off the first passage, that means you did not read the first passage well enough... reading the second passage is just putting a band-aid on something deeper."
- Argued sections over full practice tests with a training analogy: "if you're not able to run the race at the time you want to run it, why do stamina? Stamina is only useful once you have developed the skills in the first place."

## Worked examples
- **Darwin / strict constructionists passage** (question 5 and question 6 worked in full).
  - Structure built together: paragraph one introduces the strict constructionists (natural selection explains all traits and species) against Darwin himself, who objected to reducing his theory to natural selection alone. Paragraph two states the strict constructionist assumption and immediately pushes back, that not all adaptation serves reproductive success. Paragraph three gives neutral DNA mutations as an example. Paragraph four gives mass extinction, the asteroid and the dinosaurs, where survival was "dumb luck" rather than adaptation.
  - Question 5 (author's attitude): David eliminated C and E (agreement, endorsement, neutrality) instantly, split A "emphatic disagreement" versus B "mild disapproval," chose B for safety. Correct answer A. Evidence Joshua marched through: "but in fact numerous examples," the "if they are right, then" conditional, and the final paragraph's "the study of mass extinctions and paleontology has undermined the strict constructionist claim." Decisive point: no concession anywhere in the passage.
  - Question 6 (purpose of paragraph two): eliminated A ("traditional evolutionary theory," never mentioned, and paragraph two is not raising objections to it), B ("recent evidence," wrong direction, since paragraphs three and four support paragraph two rather than the reverse), C (popularity of the evidence never discussed), D ("enumerates the arguments... rebutted in the paragraphs that follow," rejected because paragraph two contains the conclusion and is not a list). Answer E: it explains the ramifications of the strict constructionist claims and "helps clarify the relevance of the evidence offered in subsequent paragraphs." Joshua's decode: "clarify the relevance means this is the main point, said in a sneaky way."
- **Comparative passage on justice and property** (question 22, Passage A main point).
  - Passage A structure: paragraph one gives the principles of justice regarding property, paragraph two the ideal entitlement to property if everything were fair, paragraph three the problem of past injustice and a rectification principle based on looking at history. David's own framing: Passage A is "the rule book," Passage B was specific, about Native Americans.
  - David's worry: the word "ought" in "what if anything ought to be done to rectify the injustice" seemed to signal an opinion. Resolved by the "if anything" qualifier making the whole thing hypothetical.
  - Answers: A eliminated (proposes a solution, and the author proposes none), C eliminated (too narrow, only two fundamental principles), D eliminated ("argue"), E eliminated ("advocate"). Answer B, since the author is informing rather than advancing a position.
