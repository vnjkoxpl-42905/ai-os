# David Salgado, 2026-04-12

## LSAT concepts
- **Entail versus logically follow**: on the LSAT these are synonyms. "Entail means 100% guarantee. If statement A entails statement B, statement A forces statement B to be true. There is no possible world where A is true but B is false." Joshua's illustration: being a golden retriever entails being a dog; it does not entail liking fetch, since some are lazy.
- **Reference words ("these," "they," "that")**: "never allow the word these, they, or that to be ambiguous. These is just a placeholder for the two ideas prior. We never want to have it gray or general familiarity. We want to be saying it's black this, white this." Substituting the referent turns a vague answer into a verbatim match.
- **Counterintuitive**: "it sounds weird or wrong based off my common sense or gut feeling." Not the same as contradictory. Procedure Joshua gave: find where the word appears, then identify how the author is using it.
- **Logically inconsistent versus incompatible**: logically inconsistent is a rigid, technical term meaning A and not-A affirmed at once, "like a mathematical contradiction." Incompatible is broad, about whether ideas fit together. "You can have a perfectly logical idea that is counterintuitive."
- **Avoid extreme stance answers**: "even if it seems like the author is taking a strong stance, it's always safer to stay on the less extreme side." Ardent approval was out of scope where respectful defense of the basic positions was correct.
- **Main conclusion versus adjacent sentence**: David's wrong answer A restated the sentence *before* the main conclusion. The trap was built to look like the wrong sentence.
- **Structural reading of a defense passage**: paragraph one introduces the position and the two reasons it was rejected; paragraph two refutes the first; paragraph three refutes the second.
- **Prephrase then look**: "identify where it is, predict it, then look at the answers," so the choice is anchored in passage evidence before evaluation.
- **Vocabulary via context clues rather than memorized lists**, David's own approach, since you cannot predict which words appear.

## Worked examples
- **PT 136, Section 3, Passage 4** (philosophical anarchism), read cold in five minutes, then four questions worked under timer.
  - Structure David produced unaided: main point is the final sentence of paragraph one, that philosophical anarchism does not entail these claims. Paragraph one introduces philosophical anarchism (no general moral duty to obey government) plus two commentator criticisms: that it implies no government is morally better than any other, and that it implies people may do whatever they please. Paragraph two refutes the first (you can still judge North Korea worse than England). Paragraph three refutes the second (you still have a moral duty not to harm the innocent; David's shorthand, "you still have to be a good guy").
  - Main point question: David chose A, "some views that certain commentators consider to be implications of philosophical anarchism are highly counterintuitive." Correct answer C, that from philosophical anarchism it does not follow that no government is better than another or that people may do as they please. C is the main conclusion with "these claims" substituted by the claims themselves. A restates the sentence immediately before the conclusion. David's stated obstacle was not the referent but the phrase "does not logically follow," which he did not map onto "does not entail."
  - Commonly-held-belief question: David answered A with 90% confidence, sourcing it to the first paragraph's mention of special exceptions for overruling governments, which implies that in general people are expected to follow the law simply because it is the law. Confirmed by process of elimination on B and E.
  - Author's stance question: David reasoned the author is defending rather than endorsing, "a bit soft on the theory," not an extremist. Rejected A for being too strong. Held E open only because he did not know "antipathy," then rejected "resolute antipathy" and "certain of its logical consequences" as too strong and unsupported. Correct answer B, a respectful defense of the theory's basic positions. His remaining discomfort was with "basic positions," since the author counters implications rather than affirming fundamentals.
  - "Counterintuitive" inference question, which Joshua had deliberately built toward: David initially associated counterintuitive with contradiction, which pulled him toward D (incompatible) and E (internal logical inconsistency). He eliminated B and C, then chose A correctly by reading how the term functions in the passage rather than defining it. Correct inference: the commentators think the implications are flawed because most people believe some governments are morally better than others. David admitted he does not use "counterintuitive" in everyday speech and reasoned entirely from context.
