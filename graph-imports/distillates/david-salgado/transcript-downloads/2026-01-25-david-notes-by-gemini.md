# David Salgado, 2026-01-25

## LSAT concepts
- **Passage map**: name what the author is *doing* in each paragraph, not what it says. Joshua: "it would give you an immense ability to really see the big pieces moving together for the main point questions."
- **Topic versus purpose versus main point**, separated deliberately: topic is what the passage is about (IP rights versus openness on the internet), purpose is why the author wrote it (present the debate and weigh in), main point is what he concluded. "The purpose is the why, the main point is the what."
- **Main point answers cannot contain anything false**: "it can't have anything wrong... you have to trust that if you see it, it wasn't there by accident."
- **Half-right main point traps**: "whenever you see the answer starting out very tempting, especially when it's suspiciously close, you want to follow the language of the last sentence, because the first half of the answer will be amazing and the second half will be where they mess it up. That's one of the guaranteed traps you're going to have on test day."
- **Over-strong language as disqualifier**: "radical," "far outweigh," "small number of individuals," "without inhibiting," "not inconsiderably difficult." Joshua: "radical is not a good choice of words" for a main point answer.
- **Relative versus absolute claims**: the passage says the password solution "would compromise the openness of the web *somewhat*." Answer C's "without inhibiting the rights of others" converts a relativistic claim into an absolute one, which kills it. This was the decisive move of the session.
- **"Moreover" as a rhetorical signpost**: "it tells us what you're about to hear is not the most important thing, it's saying on top of everything else." Used to show that answer C was built on a subordinate point.
- **Narrow but true beats broad but false**: answer A was too narrow and Joshua admitted "on the first pass I would never choose this answer," yet it wins because it directly answers the passage's central question and says nothing untrue.
- **Analogy questions as a deliberate sacrifice**: David applied the prior session's rule and Joshua reinforced it. "They'll take so much more energy and time to not get the question right, and if you do get it right at the cost of another question, you're just trading one for one. It's a dead end."
- **Flagging main point questions as a signal**: David flagged every main point question and got them all right. Joshua treated the flagging itself as diagnostic: "if you have a second guess there even if you don't get it wrong, it tells you a structure issue, understanding how all the pieces are coming together."

## Teaching moves
- Read behavior, not just accuracy. The main-point flagging pattern was the first thing he raised, before any content, because a correct-but-uncertain answer still points at a gap.
- Ran the passage map as a live exercise, one sentence per paragraph, in the student's own words: framing the debate, then the origin of the debate, then the author's solution. Then confirmed and compressed: "first paragraph frames the debate, second zooms in on the origin of the debate, third resolves the central question and gives extra thoughts."
- Withheld his own verdict to keep the student's reasoning uncontaminated: "I should be careful. What do you think about option C, do you think it's right or wrong?" and later "I won't comment on that, we'll recap it after we go through all of them."
- Set a puzzle instead of giving the answer: on option E, "there's another word in here that is kind of too strong as well. I want to see if you can point it out." David found "small number of individuals."
- Pushed for the textual source of a judgment rather than accepting the intuition: "where is it too strong? Where do we get that support from, which line?" This produced the "somewhat" that decided the question.
- Admitted his own pull toward the wrong answer to normalize the difficulty: "C looked reasonable, I won't lie. I was looking at it and thinking about it. But it can't have anything wrong."
- Named the skill on display as decision-making rather than perception: "they didn't get anything past you. You're not missing it based off of just not seeing it. It's about decision-making, saying okay, what do we do when we have no good options."
- Handled the student's life decision by refusing to answer on the spot: "I'm going to write down exactly your thoughts. I'm going to take time to think about what you said, because I want to be very careful with anything I say." Then a follow-up by email rather than an in-session opinion.
- On whether high 170s are reachable, reframed away from ability: "the difference between minus three and minus four on a test, is that because you're better or worse? Not really." Then the encouragement: "most of the hard work is behind you. Now you're just trying to get greedy, get those last few points, maybe even just five or ten percent better. That would push you over the hill."
- Endorsed the break without hedging: "take a break, breathe."

## Worked examples
- **Copyright and web links passage, main point question 15.**
  - Passage map: paragraph one frames the debate over whether copyright law should be strengthened in response to web links; paragraph two lays out the foundation of the debate, whether linking to someone's material makes you liable; paragraph three gives the author's position, that linking is not infringement, using the analogy of sharing a voicemail from a phone anyone can call, and adding that an owner who wants privacy can password-protect the material. David generated his own parallel throughout: reposting a public Instagram photo.
  - B (David's original answer): "changes in copyright law... are ill advised unless such changes amplify rather than restrict the free exchange of ideas necessary in a democracy." First half is a fair restatement. Killed by the conditional: the author never says changes would amplify free exchange, and in fact says strengthening would *impede* the web's development as a public forum. "Democracy" is also out of scope.
  - D: "not inconsiderably difficult to resolve if one applies basic common sense principles." Killed on "common sense" (the author is proposing passwords precisely because people are not doing it) and on scope, since the passage is about links on web pages rather than new media broadly. David also flagged "not inconsiderably" as inflating the difficulty.
  - E: killed on "radical alteration," "far outweigh," and "small number of individuals," none supported.
  - C: "people concerned about access can easily prevent such access without inhibiting the rights of others to exchange ideas freely." Superficially the most attractive and most abstract. Killed because the passage says the solution "would compromise the openness of the web somewhat," so an absolute "without inhibiting" contradicts the text. Also structurally subordinate, introduced with "moreover," and disconnected from paragraph two.
  - A (correct): "since the distribution of documents placed on a web page is controlled by the author of that page, creating such a link should not be considered copyright infringement." Narrow, does not capture the third paragraph's extra thoughts, but directly answers the passage's central question and contains nothing false. David's own summary: "it's narrow, but it's the entire point."
  - David's meta-observation, endorsed by Joshua: "not all main point questions are created equal."
