# David Salgado, 2026-01-11

## LSAT concepts
- **Half-right answer choices**: named as the second most common RC trap after over-strong language. "If the first half of the answer is attractive, be even more cautious, because the second half of the answer is like their favorite trick in the book."
- **Strength of language**: "this RC section is just a game of chicken. Will you bite on an answer choice that is even this slightly too heavy?" Most wrong answers are "just a little bit too much" rather than fully off-scope.
- **Primary purpose**: "these questions are just main point questions in disguise. They're asking you how did the author deliver the main point, more so than what is the main point." Main point answers should stay broad and abstract, so a word like "replaced" is disqualifying where "challenged" would have been fine.
- **Context window / localization before answers**: before reading choices, ask where in the passage a named person's opinion lives. "The moment you look at answer choices you're going to be influenced." Joshua called RC "more like a research style section."
- **Analogy / parallel questions in RC**: judged the lowest-value question type. "They're kind of more vibes... you can't verify those answers." Explicitly designated as the type to sacrifice under time pressure, unlike inference and strongly supported questions, which "you can actually verifiably find in the passage."
- **Strongly supported / inference**: cannot be given up. Process is to prephrase, localize the source of the claim, then cross-reference.
- **Noteworthy-person passages**: a structural rule Joshua stated hard. When a passage centers on a person and their contributions, the author will almost never criticize them, and the correct answer will give a nod to what makes them special. "95% of the time they will never give you negative information about an individual." A sentence that looks like criticism is nearly always clarifying the subject's own view.
- **Attribution, passage author versus subject**: the whole "Kelly is far from claiming" dispute turned on whether the sentence carried the passage author's opinion or clarified Kelly's own position. It was the latter.
- **Concessive markers ("even so")**: normally signal the writer conceding then pushing back, which is exactly why this passage's use of it is a trap.
- **LR versus RC standards**: David's own observation, that "one word could change everything" holds in both, but RC has more exceptions. The word "new" in a correct RC answer was inferable from context even though never stated, which his "LR brain" refused.

## Teaching moves
- Opened by asking David to narrate his own review process, then refused to let him treat the errors as approach failure: "your approach is very good, you're doing very well. It's not going to be whether your approach is good or bad or wrong or right. It's more like how can I sharpen it? It's like taking a sword and saying how can I make this sharp sword even sharper." Explicitly ruled out an overhaul.
- Protected a behavior while correcting the timing of it. On David picking an answer fast: "you don't want to change that, you want to keep that behavior, it's more so the homework upfront."
- Introduced a "heat check" for localization: "where would the gold be in this passage? Having a road map or a skeleton of, okay, the answer has to come from this portion or this portion." Emphasized this is not rereading, so it costs almost no time.
- The Matrix "woman in the red dress" metaphor for attractive wrong answers: "it is very tempting... it perfectly fits in, but just slightly, just one ounce off."
- Gave a budget for acceptable errors rather than demanding perfection: "you want to have an amount that you're allowed to give yourself to make mistakes and say I'm okay if I make this many mistakes. The ones you can't accept to lose are the ones you can verifiably find in the passage."
- Concrete analogy-question protocol: gut check, choose the best-feeling answer, move on, come back with fresh eyes at the end. Rationale: "if you spend three minutes and still get it wrong, you're not only getting that one wrong, you're sacrificing the questions you would have got right."
- Shut down the student's self-discounting on bias, three times in a row: "don't worry about bias, don't let that enter your mind... when you do well you're going to try to find ways to discount it, and when you do poorly you're going to look at it as I knew I wasn't good enough. You can't win."
- Refused vague error language: when David said an answer was "word salad," Joshua pushed back that generic descriptions are "hard to use as a tool to prove. What about it? The words are just longer? The words are convoluted?" and made him name the actual mechanism.
- Ran a paragraph-by-paragraph one-sentence summary exercise to settle a disagreement about the main point, rather than asserting the answer.
- Sustained a long, genuinely contested exchange over "Kelly is far from claiming," including asking David to build a parallel, then supplying his own (the American economy and Donald Trump and tariffs) when David's stalled. He kept restating David's position back to him to check understanding before correcting it.
- Validated the student's reading as reasonable while still overturning it: "you're 100% right that 95% of people would have seen that, because it's bizarre that he would write 'even so' and then not push back... they purposely put it as a last paragraph." Then named the tell: when a criticism appears in a noteworthy-person passage, "spend a minute and a half if not longer really wrestling with it, because that's one in a million."
- Closed with a character read framed as diagnostic: "the worst thing is to be a person that just kind of feels good and goes on. You have a logical reason as to why you would have chosen an answer that was wrong or right, which is a good sign, that's a high level test taker."
- Coached down the intensity of review deliberately: "do your review, but don't get yourself stuck in that review for a week or three, four, five days. We want to be efficient with our time here." Noted he sounded like "a crazy person" telling him to review less.
- On the practice test outcome, decoupled result from process: "regardless of what you do tomorrow, the day after has to be the same. If you do really well it shouldn't make you feel happy, if you do really poorly you shouldn't feel like..." The test is "a reflection point," not a verdict.

## Worked examples
- **Tulip mania passage** (McKay versus the economist Peter Garber).
  - Structure: paragraph one ends with Garber challenging McKay's view that there was no evidence of a bubble; paragraph two is McKay setup; paragraph three carries Garber's own evidence.
  - Strongly supported question: David's prephrase was correct, that original tulip prices were not irrational per Garber, contra McKay. He picked E because "mistakenly infers" matched. E was a half-right trap: it applied the inference to the low-priced bulbs, while Garber's argument concerns the high-priced original bulbs. Correct answer B.
  - Analogy question: David first chose C ("cheap substitute parts") on the knockoff misreading. On reread he saw reproduction means remaking the original bulbs, and that Garber's point involves descendants of the bulbs collectively returning the investment. Correct answer D, selling copies of an original novel, the only choice carrying the descendant element.
- **Human fossils / Darwin passage.**
  - Primary purpose question: David was between D and E, rejected D's "revisionary" as too strong, chose E. Wrong on the single word "replaced," since the new theory only challenges the old one and no consensus is described. "If it had the word challenge, then E would be correct. Describe is correct. Every word is correct. It's just replaced." Correct answer D.
  - Analogy question 17: the element to analogize is that fossils were found in reverse order, latest first, leading Darwin to a wrong inference. David gave it about a minute on the clock and guessed A ("preconceptions destroyed"). In blind review he found B: historians discover an artist's late paintings, and conclude the unrecovered first painting must have been in the same style. He judged the fast guess correct as a time decision.
- **Robin Kelly passage** (African American workers and the Communist Party).
  - Paragraph map built live: paragraph one, Kelly's unique approach focused on how it worked on the ground in Black communities; paragraph two, how Kelly's approach differs from prior scholarship; paragraph three, Kelly's argument that the "third period" was more favorable and served as a vehicle for organizing, against the standard popular-front account; paragraph four, the decline of the party.
  - Main point question: David chose B (drawn to a perceived challenging tone toward Kelly), had earlier considered A (missed the word "fails"). He rejected the correct answer E purely on the word "new," since nothing explicitly says new. Joshua's correction: the passage is a noteworthy-person passage, so the answer must credit what is special about Kelly, and B gives her no praise.
  - The sentence "even so, Kelly is far from claiming that the change to the popular front was the sole reason for the decline" was argued at length. David read it as the passage author pushing back on Kelly. Correct reading: the passage author is clarifying that Kelly herself does not treat it as the sole cause. David eventually found corroborating support elsewhere in the paragraph.
