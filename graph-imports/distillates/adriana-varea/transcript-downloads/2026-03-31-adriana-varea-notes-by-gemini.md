# Adriana Varea, 2026-03-31

## LSAT concepts

- **Flaw questions**: framed as multi-dimensional, because "there's so many types of questions that are embedded into the flaw question." The stated master skill is the answer choices, not the stimulus.
- **Objection-style answer choices**: "overlooks a possibility," "fails to address," "the author overlooks" all mean the identical thing. Test: assume whatever follows the phrase is true, and it should directly attack and destroy the conclusion. Effectively a weaken question.
- **Necessary assumption style answer choices**: "presumes without providing justification," "takes for granted," "fails to establish" all mean necessary assumption. Two-step test: first, in its natural state does it benefit the argument; second, if it passes, negate it and check whether the conclusion collapses. "If it doesn't benefit the argument, you don't have to negate it because it just doesn't make a difference." Note the paired contrast Joshua drew: objection answers should destroy the conclusion in their natural state, necessary assumption answers should support it.
- **"Assume" defaults to necessary assumption**: "if they ever bring up assume on the LSAT think necessary assumption always, that's a standard." Sufficient assumption only when they say so explicitly.
- **Method of reasoning as a filter**: identify how the argument travels from premise to conclusion. The general framework is conditional, causal, and comparative, and "conditional and causal will handle at least 50% of the arguments." Comparative is "kind of the weakest one," to be avoided as a label when a stronger one fits. An argument can have more than one.
- **Causal vocabulary as a tripwire**: words like "phenomenon," "explanation," "leads to" in an answer choice signal causation. "If you see words like phenomenon in an answer choice you want to ask yourself, did I even notice a cause and effect relationship?" If the stimulus had no causation, the choice can be filtered fast.
- **Net effect**: modifier that means all things considered, after the positives are weighed against the negatives. Compared to net income after taxes.
- **Descriptive vs prescriptive**: "should" in an answer choice makes a prescription about what one ought to do. If the stimulus is purely descriptive, that answer is out.
- **Descriptive accuracy as a hard gate**: an answer choice must accurately describe what the author actually did before the flaw itself is even assessed. "Even if it's talking about the right flaw, if it's descriptively inaccurate, we still do not accept it."
- **"Draws a conclusion" is a structural claim**: whatever follows must actually be the conclusion, and whatever follows "based on" must actually be the evidence. A two-part check.
- **Necessary vs sufficient confusion**: named as "the most popular fallacy on the LSAT."
- **"Only if" introduces a necessary condition**: "resign only if convicted" maps to resign, therefore convicted, not convicted, therefore resign.
- **Sampling flaw**: moving from a specific data point to a conclusion assuming the same holds of a larger population. Joshua noted it is rarely a real flaw on its own, "there's no study that takes every person in a population."
- **Percentage versus number**: percentages without a known total are a red flag.
- **Equivocation and ambiguity**: "fails to adequately define the key phrase" is an equivocation fallacy, and equivocation "is very rarely going to be the correct answer for any flaw question." Same for circular reasoning: "it's almost always one of the options, it's almost never the actual right answer."
- **Overly strong answer choices**: "no one can," "cannot" should trigger the same wariness as in necessary assumption. "If there's even one, it'll be okay for the argument."

## Teaching moves

- **Named the session's single skill up front: "deobstractify" answer choices.** He justified the choice by where she is: "when students are usually at the higher end of the 160s, the biggest difference maker I find is perfecting the art of the answer choices... You've figured out the stimulus side, I remember you have a very high reading comprehension." The answer choices are "the last big jump for most students."
- **Taught the vocabulary before opening the stimulus.** He deliberately withheld the argument and drilled the meaning of "overlooks a possibility" and "presumes without providing justification" in the abstract, then quizzed her choice by choice ("Second answer choice, fails to address, is what type? How about D?"). Only then: "now that we understand that, let's go into the stimulus."
- **"Filter out the noise."** The framing is that these choices are "very fluffy... unless you have a framework to approach them, it's a lot of fluff and you're just powering through them with vibes, and then these ones will catch you." The efficiency payoff: "you don't want to think about what is this answer supposed to do, you should know instantly it should do this, and then you could test it."
- **Corrected her without deflating her**, twice on the same instinct. When she guessed the negation test applied to "overlooks a possibility": "No, no, no. You're on the right path. You're thinking about it in the right way... It's actually the opposite." Then immediately turned her error into the setup for the next concept: "what you said kind of led to my next point, which is perfect."
- **Cold solve first, timed.** Every question started with two to two and a half minutes silent and alone, then a confidence percentage before any discussion ("How do you feel?" / "What's the percentage?"). This gave him calibration data separate from correctness.
- **On the hardest question he flipped the format: think aloud in real time**, narrating the approach rather than defending an answer. She produced a several-minute unbroken account of her process, which is what he then diagnosed.
- **Rewarded her for holding her ground.** When she said comparative, he agreed, then she added causal anyway: "I'm very happy you didn't shy away because I said yes to the comparative, but you stood on your guns."
- **Made her verbalize the mapping herself rather than supplying it.** For "a behavior that sometimes leads to a certain phenomenon," he asked "what behavior could be leading to a certain phenomenon in the argument here?" and let her work through a wrong candidate (immoral behavior) to the right one (moral socialization), including her own aside "how is moral socialization a behavior? It's an action" which she then resolved herself.
- **The "not" shortcut** for negating necessary-assumption-style choices: find the answer with "not" in it, read it straight through with the "not" deleted, and ask whether that scenario destroys the conclusion. "All you have to do is read the answer straight through without the not being present."
- **Framed flaw questions as a matching game**: "half the game for flaw questions is matching. If we can't take that and deobstractify it and map it onto the argument, that means the answer won't be correct." And "half of your answer choice time, especially for flaw questions, should be an inspection. Eyes should be going back and forth."
- **Gave a concrete parsing recipe** for a candidate answer choice: look for the adverbs (what actions is the author described as taking), then the nouns and pronouns (does that thing exist in the argument), then ask whether the author actually did that thing as described.
- **"Bring it to life."** For "confuses a theoretical best way with the only way": "you literally just want to bring it to life. What is the theoretical best way?" She filled in deep empathy, then confirmed the author does exactly that.
- **Argued that wrong answers are the training set**, not throwaways: "even the wrong answer choices are very useful... because there'll be another time where that answer choice style will be correct." The goal is pattern recognition, "learning the language, almost like learning a language." And a strong claim about the type: "you should never get flaw questions wrong because they're very provable. They're very mathematical."
- **Split practice mode from test mode explicitly** when Adriana asked whether her "vibes first, map the final two" habit was sloppy. His answer: "What you're doing exactly right now is perfect... you only want to use this when you get to the last two answer choices where you go into deep dive. If you did that for every answer choice you'd be exhausted." But in drilling, "you might as well just work that muscle out till you get exhausted."
- **Taught educated guessing under time**: with multiple conditionals in the stimulus, "it's very unlikely that they're not going to appeal to the conditional flaw... if I had to guess without even looking at it, I'll look for an answer that talks about conditionals." Immediately hedged it: "does that mean I would want to live and die by that? Probably not."
- **The sports anchor analogy** for descriptive accuracy: an answer choice is like a highlights reel, it has to describe what actually happened before you judge anything else.
- **Justified drilling only level 5 questions**, the homework rationale: "that's why you practice the hardest questions, because the easiest questions are not any different. They're just the same variables dialed up a notch." After level 5, easier ones feel like "you have ankle weights on."
- **Read her conditional reversal back in plain speech** rather than notation: "They're saying if you resign, then the only reason why it should be is because you're convicted. Doesn't mean that everybody that believes you're convicted should have resigned. There's a reversing of the conditions there. Very sneaky."
- **Ended on a win deliberately**: "it's good to leave on that note because that was a level five question." Plus a practical tip drawn from it: do a couple of questions before starting a section "to get yourself in that hole" because momentum is real.
- **Reassured on the accommodations process** with specific procedural detail rather than encouragement: get the doctor to write it down, "it doesn't cost her anything," and "you're more the norm rather than the exception. Double time is super normal."

## Worked examples

**Question 1, flaw, moral socialization (level 4 or 5).** Stimulus: society socializes children by shaming immoral behavior; in many people the shame produces guilt and self-loathing that can be severe hardship; therefore moral socialization has had a net effect of increasing total suffering. Adriana chose C at 75% confidence. Path: identify conclusion, then isolate "net effect" as the load-bearing modifier meaning the two variables are weighed against each other. Negative effect is guilt and self-loathing; positive effect is a decrease in immoral behavior. Structure is both comparative and causal. Gap: the argument assumes the negatives outweigh the positives. C fails because it introduces "should be changed or eliminated," a prescriptive claim absent from a purely descriptive stimulus; Adriana had noticed this herself but picked C anyway because it was the only choice mentioning beneficial consequences. D, the correct answer, reads "takes for granted that a behavior that sometimes leads to a certain phenomenon cannot also significantly reduce the overall occurrence of that phenomenon." She had rejected it by mismapping the phenomenon as guilt and self-loathing. Correct mapping: behavior equals moral socialization, phenomenon equals suffering. Delete the "not": moral socialization can also reduce the overall occurrence of suffering. If true, the benefits outweigh the costs and the net effect claim dies. E was rejected as too strong ("no one can have a positive reaction").

**Question 2, flaw, psychologists and understanding.** Conclusion: psychologists who think understanding is achieved only through a complete and direct grasp of a person's motivations are wrong. Adriana chose C at 75% and was correct. She articulated the flaw herself as confusing a necessary condition for a sufficient one; Joshua restated it as "the best way to do X is impossible, therefore X is impossible," and, "because we don't have the best way doesn't mean there's no way." Verified C by mapping: theoretical best way equals deep empathy, that is a direct and complete grasp of motivations; only way equals what the author treats it as. Answer A ("fails to adequately define the key phrase") was diagnosed as an equivocation fallacy, a type that is almost never correct.

**Question 3, flaw, survey on resignation (called the hardest of the day).** Stimulus: 50% of those polled believe elected officials should resign if indicted, whereas 35% believe they should resign only if convicted. Conclusion: more people believe officials should resign if indicted than believe they should resign if convicted. Adriana chose A at 0% confidence. Two red flags surfaced: percentages with no stated total ("could be literally two people"), and indictment (a formal accusation, charge pressed, not guilty or innocent) versus conviction. Diagrams: if indicted, then resign; and resign, then convicted. The flaw is the conclusion silently reversing the second into if convicted, then resign, which the stimulus never supports. A fails on two counts: sampling is not inherently a flaw, and it is descriptively inaccurate about both the conclusion and the evidence. B, the conditional answer, is correct and is also what educated guessing would have produced.

**Question 4, method of argument, Warhol's Brillo Boxes (level 5).** Warhol's Brillo Boxes is visually indistinguishable from the actual product packaging; his is considered art, an identical stack of ordinary boxes is not; therefore appearance alone does not entirely determine whether something is a work of art. Adriana narrowed to A and E at 10% confidence and chose E, correctly. She rejected A because the argument highlights how similar the two things are, not how they differ. She mapped E ("showing that something would be impossible if a particular thesis were true"): if appearance alone determined art status, the Brillo Boxes being art while identical boxes are not would be impossible. Joshua's added explanation of why A attracts: A describes an argument that shows people are wrong about what art is, a belief versus reality structure, but this argument accepts everyone's judgment and points out the two still look the same.
