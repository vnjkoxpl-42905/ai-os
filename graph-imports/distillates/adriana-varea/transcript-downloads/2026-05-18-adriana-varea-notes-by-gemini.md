# Adriana Varea, 2026-05-18

A 93-minute RC session opening on a disappointing 161 practice test. Adriana chose RC because she has cycled through four annotation systems without one sticking. Joshua taught his V1/V2 vertical passage map, then used a Dutch tulip passage to teach the session's big idea: on RC, pick the answer with the lowest burden of evidence.

## LSAT concepts

- **Burden of evidence / the weightlifter.** The session's central metaphor. "If you were to think of it from the perspective of a weightlifter — the Olympic weightlifter is the passage, and the weight that needs to be lifted above its head is the answer choice. In a perfect world you'd want the weightlifter to be really big and strong and the weight to be very light... You want the answer choices to be as light as possible for the passage to be able to carry them." The rule: "The answer choices that are stronger, or go on a bigger limb, or are more conceptually strong, are more dangerous. You want the answer to be delicate."
- **Language is a proxy for strength, not a substitute for scope.** "The one that's the least strong in the sense of the *scope*. Language is a huge indicator of strength — not one for one, but a very strong indicator."
- **Conditionals in answer choices are dangerous by construction.** "Whenever you see conditionals, by definition it's very strong... The *if* has to actually be able to be triggered for it to be even relevant at all. If the if is not relevant, the conditional completely goes to garbage. There's no rectifying it." And: "Think of them as very niche answers. They require very specific preconditions." Contrast: "Any choice that gets into a nitty-gritty small scope — versus one that stays broad — we love [the broad ones], because it's hard to falsify a claim where there are no hard lines."
- **"Most" is always strong.** "Most is always going to be strong... most is really very dangerous." Adriana's operating definition: "Every time I see *most*, I think of 51%." Joshua: "Or at least — no, you're correct."
- **Read every answer as a claim a real person made.** "All of this RC passage and the answer choices, they are written by a real person. These are real things. These are not just made up ideas... Every single time you look at an answer, you need to ask yourself: **would a reasonable person in the regular world agree with this?** They're all intelligent people. Never going to be some just crazy bozo."
- **Infer means must be true.** "Infer means must be true. It does not mean [what] people [naturally think]... unless he explicitly said it, we can't [assume it]. If they don't explicitly say it, then you're damn sure he didn't infer it." The qualifier matters: "If you see a question that says *this passage supports the inference*, that means it has to be 100% explicitly stated. It cannot just be that you loosely come to it." Here the stem said *most strongly supports the inference*, which "does protect it — they're qualifying it."
- **Never eliminate what you can't disqualify.** "If you're not able to explicitly disqualify an answer, you should never eliminate it. Even if there's an answer that seems better... If I can't tell you why the answer's wrong, I'm not allowed to eliminate it, because I can't just get to the answer by process of elimination." The test: "If I had to explain it to somebody that did not read this passage, I have to be able to articulate it."
- **Disqualify, don't select.** "When you're choosing answers, you're looking to not find the right answer. You're looking to disqualify answers... The one that has the least amount of issues with it, that's going to be the correct answer. RC, this is monumental. LR is important. RC is *monumental*."
- **Main point in RC works exactly like main point in LR.** "LR and RC are very similar. People try to separate them as different things. They're really the same thing. If the author tells you *the economists believe X but they are wrong*, the main point is that it's not the case that what the economists believe is true." The difference: "What RC really does differently is that instead of just saying one side believes this and they're wrong, they'll tell you nuance — they believe this, they came from this school, they went to this, they're from this country. But really, simply put, they're just saying that they're wrong."
- **Main point answers stay broad but must nod to a side.** "The main point answer should always tend to stay broad, but the one thing you can never negotiate is that it has to give a nod to the side he agrees with."
- **Passage templates.** "There's templates... when you identify the template, it makes the ability to identify all the other things that fall under it [easier], because it's repetitive. Think of it as buckets versus individual passages."
- **The author almost always takes a side.** "Whenever you see two points of view, always assume that the author is 100% on the side of one of the views, even though he might not sound like it. Don't take him being shy as being not completely on one side." Frequency: "90% of the time." The rule of thumb: "Whichever side he talks about more, he devotes more text to — he's on that side." The other case: "Sometimes he'll disagree with both. They believe this, they believe that, but they're both wrong — and *this* is the real reason. And that's the main point."
- **The two-cavemen frame.** "You want to turn it into almost like two cavemen arguing whenever there's two sides. Good, bad, very good, very bad. It makes it so you don't fall into the gray area."
- **RC question scope taxonomy.** Three buckets: "broad scope answer choices where the answer could come from anywhere in the passage, more localized which is going to be from a certain paragraph, and then the very specific ones where they ask you what a sentence's function is."
- **The split (dual-passage) approach depends on question-type mastery.** "You have to have a really good understanding of question types for RC to be able to really use that, because if you don't, you don't even know what you could potentially do with each passage." A main point question about passage A "you should never have to read passage B to answer."
- **The goal on a split pass is elimination, not selection.** "Another mistake a lot of students make with the dual passage approach is they think they have to answer the question when they're going through with the first passage. **Your goal isn't to get the right answer. It's to set you up to get the right answer.** Which ones can I get rid of? If he brings it down to two answer choices, then when you go to passage B it's a piece of cake."
- **Pre-phrase before reading answers.** "The biggest danger is jumping into the questions without anticipating. One of the biggest skills you can develop for these questions is having a clear anticipation of what the answers should look like, and almost verbalizing that prior to going through the answers, because the answers will play with your mind."
- **Main point vs main purpose.** "Purpose, main purpose, main point — they're nearly the same thing. There's some nuance there you want to understand from a broader perspective, but in a practical sense they really are the same thing."

## Teaching moves

- **Find the accuracy/speed sweet spot empirically.** The homework framing: "There is a time on the clock where you will say, okay, 60 minutes I always get minus two or minus one. But when I get to 55 minutes I notice my accuracy might drop... Take as long as you need, have the clock on, pretend like it's not there, but check how long it took you, and try to be as efficient as possible without sacrificing even 1% of accuracy — and see where that sweet spot is for you."
- **Ask for her read before giving his.** "I always like to ask you first before I give you my two cents. I noticed something actually pretty interesting, but I want you to give me your two cents." He never did give his observation.
- **Normalize the bad score by naming the section's reputation.** "The 150s [PrepTests] — I always say to every call, every session I say the same thing — the LR sections turn boys into men, girls into women. It's brutal. They're the hardest versions of what you'll see. You won't see a section on test day that will be as difficult."
- **Refuse to uproot what works.** "It's not like I want to revamp your approach and completely uproot it, because it works for you. So I want to be careful with not throwing too many foreign ideas."
- **Live-annotate on the shared screen** to demo the map, correcting himself mid-demo ("I actually lied to you. I wouldn't say P1 P2, I'll say V1 V2 — viewpoint one, viewpoint two") — P1/P2 is reserved for paragraphs in the vertical column.
- **Keep the map deliberately impoverished.** "You don't want to overdo it, because it's supposed to be a 10 to 30 second thing. You're just doing it to keep an idea of the structure so you know where to look when they ask you certain questions." And the reason students over-annotate: "A lot of students try to remember all of the things in the passage. It's natural to think you should know what the passage says in and out. But realistically, the passage is not going anywhere. The passage is going to stay there."
- **Highlighter-per-speaker.** "Having a highlighter color for each person that's speaking — yellow for one, orange for the second. So when they're switching perspectives, you know where your eyes are to go... You just have to highlight the first word. You don't have to highlight the whole paragraph, just: this is where he starts talking."
- **Hold her to her answer.** When she tried to switch off B mid-analysis: "Nope. Nope. You have to stick with me. I'm holding you to it." Later, after she vindicated herself: "So B was right." — "Yeah, don't do it. Don't change it. That's why I was like, no, hold on to it."
- **Make her rank all five by strength** rather than argue each one. This converted a question she "hates" into a mechanical exercise and got her to eliminate C on her own by applying the conditionals rule she'd just learned.
- **Take an answer choice literally to absurdity.** On (C): "If there's no speculative bubble, then in every other scenario in every single world possible, it's impossible for a market price to be wrong." Adriana: "Oh, and that's just absurd." Joshua: "Insane. What if I'm a dictator? What if I just say everybody's paying a million dollars for water?... If somebody said that in real life, you'd look at them like I don't want to talk to you ever again, because you're crazy."
- **Same move on (D)**: "Rational *what*? In which investments? All investments. How insane of a claim is that? Every investment they've ever made in their life was rational. Not one exception." He named the trap: "It sounds like a reasonable person hears that and says, I get what you mean. The LSAT doesn't want you to think that. They want you to think of it very literally."
- **Zoom to the one word that makes an answer weak.** On (B), he stopped her mid-doubt: "There's something you're missing here. Very, very, very important... What follows that?" — *could*. "What does could mean? 1%. If there's a 1% possibility, that's correct. Extremely weak." He then stacked the weakness: "Not only that they would have got it back, but *could have expected* to get it back — even lesser than that, they played it even safer... It's almost like he's fence sitting."
- **Give permission to lose questions.** On analogy questions: "They're often not even worth going to. You do not want to lose yourself in them, because you can spend as long as you want on them and sometimes they still don't make any sense. You want to let these ones go... they're worth as much as any other question. Sometimes it's worth not losing the big picture." And the sunk-cost math when she realized her answer was wrong but stayed committed: "I actually would prefer you to do that. It doesn't make sense to lose one question to get one question right, and spend the energy for a question that you may or may not get right, sacrificing ones that you know you would get right. That's just a silly game."
- **Convert her self-diagnosis into a data assignment.** "Go back to the passage you just did recently, look at the ones you got wrong, and analyze the ones you said are strong or weak. You'll see a common trend — the weakest answer, the one that's the most vague, the one that stays away from falsifiable propositions."
- **Flatter to raise the bar.** "You're an LR master. So when you hear *most strongly supports*, what comes to mind?" (She blanked: "I think everything I've ever learned about LR has left my brain." He absorbed it: "No, fair. I'm putting you on the spot.")
- **Volunteer his own uncertainty.** He mixed up McKay and Garber mid-explanation and she caught it: "Wait, isn't it Garber?" — "Yeah, you got it right. My bad."

## Worked examples

- **PT126, Section 2, Passage 4 — the Dutch tulip market** (six-minute timed read, Joshua silent). Structure: paragraph 1 defines a speculative bubble and introduces the McKay/Garber clash, closing with Garber's challenge; paragraph 2 develops McKay's case that the 17th-century Dutch tulip market was a prime speculative bubble; paragraph 3 develops Garber's counterargument from flower-growing fundamentals — descendant bulbs became more readily available through reproduction of the original, so prices fell rapidly, but earnings derivable from the millions of descendant bulbs could be very high even at low individual prices. Author sides with Garber.
  - **Main point.** Adriana initially gave "the author is generally neutral, maybe leaning towards Garber a little bit, but very soft." Joshua pushed her to name a line the way she would in LR; she picked "there's no evidence that the tulip market really involved a speculative bubble." He accepted it while sharpening: "The main point of the passage is saying that there was no bubble. In a weird way that's kind of the same thing as saying there's no evidence."
  - **Question 1, main point, answer (D)** — juxtaposes the two opinions and states that Garber argues the speculative bubble did not exist. Adriana got it in under a minute and eliminated the alternative on descriptive accuracy: "he doesn't say it's *widely and mistakenly* believed. That just didn't feel descriptively accurate." Joshua noted this passage was unusual: "Surprisingly, because the author was very neutral — it's very rare the author will be this neutral with their main point."
  - **Question 2, analogy — the one she got wrong.** Stem: which is most analogous to someone who bought a tulip bulb of a certain variety at a very high price only to sell a bulb of that variety at a much lower price? She chose (C) (rare box of motorcycle parts bought high, forced to sell low because of cheap substitute parts), reasoning by structural shape — one expensive thing, then market oversaturation. Correct answer (D): a publisher who pays an extremely high price for a new novel only to sell copies at a price affordable to nearly everyone. She had misread (D) on the first pass as the publisher buying many copies. The pre-phrase Joshua supplied and she had missed: **"I'm going to pay a ton up front, but I'll make my money back by selling a lot of the copies of the original at a very cheap price."** She admitted she "completely disregarded that last sentence" of the passage — the one about earnings derivable from the descendants — which is what the analogy turns on. She caught the error mid-explanation on the second read and still declined to change: "I already committed to C."
  - **Question 3, most strongly supported inference about Garber — answer (B).** She had it right and nearly talked herself out of it. Localization first: Garber's views appear only at the end of paragraph 1 and throughout paragraph 3, so only those areas need checking. Then the strength ranking:
    - (A) if speculative bubbles occur at all, they occur very rarely — she killed it fast: we only know Garber's view on tulips, not bubbles in general. Also a conditional.
    - (C) if there is not a speculative bubble in a market, then price levels in that market are not irrational — the strongest of the five; a conditional, and taken literally it says no market price can ever be wrong absent a bubble.
    - (D) most 17th-century Dutch tulip investors were generally rational in *all* their investments — "most" plus "all investments"; an extreme claim. Adriana had also raised the sharper point that "not irrational does not necessarily mean rational... not irrational could mean neutral." Joshua granted the logic in the abstract ("You could get there. I like the way you're thinking") but ruled it inapplicable here because Garber's actual reasoning tells us he thinks the prices made sense.
    - (E) McKay mistakenly infers from tulip prices dropping rapidly that the very low prices were irrational — unsupported: the passage never tells us what McKay thinks about the *low* prices. Plus "infer" is a must-be-true word.
    - (B) many of the owners of high-priced original tulip bulbs could have expected to at least recoup their original investments — correct, because *many* and *could* are both extremely soft. "Many can literally be two people," said Adriana.
