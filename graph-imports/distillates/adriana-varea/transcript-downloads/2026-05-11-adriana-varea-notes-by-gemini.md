# Adriana Varea, 2026-05-11

## LSAT concepts

- **Three categories of flaw answer choice**: objection, assumption, and descriptive. Objection phrasings ("the author ignores the possibility," "fails to consider") ask what happens to the conclusion if the statement is true, a weakening test. Assumption phrasings ("presumes without providing justification," "takes for granted") are necessary assumptions, tested by benefit first, then negation. Descriptive answers characterize the argument's structure and always point at both the conclusion and the evidence.
- **Two-step check for assumption-style answers**: first, does it benefit the argument? If not, delete it, no negation needed. Only if it benefits do you negate. "You don't have to start dancing with it prior to, if it just clearly is not the right answer."
- **Too strong for a necessary assumption**: an answer can benefit the argument and still be wrong because the author does not need to go that far. A conclusion about what is "sometimes" true does not require ruling out all counterexamples.
- **Opposite assumption**: an answer stating what the author assumes the reverse of. Test by asking simply "does he have to accept this to be true?"
- **Mismatch / equivocation between concepts**: an argument can swap one term for a similar one, here "moral duty to prevent harm" for "morally right to obstruct police." The correct answer must expose that specific swap.
- **Do not fight the stipulated facts**: when the stimulus says something is known, it is known. Answers that attack a stipulated premise "bait intuition" by asking you to be realistic. "You're not here to fight the facts."
- **"Infer" means conclude**: taking the evidence and deriving what must be true. Adriana defined it herself as taking the facts one step further; Joshua walked her to the realization that this describes the conclusion.
- **Evidence indicators inside answer choices**: "from," "by," "merely because," "on the grounds that" all introduce the evidence half of a descriptive answer choice. "Infer," "draws a conclusion," and "rejects a view" introduce the conclusion half. This lets you split any descriptive answer into two testable halves.
- **Definitive language mismatch**: an answer claiming the author concludes something "cannot occur" or "must be the case" is wrong when the conclusion only says the risk is "exaggerated" or "unlikely."
- **Part to whole**: safe at each individual stage does not mean safe across the whole journey. "Each individual stage maybe has a 20% chance... but if you do that 10 times over, you're in a bad spot."
- **Broad generalization**: "sometimes" in a conclusion is evidence against calling it a broad generalization.
- **Argument scope**: an argument that conditions on a fact ("when parents know the child is innocent") is not damaged by cases outside that condition.

## Worked examples

**Question 1, flaw, dairy and heart disease (correct, chose A, confident).** Stimulus: avoiding fat increases the probability of avoiding heart disease; one is less likely to eat fat if one avoids dairy; thus the probability of maintaining good health is increased by avoiding dairy foods. Adriana identified the shift from avoiding heart disease to good health, and reasoned that eliminating dairy could bring its own harms such as calcium deficiency. A is correct: the argument ignores the possibility that even though a practice may have negative consequences, its elimination may also have negative consequences. She eliminated B (the stimulus never claims this is the only way), C (it speaks to negative consequences that ought to be eliminated, not what the stimulus addresses, though it was her second most tempting), D (the evidence is clearly relevant), and E (the argument already speaks only of probability). Joshua's efficiency overlay: on seeing "the argument ignores the possibility," immediately treat what follows as an objection and ask only whether it means anything for the highlighted conclusion.

**Question 2, flaw, obstructing police (missed the reasoning, chose B correctly but unsure).** Stimulus: in all cultures it is almost universally accepted that one has a moral duty to prevent members of one's family from being harmed; thus few would deny that if a person is known by their parents to be falsely accused of a crime, it would be morally right for the parents to hide the accused from the police; hence it is also likely to be widely accepted that it is sometimes morally right to obstruct the police in their work.

The flaw, which Adriana ultimately articulated herself: "it is almost universally accepted" maps cleanly onto "it is likely to be widely accepted," but "moral duty to prevent members of one's family from being harmed" is swapped for "morally right to obstruct the police." A duty is not a right, and a moral duty is not the only moral duty; other principles such as justice could override it. Joshua's alternative-world move produced the same thing: just because it is a universally accepted moral duty does not mean we have the moral right to obstruct police.

B is correct: it fails to consider that other moral principles would be recognized as overriding any obligation to protect a family member from harm. A rejected as a descriptive flaw (single example justifying a broad generalization) since "sometimes" in the conclusion is not a broad generalization. C rejected as the opposite assumption, since the author's whole position is that obstructing is right. D rejected as too strong for a necessary assumption: "there is no moral obligation to obey the law" would benefit the argument but the author only needs the "sometimes" case. E rejected on scope: the stimulus stipulates the parents know the accusation is false, so parental error is outside the argument's domain. Adriana's counterfactual, confirmed by Joshua: if the stimulus had said "thought to be falsely accused," E would apply.

**Question 3, flaw, Mars exploration (level 5, correct, chose A).** A leading critic contends it would be wrong given current technology to send explorers to Mars since they would be unlikely to survive the trip. The author replies that this exaggerates the risk, because there would be a well-engineered backup system at every stage of the long and complicated journey, and a fatal catastrophe is quite unlikely at any given stage with such a backup in place. Adriana identified the part-to-whole flaw immediately: the critic speaks of the whole trip, the author of individual stages. A is correct.

The efficiency pass on the wrong answers, splitting each on its indicator word: B infers something "cannot occur" from evidence that it is unlikely, but the conclusion says only "exaggerates"; C draws a conclusion about what "must be the case" from evidence about what is probably the case; D infers something "will work" merely because it "could work," again too definitive; E "rejects a view" (the conclusion) "on the grounds that" (the evidence), but the author does not reject the view outright. All four die on conclusion mismatch alone, without reading their evidence halves closely.
