# Adriana Varea, 2026-04-07

## LSAT concepts

- **Role questions (role of a claim)**: the goal is never the general character of the isolated statement. "In every single role and method question, this is fundamentally the goal. Your goal is to ask yourself in relation specifically to the conclusion, what is the relationship between the two?"
- **Main conclusion vs intermediate conclusion**: an argument can contain more than one conclusion; the "why should I believe that?" test run in both directions is what separates them.
- **"Thus" in the final sentence is usually bait**: "it's unlikely the conclusion is going to be the last sentence with a keyword like thus... And if they do make it the last sentence, it's not going to have the word thus." Joshua then watched this heuristic fail on the live question and reversed himself twice.
- **Generalization, strict LSAT definition**: inferring a rule from a specific individual finding. "If I see two swans that are white and then I make a statement to say all swans are white." A generalization requires both an individual finding and the broader claim built off it. A bare broad claim is not a generalization.
- **Explanation implies causation**: "explanation" in an answer choice requires a cause and effect relationship in the stimulus. Adriana used this to eliminate a very attractive wrong answer.
- **Opposing viewpoint as a role category**: the roles available are not only premise, background, and conclusion. Someone else's conclusion is its own category.
- **Assumptions are unstated by definition**, the point that unlocked the second question's correct answer.
- **Necessary vs sufficient assumption**: Adriana distinguished the two live while diagnosing which assumption the economist actually needs.
- **Premise vs the premise**: a claim can support a conclusion in conjunction with other premises without being the sole support. "It's not saying it's the sole premise. The other premises in conjunction with this premise support that conclusion."

## Teaching moves

- **Reframed the "I always pick the wrong one of the last two" complaint as a memory bias.** "Every time you get down to the last two answers and it goes right, nobody counts those. You only remember the ones you missed." He then distinguished cases: for weaker students it is an excuse, "for you you're pretty advanced, so it's probably due to what you're saying."
- **The least-wrong rule, stated as a mantra he lives by**: "you're not allowed to choose an answer choice unless you're 100% sure it's right... The best way to do it is not to think from the perspective of why is that answer choice right? Which one's more right? You want to think about the one that's least wrong. Make a list of the flaws with each answer choice and the one with the shorter list... is almost certainly going to be the answer."
- **The underlining protocol for role questions**: underline the target statement in both the question stem and the stimulus, and highlight the conclusion in yellow. His justification is cognitive load, not neatness: "it's taking off a tab, there's tabs in your brain, and you want to be able to not have all these tabs running so your eyes can just dart back and forth."
- **Conceded he was wrong, in detail, twice, and made the concession into the lesson.** After telling her the last sentence could not be the conclusion, he read it again: "You actually might be right... I agree. No, you're right. No, I'm wrong. Completely wrong." He drew the moral against his own heuristic: "it's a testament to engaging with it rather than looking for keywords. - **Warned her explicitly that his agreement is not endorsement**: "I often have a habit of when I sound like I'm going towards a direction and I can trick it, seems like I'm almost tricking students. - **Let her exhaust herself on purpose.** When she said "I'm really talking circles now, I'm throwing myself for a loop," his answer was "it's good, because I want you to kind of exhaust yourself through this process."
- **Localized the bottleneck as a diagnostic step**: "we need to identify where is the part where there's the bottleneck. - **Named her own move back to her.** The "why should I believe that?" test: "this is like, you should trademark this, the Adriana move." ## Worked examples

**Question 1, role of a claim, political rhetoric (called ruthless, neither had seen it).** Stimulus: people's political behavior frequently does not match their rhetoric. Although many complain about government intervention in their lives, they tend not to reelect inactive politicians. A politician's activity consists largely in the passage of laws whose enforcement affects voters' lives. Thus voters often reelect politicians whose behavior they resent. The claim at issue: "they tend not to reelect inactive politicians." Adriana was between B and D and chose D.

Structure settled after extended back and forth: the first sentence is the main conclusion; the final "thus" sentence is an intermediate conclusion, an unusual construction that broke Joshua's own heuristic. Her test in both directions: "voters often reelect politicians whose behavior they resent, why should I believe that? Because political behavior doesn't match rhetoric" ran both ways at first, resolved by noticing "many complain" supplies the resentment. Plain restatement Joshua gave: people say leave me alone, but they vote for the interfering politicians, so what they say and what they do do not match.

D was wrong on the strict definition of generalization: no specific individual finding is present to generalize from. B was right: the claim is a premise supporting the intermediate conclusion, one premise among several rather than the sole support.

**Question 2, role of a claim, price gouging (level 5).** Consumer advocate: economists reason that price gouging (raising prices when no alternative seller is available) is efficient because it allocates goods to people whose willingness to pay more shows they really need those goods. But willingness to pay is not proportional to need. In the real world, some people simply cannot pay as much as others. As a result, a price increase will allocate goods to the people with the most money, not to those with the most need. Claim at issue: "but willingness to pay is not proportional to need." Adriana chose B and was wrong.

Roles mapped: sentence 1 is an opposing viewpoint, the economists' conclusion, not background. Sentence 2 is an intermediate conclusion. Sentence 3 is a premise supporting it. The final sentence is the main conclusion, confirmed with "why should I believe that?" chained backward through the argument, and confirmed negatively when running it the other way produced circular support.

Answer choices as Adriana eliminated them: "disputes one explanation to make way for an alternative explanation" out because explanation means causation and there is none (Joshua expected this to trap her); "a component of reasoning disputed in the argument" out because the disputed reasoning is the economists' claim about efficiency, and the target statement is the advocate's own; "a general principle whose validity the argument questions" out. Correct answer D: "it denies a claim that the argument takes to be assumed in the reasoning that it rejects." The unstated assumption in the economists' reasoning is that willingness to pay is proportional to need, that is, if you are willing to pay more it automatically means you need the item more. The target statement denies exactly that.
