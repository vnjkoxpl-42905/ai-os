# Adriana Varea, 2026-04-20

## LSAT concepts

- **Causal reasoning** as the session's diagnosed weakness, "the common theme, the thread that was tying all these questions together."
- **Implicit causal claims**: any word signaling influence is a causal claim. "Helps doesn't sound like oh, I'm making a causal claim, but anything that has influence on something else is a claim of causation." The test "loves to hide behind causality with innocent sounding words."
- **Experiments are always causal**: "science is a study of causation. Period. That's all it is. A hypothesis is a proposed causal claim." So the words "in an experiment" almost exclusively signal causation.
- **Relative claims cannot support absolute claims**: group A having less of something than group B does not establish that A improved. Both could have risen, one just less. "Any relative claim is always going to be problematic to make an absolute statement."
- **Comparative reasoning and causal reasoning are intertwined**: a comparison is only fair if all variables are isolated, which is "almost theoretically impossible." Therefore the manipulation is always one of two moves: give an unfair upside to the preferred option, or hide a downside of it. Equivalently, omit a secret upside of the option the author rejects.
- **Comparison need not be two groups**: it can be one scenario versus another, for example a world with a proposed law versus a world without it.
- **Necessary assumption, two forms**: the negative form rules out a potential objection (test by negation, since "every argument with an assumption has to be assuming that X bad thing won't happen"); the positive form supplies a missing link, an extra premise connecting the evidence to the conclusion. The link most commonly bridges premise to conclusion, but can bridge premise to intermediate conclusion, and occasionally two premises (Adriana raised this and Joshua confirmed it).
- **The "not" scan**: on necessary assumption questions, scan the choices for the word "not" first, delete the "not," and check whether the resulting statement destroys the conclusion.
- **Negation of quantifiers**: negation of "no" is "some" or "at least one"; of "some" is "none"; of "most" is 50% or lower; of "all" is "some are not" or "at least one is not."
- **Scope shift**: removing one incentive is not the same as guaranteeing protection.
- **False binary / false dilemma**: assuming only two options exist when a middle ground is available.
- **Prescriptive conclusions**: a "should" conclusion proposes a plan, which means assuming the world with the plan beats the world without it, which is a comparison.
- **Necessary assumption answers often look trivially obvious**: "the more duh the answer is for necessary assumption, the better."

## Worked examples

**Question 1, necessary assumption, aerobics and stress (level 5, missed).** Volunteers assigned to aerobics classes or weight training classes; after three months each performed an arduous mathematical calculation; just after, measurable stress symptoms of the aerobics volunteers were less than those of the weight training volunteers; this is good evidence that aerobic exercise helps the body handle psychological stress. Flags: "in an experiment" signals causation, and "helps" is an implicit causal claim. Objections raised: the weight training group may have done cardio independently; the result is relative, so aerobics may have done nothing while weight training raised stress.

Adriana had chosen B ("volunteers assigned to the aerobics class did not also lift weights outside of class"). Wrong because the extra weightlifting in the aerobics group does not cancel aerobics; the vulnerable direction is the weight training group doing aerobics. A was eliminated ("three months is enough time to fully benefit") as irrelevant, since partial benefit suffices. C was eliminated as helpful but not required. D was eliminated because difficulty is not stress. E is correct: on average the aerobics group got a greater amount of aerobic exercise overall, including outside class, than the weight training group. This is exactly the obvious-sounding assumption that locks the comparison in place.

**Question 2, necessary assumption, maximum wage law (very long stimulus and answer choices, missed).** Conclusion: the wages of many of the lowest paid corporate employees would be protected from cuts by enacting a maximum wage law capping executive earnings at, say, 50 times the lowest paid employees' earnings. Evidence: some executives cut employee pay to boost profits and their own salaries, and a maximum wage law would remove that incentive. Gap: removing one incentive is not protection; other reasons to cut wages remain. Adriana chose C, about executives raising wages, which is out of scope since the conclusion is only about protection from cuts; she recognized this mid-explanation. Correct answer E, negated: if such a law were enacted, one or more executives would still cut pay and benefits. If zero executives change behavior, no one is protected, so the conclusion collapses.

**Question 3, necessary assumption, no-till farming (done live in two minutes, correct).** Conclusion, prescriptive: farmers should strive to incorporate no-till methods. Evidence: deep tillage is destroying the world's topsoil supply, and no-till methods are less likely to lead to topsoil erosion. Flaw: false binary, only deep tilling or zero tilling considered, ignoring shallow or medium tilling. Adriana scanned for "not" answers and found A and C. A ("topsoil erosion does not make farmers want to till more deeply") eliminated as farmer psychology, out of scope for a prescriptive claim. C is correct: negated, "tilling by any method other than deep tillage is a viable option" opens the middle ground and destroys the case for striving specifically for no-till.
