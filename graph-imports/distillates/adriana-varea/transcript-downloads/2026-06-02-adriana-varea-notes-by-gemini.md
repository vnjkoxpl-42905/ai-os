# Adriana Varea, 2026-06-02

Final session before Adriana's June 6 LSAT, spent entirely on Reading Comprehension. Joshua taught the CEER method (Comparison, Explanation, Evaluation, Recommendation) for the first time, then applied it live to two level-5 passages she had missed, and closed with the taper plan for test week.

## LSAT concepts
- **CEER method** — the core framework of the session. Every sentence, paragraph, and passage is performing one of four rhetorical moves: **Comparison**, **Explanation**, **Evaluation**, **Recommendation**. "When you ask yourself what is the author doing at all times of the passage, you want to be able to answer that question at least for that paragraph, in that sentence." Every unit gets tagged with one move, two at most.
  - **Comparison** — "the author is comparing two ideas, two theories, two viewpoints or groups."
  - **Explanation** — "when the author is just explaining a topic, concept, or problem or a theory or how something came to pass... building up an idea or giving you background details."
  - **Evaluation** — "the author will criticize a position... goes on to assess or judge or review a certain topic matter." Can be positive as well as negative.
  - **Recommendation** — proposing a solution. "Recommendation, think about it as his solution-proposing. Whenever the author proposes a solution, that's a recommendation. He's not just saying your side is bad, your side is good. He's saying what we should do to solve this problem."
- **Evaluation vs. recommendation** — the one distinction Joshua treats as load-bearing. Adriana produced it herself: "recommendation would be something prescriptive whereas evaluation would be something descriptive." Joshua's version: "to criticize something is one thing, but to say therefore you should do this thing is one other extra step... you're actually positing a position versus just criticizing a position." He also frames it as posture: "He's not playing defense... He's actually going on offense."
- **Main point lives in the highest move present** — "if the author stops at evaluation, the main point should be the evaluation. If the author gives you an evaluation but then gives you a recommendation, then the main point should be the recommendation."
- **Primary purpose = main point** — "primary purpose is just another fancy way of saying main point. The main point is the content of the purpose" — the move is the purpose, the compared items are the topic.
- **Build or pivot** — the sentence-level fallback when a sentence will not resolve. "Every sentence is either going to be building on the sentence prior to it or it's going to be pivoting away." Used instead of rereading.
- **Hypothesis-driven reading** — form a guess at the main point in the first few sentences and revise it against incoming information. "Your goal is not to get the hypothesis right your first shot. Your goal is to just force yourself to make a hypothesis, [which] by definition is going to naturally force you to really understand what's going on."
- **The three orders of purpose** — "the sentences are serving the paragraphs. The paragraphs are serving the passage." Any purpose-of-a-sentence question is answered top-down: passage move, then paragraph move, then sentence.
- **Author always takes a side** — Adriana's own carry-over from an earlier session, which Joshua endorsed: "Don't think the author is ever going to play the neutral game." Any answer choice that refuses to take a side is therefore wrong on primary purpose.
- **Opposing viewpoints are instrumental** — "the only reason why the opposing side is there is to show you the reasons why that side is inferior to what I believe. It's never there to just genuinely explore it." He explicitly links this to LR main conclusion.
- **What vs. why (correlation/causation in RC)** — the trap that cost her Q5. "Stating something is successful is entirely different from telling you the causal effect, the causal reason why it's successful." Adriana's reaction: "No way they snuck a causal correlation flaw in there."
- **Rhetorical tool** — content introduced to serve a contrast rather than as subject matter. "The scientists are not brought up to teach us about science. They're just a rhetorical tool to contrast the old method to the new approach."
- **Callbacks in the final paragraph** — "whenever they especially make a call back to earlier in the passage in the final paragraph, you should really pay attention to that, because it's usually addressing something they've said earlier on."
- **Absolute language as a wrongness signal** — "anything that says 'all' in it, you should always avoid. Safely worded, defensible answers are always the way to go... the stronger they go, the more dangerous it is." His RC answer profile: "stay broad and fluffy and hard to disprove because it's too open-ended."
- **Educated-reasonable-person standard** — "the answers should be coming from a very educated, well-reasoned person. If the answers don't sound like they're coming from that perspective," discard.
- **LSAC sensitivity heuristic** — the test writers will never rank a European practice above an indigenous or minority one. See Teaching moves.

## Teaching moves
- **Dismisses a competing method by name and gives the reason.** Adriana had been using ViewSTAMP from the PowerScore RC Bible. Joshua: "the ViewSTAMP method kind of is just... it's just a lot. I'm not going to actually sit down and think of [it]" mid-passage. He credits it as orientation ("good to get you to have a familiar idea of what RC is") but rejects it as an in-the-moment procedure. He does not dismiss the parts she reports working — she had already told him argument and main point collapse into each other and structure rarely earns its keep.
- **Held the method back deliberately until now.** "That's the part where I've never gone into this with you because I didn't want to throw too many curve balls. I threw in a little bit the last time." He staged the most powerful RC framework for the last session before the test rather than front-loading it.
- **Makes her guess the distinction before defining it.** "I'll let you guess the nuance. What's the nuance difference between a recommendation and evaluation?" She produced prescriptive/descriptive; he then adopted her wording for the rest of the session ("prescriptive, [you] said it before very well").
- **Names her engagement in real time and uses it as proof of the method.** She anticipated the Evaluation move before he introduced it. He stopped to make it the lesson: "that's how I know you're engaging with what I'm saying, because you're forward thinking... What you just did there is exactly what you want to do. You're not just listening to the information passively. You're asserting yourself."
- **The hypothesis-as-science-experiment analogy.** "Like a science experiment, right? I hypothesize that when I combine these two chemicals this outcome will happen. And then with the new information, you adjust your hypothesis." Also frames the corrective moment as the payoff: "'oh, correction, actually it's not the direction I thought.' That's a powerful thing, because to know that means that you know by definition you're engaged."
- **Tells her not to reread.** "When you read a sentence over and over, it often does not come to be more. You might think and convince yourself that oh, now I get it more. You probably don't." The replacement move is build-or-pivot, then keep going. This directly targets her stated habit of not advancing until she fully understands a sentence.
- **Buckets, not details** — the memory argument for CEER. "That's the thing that makes your life a lot easier, because you don't have to remember the details. You can remember the buckets of ideas. This paragraph is just comparing side A and side B. Then you could fill in the details of what you remember, but you're not forcing yourself to remember all the details."
- **Answer-choice pre-screening by move.** Once the passage is tagged Recommendation, "any answer that does not talk about recommendation in some synonymous term, you should not even engage with it." He drilled it by asking her to read only the first word of each choice: criticize, defend — both Evaluation, both dead. "This is why you should get really efficient at this."
- **Questions as a snowball.** "You get a snowball effect... when you know what the answer to the first question is, you get more information to work with. Your database gets larger. Use the questions as almost like a weapon to get better at the later questions." She had solved Q3 without recalling that Q1's credited answer already told her the passage was a recommendation.
- **The contract-lawyer frame for support.** "Remember, the passage is a contract. You're a lawyer, and you're not reading to just read freestyle... All of the answers for a contract should be directly stated in the actual contract." Practically: underline the explicit support for every choice.
- **The guinea-pig image for two-sided passages.** "He wrote this passage in mind knowing his final solution is to do this outcome, and he uses two sides as almost his guinea pigs to say, okay, I'm using this to demonstrate why my side's correct."
- **Lets her argue toward the wrong answer and then closes it.** She talked herself further into E on Q5 ("the more I'm arguing for E, the more it makes sense to me"), and he did not interrupt — he responded "No, it's fair" and then separated what from why in the text itself.
- **Tolerance for tagging error.** "You don't want to get too lost into the tag of how you tag the paragraph, as long as you get the idea. If you get a mistake between evaluation [and] recommendation, it's not the end of the world." The framework is a thinking aid, not a scoring rubric.
- **Teaches the test-maker's politics as an answer-elimination tool.** On the Hopi passage: "They will never ever neglect a minority group... They'll never look down upon the native group or the indigenous group and then say the European names are the great name. They'll never do that... If they ever talk about Europeans and any other group, the Europeans are either going to be neutral or the negative side. Always." His stated reason is institutional risk: "They would be a lawsuit the next day." He extends it to "all" claims about a group: "they would never say all of the minority group does this thing... you're putting us in a monolith." He personalizes it to her ("you said you're Mexican background... if you saw some writing about Mexican heritage, they're never going to dunk on them"), and hedges the framing as an observation about the writers rather than a political claim of his own ("without making this political here"). He also insists this is not the primary reason B is wrong — paragraph 4 contradicts B on the text — the heuristic is a pre-read filter.
- **Test-week taper, argued not asserted.** He replaced her planned full sections with isolated questions and closed Friday down almost entirely. Reasoning given in full below.
- **Confirms difficulty to protect her confidence.** "This is a really challenging passage, by the way... As I read it I'm like, whoa, this is not [an easy] one. You definitely want to slow down." Same move as the prior session with the 7Sage rating.

## Worked examples
- **Subjectivist vs. objectivist philosophy of mind passage (level 5, one she failed twice including on blind review).** CEER tagging worked out live: P1 Explanation (side A, the objectivists), P2 Comparison (side B, the subjectivists), P3 Evaluation (the author declares the debate deadlocked because "the two sides lack a common context and don't share any foundational beliefs"), P4 Recommendation (stop fighting over the data, investigate the authority of the differing perspectives).
  - *Q on primary purpose.* She picked A, then E; credited answer B, "advocate a possible solution." Her block was tonal — she hunts for adverbs and emotional language as opinion markers ("one of the brain hacks that I do is look for stuff that ends in -ly") and found the passage neutral, so "advocate" read as too strong. Joshua's fix: purpose is structural, not tonal. The passage ends in a Recommendation, so only a recommendation-shaped answer survives; A is neutral and takes no side, C ("criticize") and D ("defend") are Evaluation, E ("evaluate the legitimacy") is Evaluation. She had also read "what would be required is an investigation" as a statement of fact rather than a prescription; Joshua: proposing a resolution to a debate *is* proposing a solution. She restated the rule back to check it: "a recommendation is proposing any solution... anytime I see a solution, resolution, resolve the debate is a solution."
  - *Q5, why the author discusses the work of scientists.* She chose E, "clarify why the objectivist approach has been successful," twice. The text says the objective approach "has been remarkably successful in yielding knowledge" and that scientists pursue hard data. Her inference: the hard data is obviously the reason. Joshua's separation: "The objectivist pursuing hard data is just a definition of what the objectivist approach is. We don't know if that's the reason why." The passage states *that* it is successful, never *why*. The sentence's actual job, read top-down: P1 explains side A so P2 can compare side B, all in service of the final recommendation, so the scientists are there to show what the new approach looks like against the outdated traditional one.
- **Hopi names passage (previously missed, corrected on blind review; re-run for CEER practice).** Tagging: P1 Comparison (European naming assumptions about identification and social classification, immediately contrasted with Hopi reality), P2 and P3 Explanation (how Hopi names work; example "beautifully ascended"), P4 Evaluation (the author steps back, names Mill and Levi, and reconciles the two — Hopi names identify persons, mark social relationships, *and* carry poetic beauty simultaneously). Adriana first proposed Recommendation for P4 and self-corrected toward Comparison; Joshua landed it on Evaluation and told her the near-miss was acceptable.
  - *Q, why B is wrong.* B claimed Hopi names neglect the functions of identification and social classification. Textually dead: paragraph 4 states explicitly that they do perform those functions. Structurally dead before reading: it ranks the European convention above the Hopi one, which LSAC will not print. Credited answer E ("Hopi names also possess a significant aesthetic quality") shows the group in "the strong seat." She also asked about D ("all Hopi names conform to a formula") — Joshua called it neutral in tone but dead on "all," on both the monolith objection and the general absolutes rule.
