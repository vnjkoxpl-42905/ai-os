# Adriana Varea, 2026-05-25

Blind-review session on a practice test Adriana scored 173 on, twelve days out from her June 6 LSAT. Three LR questions worked in depth, converging on one diagnosis: her only recurring weakness is comparative/net-effect arguments, where she does not look for a symmetry breaker.

## LSAT concepts
- **Necessary assumption** — the assumption the argument depends on. Joshua's structural rule: when the stimulus contains a conditional, necessary assumption almost always wants a **link**, not a negation. "We see a conditional, we see necessary assumption, automatically... we're looking to do a linking. The job is to be linking something that's either amongst the premises or between the conclusion to where the premises left off at."
- **New concept in the conclusion** — the primary tell on necessary assumption with a conditional. "There's going to be almost with like the highest degree of certainty a new concept in a conclusion." Here it was "inevitably distorted," which appears nowhere in the evidence. "So by definition that has to be the answer... there's just no answer that can't have that in it." He immediately calibrates the certainty: 100% true for sufficient assumption, "95% chance" for necessary assumption.
- **Negation test** — negating an answer to see if the argument collapses. Joshua treats it as valid but expensive, and here actively discourages it: "it's really brutal to negate this statement. Even even as I did it, it was tricky. So I wouldn't I wouldn't ever recommend you to negate it."
- **Negating a conditional** — "when we're negating a conditional, how do we do that?" Adriana: "you negate the necessary." Joshua adds the missing half: "And while the sufficient still happening."
- **"Only" as a conditional indicator** — "when you see that the answer choice is saying that there's the 'only,' you can remember that just means if-then; you can take it and turn it into an if-then."
- **Negation must hold across all readings** — negating "only a few" opens both "none" and "a million"; the answer is only correct if the argument collapses in *every* one of those worlds. "If it's zero you could argue that answer choice would be correct. But if it's a million then... the argument could stand without that answer choice, and that means that by itself that's already enough to say that's not the right answer."
- **Correlation vs. causation** — the stimulus gave a correlation between adrenaline and memory; the conclusion asserted a comparative causal claim. "They're assuming a causal claim, making a causal claim; the conclusion evidence is just showing us a correlation... That doesn't mean it's because of that thing. We have no idea why it's the case."
- **Symmetry breaker** — the session's central concept and the assigned homework skill. In any argument comparing two things, a strengthener or weakener must load one side of the scale only. "Whenever there's an answer choice that does not make a symmetry breaker between the two... the answer choice can't apply equally to both or we haven't moved the scale, because we already have one ahead of the other." Stated as an absolute: "You can never have an answer that equally applies to both."
- **Net effect** — named as the same idea wearing a different costume: "whether it's net effect is another way that they go about that same kind of like which one's net more, which one's net less. These are the same ideas overlapping."
- **The tie game** — weakening a comparative conclusion does not require proving the reverse. "We just need to prove it's a tie game... If it's saying A is more than B, I don't have to prove that B is more than A. I just have to prove that A and B are at least equal."
- **The special ingredient** — the general form of the above. "When an author concludes that A is better than B because A has a special ingredient, the most devastating way to weaken the argument is to prove that B has that exact same" ingredient, which "neutralizes it."
- **Principle / conform questions** — matching the abstract dynamic, not the surface domain. The dynamic here: "a competitive incentive that drives participants to take more of an action than it is collectively optimal."

## Teaching moves
- **Uses time-spent as the diagnostic, not accuracy.** He noticed from the platform breakdown that her wrong answers were exactly her slowest ones. "The questions that you got wrong, ironically, were the ones you spent like an excessive amount of time on them, which you think would be the inverse."
- **Two-mode framework: flow vs. friction.** He explicitly tells her not to run the checklist when the question is working. "If it's coming to you naturally, just do it because you'll get the question right. Whenever you're getting pushed, you're like, 'uh, this one feels a bit awkward'" — that is the cue to switch into the conscious trait-hunting mode: "which traits should I be looking for for a right answer?"
- **Question-type + stimulus-shape lookup table.** He teaches the pairing rather than the type alone: conditional + necessary assumption means link and affirm; causation + necessary assumption means negation-flavored answers with "not" in them. "This is going to be likely to say I need to affirm something and they need to explicitly spit out what the author didn't think of that we have to link. Your job is to add now, not to see what would fall."
- **Makes her locate the new concept by Socratic narrowing.** She could not find it; rather than telling her, he asked one closing question: "Do we see 'inevitably distorted' in evidence?" She got it immediately.
- **Validates before correcting.** With a strong student he repeatedly confirms the parts she got right before touching the error: "I don't think you missed anything that I just said up to this point. But the problem is..." and "so far we're good. No issue with that."
- **Lets her argue.** Adriana said she still disagrees with the credited answer and would pick A again; he did not shut it down. "Fair. Fair. The thing is like we can just test it, right?" and then rebuilt the argument as an A→B bridge to test it live. When she said she was "quadrupling down," he engaged the substance rather than the authority.
- **Names bad platform data.** She was demoralized that a question this hard was rated low difficulty. He corrected the data itself: "This is a level five question on every other platform because I have this question. I've found this question several times... LSAT Lab and Demon, it's both a level five. I don't know why the LSAT [7Sage] is the worst I think of all platforms of their answer choice rating difficulty."
- **Permission to lose a point.** The session's locked decision. "This is one of those questions that we're doing it for because we should, but you can afford to forgive... You're like, I'm okay with that. I'm okay with that to be a lost point, especially because it's the only one." Justified probabilistically: coin-flip questions split roughly evenly over a test, so protecting pace matters more than winning any one of them. "If you can let those questions go... you should be a happy camper."
- **Distinguishes forgivable from unforgivable misses.** "The ones you can't forgive yourself for are the ones... you know that you know that answer, you shouldn't have got that one wrong. But you did everything you're supposed to do" on this one.
- **Reframes a stimulus as a competition to unlock the answer.** Adriana had read Q20 as "oversight with unintended consequences." He renamed the dynamic as gaming a relative-performance reward, and she saw C instantly: "I didn't think of it like as a competition. And I think when you phrase it in that way, like it becomes a lot more obvious that C is the right answer."
- **Teaches the weakener by pre-generating candidate answers before reading any.** He asked "how could we argue that non-frightening experiences are at least as memorable?" and produced two families: another source of the same adrenaline, or a counteracting force unique to fear ("trauma or repression... which will counteract the memory boost afforded by adrenaline").
- **Scale imagery, sustained.** "Side A has frightening so it gets the adrenaline boost... Side B is non-frightening. They're assuming to have no adrenaline boost." Then the crux: "The researchers' conclusion only survives if fear has a monopoly on adrenaline. If fear is the only thing putting weight on the memory scale, then fear wins. But if it doesn't, we have a problem."
- **Names the trap as a student behavior, not just a logic error.** "This is the reoccurring flawed weakening answer choice that students often fall into. They will choose an answer that would benefit one side that they're trying to help out, but it doesn't cancel out the other side... it's not enough to stop there."
- **Frames ceiling-level coaching honestly.** "When you get into the mid-170s you're not going to have... 'you're just terrible at everything.' That's not going to be what anybody can say, because you're really at a score where it's really difficult to get much better." The work is anomaly-hunting, not remediation.
- **Takes live notes to build the homework.** "There will be a lot that I'm going to take today just as notes on my side to give you like an aggressive week for this week."

## Worked examples
- **Q8, necessary assumption (historian / popular historical awareness).** Stimulus: democracy requires citizens know some history; however popular historical awareness is inevitably distorted; most people learn history through popular narratives that sustain interest by implying a few famous heroes and notorious villains shaped all of history. Adriana narrowed to A and E, chose A, credited answer E. Her reasoning for A: negating it appears to contradict "inevitably distorted." Her objection to E: it says "distorts history," but the conclusion is about *historical awareness*, and conflating the two is the LSAT's own standard trap. Joshua's path: (1) conditional + necessary assumption means find the new concept in the conclusion, which is "inevitably distorted"; (2) locate what the author is silently swapping — the assumption that the heroes/villains implication is itself false, which Adriana supplied ("it's distorted because that's not true"); (3) A fails on precision, since it says awareness is distorted by the view that only a few heroes existed, when the claim is about their having *shaped all of history* — "the claim is way more specific than" A. Adriana: "I understand why it's wrong. I just... I don't agree." Joshua conceded the question is "devious" and filed it as an acceptable loss.
- **Q20, principle/conform (national funding of local projects).** Local governments get a larger share of national funds by creating more projects than the average locality, so everyone overbuilds and total spending and taxation rise everywhere. Adriana's abstraction: "centralized organization giving stuff to other organizations... and it has an unexpectedly negative effect" — which led her to D (bulk food buying, unintended consequences). Joshua's abstraction: relative-performance incentive → each participant ramps up → collectively worse outcome. Under that reading C (sales manager offering a prize to the top seller, salespeople undercutting each other, profits fall) is the only match. D's defect named precisely: "the competitive aspect is what's missing out of D... it just seems like a bad outcome because of people essentially gamifying." Her stated objection to C was direction (stimulus compounds upward, C drives prices down), which the competition frame dissolves. She also flagged fatigue: "putting this long of a question at 20 is just disrespectful."
- **Q22, weaken (adrenaline and memory).** Stimulus: experiences with increased adrenaline secretion, typically fear situations, are remembered more clearly; thus details of frightening experiences are remembered more clearly than details of non-frightening ones. Adriana chose B (people best remember details relevant to the satisfaction of desires), credited answer C (highly pleasurable experiences are accompanied by increased adrenaline). B's fatal flaw, per Joshua: satisfaction of desires applies to *both* sides — even a frightening experience has a desire, "your desire to get the heck out of the way from like the spooky sound" — so it never tips the scale; and it addresses which details within an event are remembered, not which type of event. Adriana's live objection to C was strong and unresolved-adjacent: C says pleasurable experiences have increased adrenaline but not *how much*, so maybe fear still has quadruple the dose. Joshua's answer: the argument was built on fear having a monopoly on adrenaline, so any adrenaline dropped on the non-frightening side breaks the symmetry, and a weakener only needs the tie. She landed on the takeaway herself: "He also has the special ingredient... that's a good generic thing to remember."
- **Section 4, Q18** — not worked (time), but Joshua identified it as "the exact same issue actually with the idea of the net effect," making the comparative/symmetry pattern a confirmed repeat rather than a one-off.
