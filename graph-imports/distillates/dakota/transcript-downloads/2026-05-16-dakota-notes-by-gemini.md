# Dakota Brown, 2026-05-16

## LSAT concepts

- **Inside-the-box vs outside-the-box question families.** Joshua splits LR by whether new information is welcome. Sufficient assumption, strengthen, weaken, and paradox invite you to add a premise. Necessary assumption, parallel reasoning, and method of reasoning "will punish you for doing that exact thing." His line: "in one hand you'll get a slap on the wrist for doing it, and the other hand you'll get extra points for it."
- **Answer-choice language intensity.** Default to weaker, safer language for must be true, most strongly supported, necessary assumption, main conclusion, role, and reading comprehension, because "the stronger the claim, the more the burden of evidence will be stronger." Invert it for paradox, strengthen, weaken, and sufficient assumption, where stronger is better. He stresses it is conceptual strength, not just the words.
- **Conditionals in answer choices.** "Conditionals are very strong in their nature, but they're only strong if the condition applies to what's being spoken of. If the if is not relevant, then this whole conditional completely means nothing, has zero strength." Rarely the credited answer because both the trigger and the outcome have to land.
- **Paradox.** RE has "the word explain in it." You want the choice with the most explanatory, causal power to reconcile the two facts. Pivot words (but, however, nevertheless, yet) mark the point of tension.
- **Correlation vs causation** carried over from the prior session; Dakota used "corresponding" as a correlation flag to eliminate a choice.
- **Reversal trap / illicit conditional conversion.** Answer E on the anchovy question asserts plankton cannot survive without the acid; concluding from "the plankton swarms went away" that "there is no acid" is the wrong direction.
- **Relative vs absolute claims.** "Below the country's average" is relative and tells you nothing about whether conditions are good or bad. "General dissatisfaction" is absolute. His analogy: "if I say that you're taller than the guy beside you, that doesn't mean you're tall."

## Teaching moves

- **Format pivot announced up front.** He names the shift away from lecture: "we went through a decent amount of me ranting and talking about all the causation stuff... the session will be largely just more questions and you're just doing them and we'll fix and iterate as you go."
- **Olympic-lifter analogy for stimulus-versus-answer burden.** "One type of question will make it so the answer choices are doing the heavy lifting. They're the Olympian lifter. And then some other questions will have the stimulus be the heavy lifter, and it's lifting up the answer choices." For must be true, "which one of these answer choices can be lifted above the head of the stimulus... Can you lift a hundred pounds? Maybe. Can you lift a thousand pounds? Probably not." Dakota had brought "heavy lifting" in from Seven Sage and Joshua adopted and extended it.
- **Burden-of-proof analogy for weak language.** "If I want to prove to you that I'm a human being, that's not as difficult as to prove to you that I'm the son of God, or the true prince of Africa."
- **Told him not to memorize question stems.** When Dakota asked how to memorize the stem variations, Joshua redirected to process: "You want to have a mission statement... Indirectly you'll be able to learn the question type based off the question stem. Don't focus too much right now memorizing the question stems."
- **Mission before answers.** Restated at the close as the core takeaway: "what is my mission going into the answer choices... you want to have a question you're looking to answer, and then one of the answer choices will give you the answer to that," versus "trying to just freestyle it."
- **Line-by-line paraphrase drill.** Forces Dakota to restate each sentence in plain English and refuses vague restatements. When Dakota said "existing transportation infrastructure," Joshua pushed: "What does that mean?" until Dakota landed on roads and potholes. He named why he does it: "whenever you are able to succinctly tell me what the stimulus is getting at, you're almost always able to get the answer."
- **Explicit skeptic exercise.** "Tell me all the potential downsides for each answer choice... Not why it's right, not all the good things, all the negative things. I want you to be as skeptical as possible."
- **Dentist analogy for the infrastructure paradox**, offered before the answer choices: why do people skip cheap cleanings and only show up for an expensive root canal? "Human beings are just terrible at prioritizing things that don't feel like immediate problems." Later extended to smoking: people know it is bad but "in the current moment they look at it and it's not a big deal because they don't see any current issues."
- **Naming the money trap.** Dakota objected that E never mentions cost. Joshua validated the instinct then flipped it: "the LSAT writers actually count on students getting obsessed with the word money. They know test takers will actively hunt for answer choices that mention dollars, cost, or revenue." Because the stimulus already stipulates continuous maintenance is cheaper, "the math is already settled. We don't negotiate with that. The mystery is about the human psychology component."
- **Rejecting a wrong answer for the right reason.** He would not let Dakota discard C as "contradicting the premises," correcting him: C is wrong because it "enhances the paradox," not because it conflicts. The test is impact on the mystery, not consistency.
- **Explicit sequencing on later questions.** "I want you to first explain to me the conundrum, but I want you to do the stimulus part by yourself," with a timed minute or two before reconvening, and answers held back until the stimulus is nailed.
- **Plate-not-ocean framing** for the irrelevant-population answer: "we don't care about how many fish there are. We only care about the ones on our plate that will kill us."
- **Salary numbers to make the relative/absolute paradox concrete.** National average $1000, area L at $800, now at $1000. "Why are we assuming we went up to a thousand? More likely the national average went down instead. Maybe now it's 800, maybe 600. The whole country got worse."
- **Diagnosing a wrong answer as a reading error, not a reasoning error.** Once Dakota revealed he had read "county" for "country," Joshua reframed the miss as reassuring: "it wasn't an issue of misunderstanding... I was surprised by how specifically you got the answer right with your explanation of your hypothesis. It couldn't have been more accurate. That kind of solves it."
- **Naming a distractor's design.** On the tempting answer A: "that's a very sneaky one... this is why it's so devious, because it kind of flirts with a perfect idea that could have fixed the bomb."

## Worked examples

- **Transportation infrastructure (paradox).** Two options: continuous maintenance at adequate levels, or periodic radical reconstruction. Continuous maintenance dispenses with the need for reconstruction and is far less expensive over the long run, nevertheless it almost never happens. Dakota was stuck between C and E. Correct answer E: for long periods regular maintenance lacks urgency since the consequences of neglect are very slow to manifest. A rejected as backwards (says reconstruction is hard to coordinate, but it happens constantly). B rejected because it only says which roads get money, not how the money is spent. C rejected because sub-adequate maintenance still buying time does not explain why maintenance is skipped entirely; if anything it deepens the puzzle. D rejected as the opposite of the stimulus.
- **Indonesian coral reef shrimp (paradox).** Eleven reefs, substantial genetic differences between shrimp populations, surprising because strong currents should carry larvae between reefs and homogenize them. Dakota had seen it; run as a quick stimulus-paraphrase drill only. He flagged on his own that "indistinguishable" does not mean "identical," which Joshua endorsed.
- **Anchovies and domoic acid (paradox).** People got sick from locally caught anchovies contaminated with domoic acid, the city advised against eating them; however the population of a specific plankton dropped back to normal levels, therefore the city declared the anchovies safe. The unstated gap is the link between plankton and acid. Correct answer B: the plankton produce the acid, and anchovies only become toxic when they consume very large quantities, which happens only when plankton numbers are extraordinary. A rejected because it implies any ingestion produces acid. C rejected as lab conditions saying nothing about wild toxicity thresholds. D rejected because a drop in the anchovy population says nothing about whether the remaining fish are poisonous. E rejected as a reversal trap.
- **Area L living conditions (paradox).** Ten years ago conditions were slightly below the country's average and residents reported satisfaction; now conditions are about the same as the national average yet residents report general dissatisfaction. Dakota produced the correct hypothesis immediately (the national average could have fallen) but answered A because he had misread "country" as "county" in the stimulus. Correct answer B: the country's average worsened. A rejected because nothing is known about adjacent areas. C rejected as three continents and irrelevant policy inputs. D rejected as a psychological rule about when conditions improve, not an explanation of the mystery. E rejected because unawareness ten years ago deepens the paradox; learning you are better off should raise satisfaction, not lower it.
