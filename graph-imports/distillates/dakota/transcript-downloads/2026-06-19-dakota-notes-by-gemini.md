# Dakota Brown, 2026-06-19

## LSAT concepts

- **Causation as explanation.** The recurring structure: the evidence reports a phenomenon or study result, the conclusion offers an interpretation of how it came to be. "The conclusion is just the explanation for the situation." Joshua calls the author's explanation his alibi.
- **Two ways to attack any causal explanation**: offer an alternative explanation, or directly undermine the plausibility of the author's. Strengthen is the exact inverse: rule out an alternative, or boost the author's explanation.
- **No cause, no effect / cause, no effect.** Strengthen by showing that when the purported cause was absent, the effect did not occur. Weaken by showing the cause present without the effect, or the effect present without the cause.
- **Correlation vs causation** revisited: "Patterns don't tell us what the cause is. It just tells us there's a pattern. If we want to identify the cause, we take away the cause and see if the thing still happens."
- **Unfair comparison / uncontrolled variable.** For any two-group study, ask whether the groups were fair to compare. "If they don't establish that the only difference between the two groups was the thing that was presented to them, then something else could have been the reason." Joshua names this as one of the most common underlying structures on the test.
- **Precision about what the conclusion actually claims.** In the GMO potato question the claim is specifically about genetic modification, not potatoes generally, so an answer showing the control diet contained no potatoes at all breaks the comparison.
- **Proportion vs raw number.** An answer about a declining number of trees does not touch a conclusion resting on the proportion of pears damaged.
- **EXCEPT questions.** Substitute "the least": four choices weaken, one does not. The credited answer usually has no impact at all rather than strengthening. "People often go to the opposite side and say, okay, because it's not weakening, it should strengthen. No, there's obviously an in between, and it'll very rarely be a strengthener."
- **Strong language is preferred for strengthen and weaken.** "We want the strongest strengthener possible. If we want a weakener, we want the most damaging answer choice." A choice saying "no" or "none" outperforms one saying "some" or "many," though conceptual impact still beats magic words.
- **Negation as a tiebreaker on strengthen/weaken**, not just necessary assumption. If negating a choice moves the conclusion's likelihood, it has impact; if nothing moves, it does nothing. Useful when deciding between two finalists or verifying an EXCEPT answer.
- **Theory-laden observation.** "The data doesn't in itself... we read things into what we see. If we see a certain event, we are very biased to seeing it through a certain lens of how that came to be. It's almost instinctual."

## Teaching moves

- **Teach-me-the-stimulus as the opening move**, every question. "Walk me through what the stimulus was saying. Teach me the stimulus, and then tell me how you approached it after." Answer choice discussion is deliberately deferred.
- **Pushing past a vague diagnosis.** Dakota said the gap was "they don't provide enough details about the study." Joshua accepted it, then demanded specificity: "if you were to give me more specific, what's the conclusion saying if you were to boil it down into a simple sentiment?"
- **Laddering the student to the category name.** On the guppy question he asked what the author was appealing to; Dakota said "a hypothesis"; Joshua pressed for "the big overlying family," and Dakota produced "causation" himself rather than being told.
- **Making Dakota restate the framework in his own words and write it down.** Dakota asked for the two attack routes again explicitly to record them: "alternate hypothesis, or critiquing the argument in the sense of cause with no effect, or no cause with the same effect." Joshua confirmed and corrected the phrasing.
- **Correcting a subtle misstatement.** When Dakota said the second route is "critiquing the evidence," Joshua drew the line: "we're not critiquing the evidence, we're critiquing their explanation. The explanation is the alibi to the story."
- **Prephrasing an alternative before reading answers**, taught as a speed and accuracy device: "it's very good practice to design one for each scenario, or at least foreshadow what it could look like prior to the answers. Once you do it, you get very fast at it. So accuracy goes up and speed."
- **Sustained patience through a stalled prephrase.** On the GMO question Dakota circled twice around nutritional value. Joshua declined to supply the answer, restated the constraint ("there's two groups, one exposed and one not, what led to the different outcomes?"), and only after the second miss introduced the fair-comparison frame.
- **Generalizing a question's structure to a family.** "They'll replace rats with kids who read, or people who are exposed to this certain thing. They'll always do this. You always want to think about how can I look for a factor they didn't consider that could have made it unfair to compare."
- **Flagging the sneakiness of the distractor design.** On the GMO question: "This one is a very sneaky subtle nuance... usually it'll just introduce something completely random, like the rats were from a different collection from South America, which is a more glaring issue. This is a very good example of how sly the LSAT can be."
- **Letting the student run the elimination.** On several questions Joshua handed over the remaining choices entirely: "I'll let you walk through the other answers before I give my input." Dakota then named alternate explanation, critique of explanation, and no-impact for C, D, and E without help.
- **Metaphor for the shielding answer.** On the fifth force question: "this is like a massive shield. If you're going to invent a brand new universe-altering force, the biggest threat to your theory is that it breaks all existing rules of physics. By guaranteeing this new theory doesn't clash with established science, it removes a huge potential weakener." He was careful to add that it does not prove the force is real, only defends its plausibility.
- **Grounding praise in difficulty.** "These are all level four questions, which is really hard. Strengthening and weakening at the level four range are actually some of the hardest questions, which is promising."

## Worked examples

- **Guppy courtship (strengthen).** A study concluded that male guppies alter their courtship patterns in response to female feedback: females are drawn to more orange showing, and males with more orange on one side were free to vary which side they displayed. Dakota answered A correctly, a control condition using a model rather than a live female where the orange-display behavior did not occur. Joshua framed it as the no-cause-no-effect strengthener.
- **Arctic dinosaur fossils (weaken).** Scientists once assumed all dinosaurs were cold-blooded, but dinosaur fossils found in the Arctic suggest at least some were warm-blooded, since only warm-blooded animals could have survived those temperatures. Dakota answered D correctly: the fossils indicate the dinosaurs migrated for food, so cold-blooded dinosaurs may have passed through the Arctic during warm months and died there rather than living through winters. Joshua had Dakota classify it as an alternate explanation rather than a direct attack. A rejected (reptiles today live in warm climates) as explaining nothing about the Arctic fossils. B rejected (small size) as not explaining winter survival. C and E rejected as running the wrong direction, confirming the Arctic was genuinely freezing then, which helps the author.
- **GMO potatoes and rats (weaken).** Rats fed genetically modified potatoes developed intestinal deformities and weakened immune systems; rats on normal diets did not; the conclusion is that GM potatoes caused the harm. Dakota initially chose E, that the researchers could not explain how the potatoes would cause the effects. Joshua rejected it: the fact that these particular researchers lacked a mechanism moves little, whereas "the scientific community at large has no theory for how GM would impact gut immunity" would at least register. Correct answer A: the normal diets contained no potatoes at all, so the comparison isolates potatoes rather than genetic modification. Dakota drew the takeaway himself, that a weakener must still track the conclusion's exact claim.
- **Pear pesticide (weaken, EXCEPT).** Over the last three years the proportion of pears damaged by insects fell compared with the previous three years, concluded to show the new pesticide is more effective than the old. Dakota answered A immediately and confidently: a steady decline in the number of mature pear trees. Correct, because the evidence rests on proportion, not raw count, so fewer trees does not explain a lower percentage damaged. He confirmed by checking that each other choice did supply an alternate explanation.
- **Fifth universal force (strengthen).** Before 1986 physicists described the universe with four universal forces; experiments then suggested a fifth force of mutual repulsion between particles of matter, which would explain the observed weaker-than-predicted gravitational attraction between bodies. Dakota chose B and was correct: no previously obtained results are incompatible with the notion of a fifth force. Joshua highlighted both the double negative Dakota had to read through and the strength of "no." A rejected because equipment age says nothing about measurement accuracy. C rejected as an alternate explanation and therefore a weakener. D rejected as attacking the measurements. E rejected because an exciting era in physics does not bear on truth.
