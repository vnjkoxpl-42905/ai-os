## LSAT concepts
- **Conditional reasoning** — "all a conditional is, ultimately, what it comes down to, is just the art of the if-then... if I know this to be true, what else must be true?" Absolute, not probabilistic: "this is not saying it can't happen. It must happen. There's no way around this relationship."
- **Must be true, not could be true** — the governing question. "With conditionals, you want to know what must be true. We're not looking to see what could be true. They'll bait you into what something could be true, but they're always going to test you on what has to be true."
- **Sufficient condition** — the "if" part. "Think of it as the door opener." Working gloss from Dakota's own vocabulary: sufficient means "enough." "It's the condition that is enough to know another state of affairs."
- **Activation** — a conditional says nothing until its sufficient condition is known true. "For this statement to be true, it has to be activated... I can't assume I am breathing or I am in Ohio. I'm just saying if I'm in that scenario." Also framed as a hypothetical: "think of 'if' as a hypothetical."
- **Sufficient absent = you know nothing.** "If you're not in Ohio, I just don't know where you could be. You could be in Ethiopia, you could be in Afghanistan. You could also be in America — I'm not denying that fact. I just don't know one or the other."
- **A guaranteed way, not the only way** — "the sufficient condition being present is a guaranteed way, but it's not the only way. Not necessarily. We just don't know. It could be the only way as well. We just don't know. We don't go one way or the other."
- **Necessary condition** — the "then" part. "Must is the best answer. It's literally what it means by definition." Also the required condition. "Meeting the requirement doesn't mean you'll get to the outcome... there can be multiple requirements." Image: "the sufficient condition opens the door, the necessary condition slams the door."
- **Sufficient indicator words (group 1)** — if, when, whenever, every, all, any. "Whatever idea follows it is going to be the sufficient condition." Characterized by feel: "they have a lighter feel to them... they're all kind of inclusive, they're open-sounding words. They're more lighthearted. It's not as restrictive."
- **Necessary indicator words (group 2)** — must, require, only, only if, only when, only where. "Whatever follows these words [is a] necessary condition."
- **Groups 3 and 4** — group 3 is the "un" words: unless, until, without. Group 4: never, not both, cannot. "Those are just modifications of the first two groups... the same principles though. Nothing changes."
- **Every indicator implies both halves** — "if you have a sufficient indicator word, that means you know that a necessary condition has to be attached to it. And if you have a necessary condition word, there has to be a sufficient condition attached to it... you can't have one without the other, it just grammatically won't make sense."
- **Order does not determine role** — "the order of the statements doesn't tell you which condition is sufficient or necessary... the requirement is always going to be the necessary condition. Doesn't matter which one comes first. For easier questions [the sufficient will come first]. But a lot of times the necessary condition might come first."
- **Diagramming and the must arrow** — "all you have to do is this arrow. You can call it the must arrow. You just follow the direction of the arrow." The purpose is explicit: "diagramming makes it so you don't have to think through the language. You can just see the logical relationships." English "can be a complicated way of expressing these relationships."
- **Read left to right only** — never reverse.
- **Inference** — "a statement that you know must be true based off what's presented to you."
- **Contrapositive** — the only inference a lone conditional yields. "Every conditional statement, there's only one thing which we call an inference... logically equivalent statements, interchangeable statements." Generated by **flip and negate**, two steps, then read it back in the order presented. "When you try to think about it conceptually is where you get trapped."
- **Negation** — "all you're looking to do is add a not to an idea." Not the extreme opposite: "not rich" does not mean poor. Minimal force: "what is the logical force required for me to just push me out of the category... in a minimal amount of force, not the most amount of force. It's binary. You want to get out of that circle."
- **The two-things rule** — "there's only two things you ever know": the original statement and its contrapositive. "Anything else I say but this thing, you can't know it. You just have to almost turn your brain off. You don't even engage, wrestle with it."
- **Mistaken reversal / affirming the necessary** — "the most common type of flaw on the LSAT." Concluding the sufficient from the necessary being met.
- **Rule + situation** — how conditional questions are built. "There'll be a conditional and a situational trigger, a trigger fact and a trigger outcome... all conditionals are rules. Our goal is to see how do the facts of the situation apply to the rule. Does this activate the rule at all?"
- **"Inferred" in an answer stem = must be true** — "it has to be literally impossible for that answer choice to be wrong."
- **Relative terms as disqualifiers** — "better is a relative claim. 1% better technically would be considered better. Doesn't mean they're in a healthy environment."
- **Meaning match, not word match** — "that doesn't have to be a verbatim match. We want as close as possible, but it's meaning match that we're ultimately looking for."
- **Every argument on the LSAT is flawed** — "there's never going to be really a question that's not... Every question type actually. So every argument." Consequence: an answer saying the argument is valid is never correct.

## Teaching moves
- **Frames conditionals by area of law, as a family of reasoning styles.** "Think of it from the perspective of statutes and torts... this is very much rule-based, like contracts, is how you want to think about with regards to conditionals." Causation gets mapped to criminal law, comparative reasoning to constitutional law and precedent. "These are just different ways of thinking."
- **Pre-warns the emotional dip and normalizes it before it happens.** "It's going to be somewhat dense — don't feel frustrated by the initial feeling, because it's kind of something that's very foreign." Later, more directly: "a lot of students crack at this point, funny enough. This is where they get like — I don't know what I'm doing — and they panic, and I usually have to be like, relax. It's almost like I can script it out. I know exactly when students always find a problem. It's always conditionals."
- **Asks for Dakota's definition before giving his own**, on every term: sufficient, necessary, if-then, negation. When Dakota guessed sufficient meant "a valid argument," Joshua redirected without dismissing — "it's not exactly that, but you're on the right path" — then asked what "sufficient" means in ordinary conversation, and built the technical meaning off "it's enough."
- **Baits the mistaken negation deliberately.** Asked what follows when the sufficient does not happen; Dakota said the then-statement also fails. Joshua: "That's the bait. That's a natural counterintuitive thing that we will naturally fall into. That exact thing I just tested you on is the most guaranteed thing you'll be tested on."
- **Uses the student's own facts.** He asked whether Dakota was from Iowa, was corrected to Ohio, and rebuilt the whole worked example around Ohio → USA.
- **The train metaphor for one-way reading.** Sets it up as a scenario rather than an assertion: you left your bag at station A and you are halfway to B — would you ask the conductor to reverse? "You'd look like a crazy person, right? So you can't do that... the conditional only goes left to right."
- **The coin metaphor for the contrapositive.** Quarter tails-up versus heads-up: still 25 cents. "It's the same thing, but it's just the other side of the coin. They can be used interchangeably." Also called it "its evil twin."
- **The Yale/LSAT example for necessary conditions**, chosen for a pre-law student and drilled through all four permutations. "If you don't take the LSAT, you're not getting into Yale. But just because you do take the LSAT does not mean you're going to get into Yale."
- **Successive landmines** as the image for triggered chains.
- **"Get dumber" as an instruction.** "It's almost like the dumber you can be, the better you'll be at this. When you try to be creative is when you get punished. When you follow the system in place, you just be machine-like."
- **Content is irrelevant, relationships are everything.** "The actual content of the if and the then doesn't really matter. The relationships stay the same... The LSAT is testing the ability to identify relationships."
- **Corrects a wrong answer by asking the prior question instead.** When Dakota diagrammed the mice conditional backwards, Joshua did not say "that's reversed" — he asked "what's the original if-then?", let Dakota restate it correctly, then asked for the contrapositive again. Dakota caught himself: "Oh yeah, I said it backwards, my bad." Joshua: "I was waiting for you."
- **Solve before reading the answers.** Stated as the whole technique for this question type: "first, the answers — we want to solve up front. You should know the answer verbatim before you go into it. That's how you get good at these."
- **Sells the question type on its determinacy.** "These are questions that you should never ever get wrong and you get good at them really fast, because you can solve for it like an equation. There's no arbitrariness to it. Very mathematical. It has a mathy kind of feel... people that really get good at this, they can almost gamify these questions."
- **Positions conditionals as the meta-skill, not one topic among sixty.** "Conditionals are at the heart of the LR. It's the backbone of the LR section... it can be in parallel questions, it can be sufficient assumption, and those are just downstream. But conditionals is the meta skill. So I like to get really good with students at that."

Skill drills get an explicit exception here: "this is the one type of part of the LSAT that I give a lot of skill drills, because it's so important."

## Worked examples
- **Diagramming drills (no answer choices).** "Stay informed → regularly check reliable news sources," with the contrapositive produced correctly. Then "you receive a discount only if you have a coupon," which required the full machine: identify the logical operator ("only if"), which introduces the necessary condition (having a coupon), so the other idea is sufficient; replace the operator with the arrow; convert to if-then — "if you receive a discount, then you have a coupon"; contrapositive — no coupon, no discount. Dakota flagged that mechanical negation reads badly in English and Joshua endorsed his sequence: "it's good to do that first though, what you did. Think about it as the not, then work backwards" into natural language.
- **Flaw question: art and the natural world.** Stimulus: all works of art that are beautiful have something to teach us; since the natural world as a whole is both beautiful and instructive, it is a work of art. Dakota diagrammed beautiful art → something to teach us, and the contrapositive: no teaching → not a beautiful work of art. He then read the flaw unaided — "it basically works backwards... instead of going from left to right, it goes from right to left." Joshua noted the argument is unusually messy: "they didn't really — usually it'd be a clean swap. In this case they just kind of took a piece of everything, just a mash at the end. They mix up the conditional entirely." Dakota chose C by predicting the flaw first and then discarding A (definitions, irrelevant), B (establishing value, does not describe a reversal), D (justification, not what is happening), and E (a possibility about things outside the natural world, irrelevant). This is where he asked whether an answer could ever say the argument is valid.
- **Must-be-true: neurons and the striatum.** Rule: newly formed neurons can help heal an injured brain, but only if they develop into the type most common in the injured area. Dakota flagged "but only if" as the indicator on sight and split the conditional correctly, then produced the contrapositive. Situational fact: in an injured striatum, newly formed neurons never become the most common type there. He connected it himself — "to me that indicates the contrapositive... the only thing I know is that means it won't heal the brain." Credited answer D, essentially verbatim what he had already derived. Joshua's lawyer framing: "I come to you, I say I have a problem with the law. You're going to ask me what I did, and you're going to see what the law tells you to do and apply that to give me advice."
- **Must-be-true: lab mice in small cages.** Facts: lab mice used in human health research are usually kept in small cages; such an environment is neither normal nor healthy for mice. Rule: the reliability of research using animals is diminished if those animals are not in an environment normal for them. Dakota's first pass mis-assigned the halves (calling the diminished reliability sufficient) and produced a scrambled contrapositive, tripped up by the "not" already in the rule — "I took it as like a double negative." Rebuilt correctly: not normal environment → reliability diminished; contrapositive, reliability not diminished → normal environment. Applying the rule to the facts activates the original, so reliability is diminished. He was torn between B and C and had stayed with C. B ("it would be appropriate to use them for research if they were kept under better conditions") dies twice: "better" is relative and does not establish a normal environment, and it speaks to a future situation not in evidence. Credited C: the usefulness of using mice for human health research is compromised by their conditions — "compromised" as a meaning-match for "diminished."
