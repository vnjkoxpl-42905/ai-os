# Dakota Brown, 2026-05-29

## LSAT concepts

- **Argument** = premise plus conclusion. Established from first principles at the top of the session.
- **Validity**, LSAT sense: not "more likely to be true" (Dakota's initial answer, corrected) but "100% impossible to falsify based off the evidence," with no appeal to outside evidence. Example: you are from Ohio, Ohio is in the US, therefore you are from the US.
- **Sufficient assumption**: you are handed an invalid argument and must supply the one premise that makes the conclusion guaranteed. "The answer choices given to you are extra premises that they want to supply to you to make the argument whole again."
- **Plug-back test**: after locating the gap, insert the choice as a new premise and ask whether the conclusion is now 100% guaranteed.
- **New concept in the conclusion.** If the conclusion contains any term absent from the evidence, the credited answer must address that term. "It's impossible for the answer to not address that new concept."
- **One-for-one restatement.** For an argument to be valid the conclusion must literally restate the evidence. "If the evidence says apples, the conclusion has to say apples. If they say fruit, we can't accept that." Any swap (electronics to technology, America to powerful empire) is the gap.
- **Fact vs rule.** Every sufficient assumption stimulus is missing either a situational fact or a governing rule. Joshua flags this as under-the-hood mechanics, useful to understand but not something to consciously sort during practice.
- **Universal default.** When the LSAT states no quantifier (no "some," "most," "a few"), read the claim as "all." Dakota asked whether this holds for every question type; Joshua said yes, every question type.
- **Sufficient vs necessary direction.** "Don't let the sufficient be the necessary and the necessary be sufficient." All genuine creative geniuses are dissatisfied with habitual assent does not license the reverse.
- **Strength preference for sufficient assumption**: stronger is better. Universal, unqualified answers do more work than qualified ones, and "sometimes" can never guarantee a "no threat" conclusion.
- **Reference precision.** The conclusion's demonstrative ("these successes") must be traced to its exact antecedent, not treated as a vibe.

## Teaching moves

- **First principles every session.** "Like always we're going to touch on the basics and then go from there... we're going to start from just first principles and then we're going to go up." He asked Dakota to define argument, then valid argument, before touching a question.
- **Reframing the difficulty.** Dakota called sufficient assumption hard. Joshua reframed: "they seem hard at first, but they're actually one of the easiest question types for you to get really good at... they're your speed bump questions, and they're not easy, but they're easy to solve if you have the right methods."
- **Dismantling before answering, named as the differentiator.** "The big aha moment for students who are able to really master the test is when they get really good at dismantling the arguments. It's not the people who are able to find, oh, sufficient assumption, that do really well on this test. It's the people who can first dismantle the question very well, then work backwards."
- **Prosecutor role carried into sufficient assumption.** "Your goal is to play the prosecutor role. Your goal is to make sure this person gets locked up. Whatever the conclusion is, you have to guarantee the conclusion is found guilty."
- **The Alex arson worked model.** Joshua builds a two-sided toy argument to teach fact-vs-rule. Version one: "Alex burned down a building, therefore Alex is guilty of arson." Dakota supplies the missing rule (the definition of arson). Version two: supply the rule (burning a building down equals arson) and Dakota must now supply the fact (Alex burned the building). "One of these two is going to be missing. The game is really just figuring out which one is missing and adding it."
- **Autofill analogy for the new-concept trap.** He compares it to the old ads with a letter missing that your eye silently repairs: "your brain will automatically autofill it... then you look at the conclusion like, wait, it didn't actually say what I thought it said. The words count. The essence might seem the same but the words are different."
- **Train station metaphor for the gap.** "Think of it as a train station. The evidence left off at one station and the conclusion picked up from a new station. The problem is there's no connecting train between the two." Later: "they'll lead you to believe the train takes you to the station which is the conclusion, but they actually drop you off not close enough."
- **Bridge building as the whole job.** "We're just literally bridge building. That's all we're doing. It's always the same thing. It's not going to change."
- **Answer-choice prefilter before analysis.** Once the new concept was identified as "angering the majority," he had Dakota scan all five choices and strike every one that fails to mention it, eliminating two before any reasoning.
- **Context-inference for unknown vocabulary.** Dakota did not know "assent." Joshua would not define it directly: "take that word out and look at what the words around it say," then reread the sentence with a blank. Dakota reached "conforming" himself. Joshua added a Kanye West illustration of the creative genius who will not conform, then cut it off: "you don't want to get too lost in that."
- **Color-coding repeated ideas.** "Whenever there's repeating ideas, highlight them in the same color, so you can visually see the argument very clearly. Then you never fall for the referential problem." He did this live on the herbal remedy question, yellow for herbal remedy, pink for prescription drug, then asked which color "chemical agents" matched. When Dakota said pink, Joshua asked "but did they say prescription drugs?" and the mismatch surfaced.
- **Naming bias as the real enemy.** On the herbal question: "because we hear the word herbal, we assume that there's no chemicals. But they never said that... chemicals are in nature. We think of synthetic versus natural, but that's our biases reading into what's being said. Sufficient assumption is forcing yourself not to allow yourself to have the biases of what you understand it to be."
- **Zero-percent framing for absolute conclusions.** "If there's even 1% threat, this conclusion falls and collapses entirely." Used to explain why "sometimes" in choice D is fatal and why the unqualified choice A wins.
- **Nuclear bomb for a gunfight.** His phrase for an overpowered but correct sufficient assumption answer. Extended: if you conclude "basketball is good for you," a premise saying "every single thing that starts with the letter B is good for you" is excessive but does the job. "Stronger is better than weak for a sufficient assumption. The more powerful the answer, the better."
- **Technique subordinated to comprehension.** "All of these techniques and things are cool, but they all pale in comparison to the one fundamental thing, which is just understanding what the argument says."
- **Correcting Dakota's own overgeneralization at the close.** Dakota proposed "the conclusion should be restated in the answer choice." Joshua tightened it: the answer must address the conclusion, and specifically must guarantee it.

## Worked examples

- **Genuine creative geniuses (sufficient assumption).** Chain: genuine creative geniuses are dissatisfied with merely habitual assent to widely held beliefs; those so dissatisfied tend to seek out controversy; controversy seekers enjoy demonstrating the falsehood of popular viewpoints. Conclusion: thus these rare innovators tend to anger the majority. Evidence stops at "demonstrating the falsehood of popular viewpoints"; the conclusion introduces "angering the majority." Choices A and C eliminated on the prefilter for not mentioning angering the majority. Correct answer B links demonstrating the falsehood of popular viewpoints to angering the majority.
- **Herbal remedy (sufficient assumption).** Clinical tests like those used for prescription drugs found a popular non-prescription herbal remedy as effective at treating painful joints as a certain prescription drug that has been used successfully. The manufacturer cites this as proof that chemical agents are unnecessary for successful treatment. Joshua walked the transitive step first (non-prescription is as effective as prescription, prescription is successful, therefore non-prescription is successful), then isolated the real gap: nothing establishes that the herbal remedy is free of chemical agents. Dakota answered B correctly.
- **Extremist parties (sufficient assumption).** Commentators note with concern recent electoral success by extremist parties in several democratic countries, but these successes pose no threat to democracy: the extremists have won pluralities, not majorities, and only when moderate parties were preoccupied arguing among themselves. New concept in the conclusion: threat. Referent of "these successes": winning pluralities, not majorities. Dakota chose D (members of moderate parties who consider extremists a serious threat will sometimes put aside their differences to oppose them), rejected because "sometimes" leaves the other cases open and only strengthens. Correct answer A: parties that win pluralities but not majorities never directly or indirectly affect changes in their country. Credited because "changes" is unqualified, so it covers every conceivable change, direct and indirect, which more than guarantees zero threat.
- Also referenced in passing: a prior sufficient assumption question involving synthetic versus natural concepts, which Dakota had answered correctly.
