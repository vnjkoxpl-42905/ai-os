## LSAT concepts

- **Argument**: premise plus conclusion, nothing else. "There's a sweet dance between the two. We're trying to assess if their dance is in sync." One piece of evidence plus one claim qualifies, however bad the reasoning.
- **Premise**: evidence. "A statement that gives evidence for another statement." Used interchangeably with "evidence."
- **Conclusion**: the author's claim. Never labeled by the test; identifying it is part of the game.
- **Premises are never negotiable**: "You never negotiate or question the premises of whether they're true or not... we grant them to be true." This is why no subject-matter background is needed for the LSAT.
- **Conclusions are always attacked**: "Conclusions are the part of the argument that you want to wrestle with. We grant their evidence. We deny their conclusion."
- **Premise indicator words, FABS**: For, After all, Because, Since. Whatever follows must be a premise, no exceptions. Because of that, they are indirectly conclusion indicators: whatever precedes them is a conclusion of some kind.
- **Conclusion indicator words**: therefore, thus, so, hence, consequently, it follows that. Looser than premise indicators because there are multiple kinds of conclusion. Therefore, thus, and so are the three most common.
- **Intermediate / sub-conclusion**: supported by a premise and supporting the main conclusion. "The intermediate conclusion essentially becomes a premise" for the main conclusion (Dakota's own formulation, confirmed).
- **Validity**: binary, not a spectrum. "Valid is not between reasonable and unreasonable. Valid is binary. It's either 100% valid or it's zero. No in between." A valid conclusion is a must-be-true statement given the evidence.
- **The spoiler**: LSAT conclusions are essentially never valid. "There might be one exception every section and that's going to be a parallel question... for all purposes, 95% of them are invalid."
- **Correlation**: "two things that occur together frequently. We don't know the cause for it." Correlations are typically used as evidence, not conclusions.
- **Background information and opposing viewpoints**: not every line is premise or conclusion. When a group is named (economists, biologists), the author will disagree with them.
- **Section structure**: three scored sections, two LR and one RC, so LR is two thirds of the test. A fourth section is experimental and unscored, and you are not told which. LR skills transfer to RC; the difference is largely superficial.

## Teaching moves

- **Corrects the everyday definition without dismissing it.** Dakota defined argument as a position you defend or persuade with. "That's actually not a bad way of conceptualizing it. It's not exactly what it is on the LSAT."
- **The architectural engineer framing.** "You should not look at the LSAT as a subjective test. Think of it from the perspective of an architectural engineer. You're looking at it from a structural point of view. Your goal is to identify the structure."
- **The wedding cake on blocks.** Premises are solid blocks, the conclusion is a fluffy three-tier cake. Extended past the basic image in a way the other students did not get: arrangement matters, not just sturdiness. "It's not enough to say those blocks have to be sturdy. They can be the most sturdy blocks ever. But if they're all supporting half the cake, the cake is still going to fall over. If I put the two blocks on the left side of the cake, it's going to fall towards the right."
- **The international lawyer analogy for accepting premises.** Personalized to Dakota's Ohio upbringing: if you came to an Ontario court and said "your Canadian laws are cute, but that's not how we do it in Ohio, could we change the laws for my case," you would look crazy. "We treat the stimulus as though we're living in their world... we take the rules that they give us, which are the evidence, and we try to beat them at their game."
- **Confirms and elevates the student's own example.** Dakota offered "turtles are mammals and here's why" as a premise you would not dispute. Joshua reinforced it, then reused it later verbatim as the shared reference.
- **Rewards a question that jumped ahead.** Dakota asked whether indicator words follow the premise or the conclusion. "She jumped the gun there, but you caught something very important." He then made her derive the conclusion-indicator corollary herself, and flagged its rarity: "most people go through their whole prep and not know what you just said."
- **LeBron and Kyrie for conclusion hierarchy.** The main conclusion is LeBron, the superstar being supported by everyone. The intermediate conclusion is Kyrie, a star in his own right who is still supporting the main act. Premises are at the bottom.
- **Logical hostility, again via the lawyer.** "You're not reading to be friendly. I don't want a friendly attorney. I don't want my lawyer to say, you know what, let's think about giving them more. I want my lawyer to say I want to take their kids. That's why lawyers are the most hated people."
- **The "from" vs "in" trap, taught by walking Dakota into it.** He presented "Alex is in Chicago. Chicago is in the USA. Therefore Alex is from the USA." Dakota called it valid. He revealed the switch. "From does not mean in... on the LSAT they're requiring you to be robotic. You would never want to be this person at a party. Nobody wants to hear 'no, technically speaking, you're not from here.' But on the LSAT they require you to be almost like an antisocial weirdo. Every detail counts."
- **"Basically true is the enemy of the LSAT."** Stated as a phrase to be repeated many times. "There's no such thing as basically right. It has to be exactly right. Every word counts."
- **Nothing is accidental.** "Nothing on the LSAT is ever by accident. Every detail, every letter, every stroke of the pen... You might think, oh, they must mean it like this. No, if they did mean it that way, they would have wrote it that way."
- **Full-time validity checker / devil's advocate.** The recurring job description. The test question: "Is there any possible way the conclusion could be wrong?"
- **The why / because test, with an honest self-critique of his own example.** Taught by inserting "why" between two ideas and seeing which order is coherent. His example (Maya is a great athlete since she is a longtime marathon runner) read awkwardly and he said so twice: "this is kind of poorly written on my side... I probably should have used a better example." He then let Dakota discover that "because" flowed better than "why" here, and confirmed the two are interchangeable, pick whichever sounds natural.
- **Translate the stimulus, one complete thought at a time.** "We are going to stop after one complete thought. It could be half a sentence. We're going to pause, look away, and try to rephrase it into a natural language that we speak." Role-play framing: "You're the lawyer. You're telling me what it means in normal English. I'm the janitor. I hire you to decipher the LSAT language."
- **Scaffolds the recap down when it fails.** When Dakota's summary of a two-clause sentence got muddled, he shrank the unit: "I'll have you summarize up until 'undesirable locations'," then built back up to a whole-stimulus recap.
- **Slow is fast, argued from evidence and from cost.** "The best indicator, if a student is in the highest scoring range, is that they read very slow." The mechanism: reading fast means bouncing between answers and stimulus repeatedly. The cost argument: "if you save 30 seconds and get it wrong, you might as well have not done the question at all. You could have gone and got a coffee, come back, and got the same value for your time."
- **Praises process, not the right answer.** When Dakota narrowed to A and E and then reasoned to E: "I like that you wrestled with the two. That's exactly what you want to do... you want to examine, because you did the homework up front."
- **Removes the score anxiety flatly.** On her 144: "144, 154, 134 doesn't matter. It's still the same process. It's not a strong indication of where you can be. Don't think about the score for now. All your goal is to do is to do the work for the day."
- **Explicit permission to use notes.** "Do not shy away from using notes. Use them and have them beside you. There's no shame in that. Your goal is just to get the reps in."
- **Credits students for the tools.** FABS came from a student a couple of years back, and he said so.

## Worked examples

- **Main conclusion question, values and actions.** Stimulus: a strong correlation exists between what people value and the way they act. For example, those who value wealth tend to choose higher paying jobs in undesirable locations over lower paying jobs in desirable locations. Thus, knowing what people value can help one predict their actions.
  - Read line by line with a forced paraphrase after each. Dakota's paraphrases were accurate but needed one scaffold-down.
  - Indicators spotted by Dakota: "for example" (premise) and "thus" (conclusion).
  - Two conclusions present. Joshua flagged this as making it a trickier question and admitted the choice was accidental: "for some reason this one was the one I chose."
  - Dakota initially ordered them backwards, putting the correlation as the main conclusion. Resolved by asking which claim makes the other more believable, and by the nature of correlations: "usually correlations are going to be used as evidence." First sentence is the intermediate conclusion, last sentence is the main conclusion.
  - Answer selection: narrowed to A and E, re-read both aloud, rejected A ("knowing how people behave") because the conclusion runs the other direction, from values to actions. Chose E. Correct.
  - Framing on answer matching: "we're looking for an answer that doesn't have to be verbatim. It has to be a meaning match."
