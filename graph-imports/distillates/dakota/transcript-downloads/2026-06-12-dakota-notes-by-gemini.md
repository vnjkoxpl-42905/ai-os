# Dakota Brown, 2026-06-12

## LSAT concepts

- **Method of reasoning**: identify the vehicle that carries the argument from premise to conclusion. "How do they get from premise to conclusion?"
- **Ruling out alternatives**: proving a claim by dismissing the other options rather than arguing for it directly. Inherently flawed because it presumes the listed options are exhaustive.
- **Pointing out an assumption**: challenging the link between evidence and conclusion. Common in the second speaker of a two-speaker stimulus, and the backbone of necessary and sufficient assumption questions.
- **Raising an overlooked consideration**: an objection, external in character. Introduces a new scenario that, if true, destroys the argument. Closer in spirit to strengthen and weaken.
- **Analogy and disanalogy** as reasoning structures. "Just as X, so Y" implies a comparison. Disanalogous just means the two groups differ in some important respect.
- **The question-type ladder.** Main conclusion, role, argument part, method of reasoning, and flaw are one family. Main conclusion asks what the north star is; role asks what each other piece does relative to it; method of reasoning builds on both to name the structure; flaw is the final iteration, naming the specific fallacy. Getting the first three right makes flaw automatic.
- **Matching game vs adding game.** For method, role, and flaw, the answer must accurately describe the stimulus and introduce nothing new. For sufficient assumption, strengthen, and weaken, the answer must introduce something new. "If something mirrors the stimulus in the answer choices, that would be a terrible answer for sufficient assumption."
- **Least wrong, not most right.** On these description questions, a single mischaracterized component is disqualifying. "It has to do two things: it can't say anything wrong, and it obviously has to say something right."
- **Two-part answer choice mapping.** Answer choices for these types split into an evidence half and a conclusion half. The word "by" always introduces evidence: "the author shows, by doing X, that Y" means X must match the evidence and Y must match the conclusion. Cross-reference each half separately.
- **Descriptive vs prescriptive.** "Should" is prescriptive and enormously demanding: the author must first assume a universal goal, set a standard, then claim the best route to it. An answer choice smuggling "should" into an argument that never made a normative claim is wrong.
- **Causation equals explanation.** "Explanation just means another way of saying cause."

## Teaching moves

- **Concrete toy examples for each abstract reasoning label.** Ruling out alternatives: Eliza wants Herbert to learn drums, piano, or flute; drums and piano do not fit the apartment, so buy a flute. Then a sharper version to expose the flaw: "because Buddhism is not true, and Hinduism is not true, and Judaism is not true, therefore Christianity is true. Why did you just assume Christianity is the only one left over?" Pointing out an assumption: the niece who loved Twilight books a year ago, so a Twilight calendar this Christmas.
- **Sports commentator analogy for the whole question family.** "It's asking you to be like a sportscaster giving me a play by play about what happened in the game. The game is the stimulus. Your goal is to describe what happened, versus trying to add or introduce something new. I'm recapping it. So the description of the story has to be accurate."
- **Selling the question type on leverage.** Even though method and role questions are only a few per section, "they give you a superpower. If you can do all of these three question types perfectly, you can always get flaw questions perfectly." He frames them as the highest-return practice target.
- **Paraphrase-first, always.** "Say it in your own words. You need to know the reasoning and then take away the words but know the logic underlying it. That's what method of reasoning questions are."
- **Variable substitution to expose form.** "Whenever we're talking about a description of people, or a group or category of things, you can likely just replace it with a variable." He rewrote the medical fraud argument live as "you can't explain X's behavior by citing Y, after all Y wouldn't apply to X." He offered it as a personal preference rather than a mandate: "that's what I like to do personally, but it's not for everybody."
- **Content replacement to see relationships.** "Replacing the content with something else that's more relatable to you forces you to see the relationship between the statements. These words are just used as ways to disguise the logic and the underlying moving parts. When you replace it with actual analogies, these questions become a piece of cake."
- **Plug the argument into the answer choice, not the reverse.** "Try to plug in what was said in the argument into the actual answer choice and see if it follows. It's a matching game."
- **The evidence/conclusion split drill run on all five choices.** After confirming the correct answer, he had Dakota do the same two-part decomposition on A, B, C, and E, asking each time "what part is the evidence, what part is the conclusion," so the technique got four extra reps.
- **Turning Dakota's own instinct into a rule.** Dakota rejected choice A because "should not be susceptible" appeared nowhere in the stimulus, then asked whether that was a fair basis. Joshua asked "what does should mean?", Dakota said "prescriptive," and Joshua confirmed and generalized it: "you perfectly answered that... a lot of students don't catch this."
- **Naming the "arguing that" cue.** "Whatever thing they're arguing for is always going to be the conclusion."
- **Certainty framing.** "This is why you should never get these questions wrong: you can always cross reference what actually happened, because it has to be describing the story of the argument properly."
- **Explicit praise tied to difficulty level.** "You got every question right, which is a very good sign. Three or four in a row, and these are all level three and above."

## Worked examples

- **Newspaper industry (method of reasoning).** The industry blames rising newsprint costs for falling profits, but corrected for inflation newsprint costs no more than a decade ago; far from being victims of high costs, newspapers have benefited from cheap newsprint for decades, and the real threats are falling circulation and falling advertising. Dakota answered D correctly: the author attacks the industry's explanation and offers a different explanation for the same phenomenon. Joshua tied it to causation, "causation just means explanation," and to the ruling-out-alternatives family. The first choice was rejected because it required an analogy that the argument does not use.
- **Societal laws and manners (method of reasoning).** Conclusion: laws are obeyed not because the behavior is ethically required or from fear of penalties, but because acting otherwise would be uncustomary, just as manners are observed because repetition makes contrary behavior unthinkable. Dakota named the structure as an analogy from his own paraphrase and picked the choice capturing the comparison between manners and laws.
- **Medical professionals and fraud (method of reasoning).** Most people are taken in by those marketing certain medical products, easily explained by their lack of extensive medical knowledge and their desire for easy solutions; but the same explanation cannot account for medical professionals falling for the same claims, since they do have that knowledge. Dakota answered D: the two groups are disanalogous in important respects, so there must be different explanations for their similar behavior. Verified by mapping: evidence half names two groups differing in medical knowledge (matches), conclusion half says the same explanation cannot cover both (matches). A rejected because "showing by analogy that medical professionals should not be susceptible to fraudulent claims" is prescriptive and misstates the conclusion, which is that susceptibility cannot be explained by citing a lack of medical knowledge. B rejected because no hypothesis is introduced and no second viewpoint exists. C rejected because nothing casts doubt on the professionals' expertise. E rejected as a conclusion that an explanation should be accepted despite apparent evidence against it, which does not match.
