# Dakota Brown, 2026-04-24

Three role-of-a-claim questions worked end to end. Heavy focus on answer-choice mechanics.

## LSAT concepts

- **Role of a claim (method / function questions)**: reduces to one question. "All you're asking yourself is what is the relationship of this statement to the main conclusion. That's all it's asking you." Not what the statement does at face value.
- **Opposing viewpoint markers**: "some people maintain," "some would," any "some." Signals a view the author will reject.
- **Concession vs plain opposing viewpoint**: a concession grants the other side a point ("many of them are reasonable") without agreeing. This changes which answer choices are viable, because the author is not dismissing anything.
- **The rebuttal is always the conclusion.** Dakota asked if this holds in most cases. "It's always going to be the conclusion. Every single time. Never going to be without exception."
- **Pivot words**: but, yet, however. After an opposing viewpoint one of these three appears almost exclusively, and what follows is either premise then conclusion or conclusion then premise. Conclusion first is the more common order.
- **"Why should I believe that?"**: applied to every claim to sort premise from conclusion.
- **Referential language**: resolve every "it," "this," "they" before proceeding.
- **"Illustrates" strictly means example**: "an individual instance of whatever idea I'm trying to prove." A broad statement supporting a broad statement is not an example.
- **Two-part check on answer choices**: every role answer has two halves, the role claimed ("it supports") and the statement it is claimed to relate to ("the conclusion that X"). Both must be descriptively accurate. "Check and check, not just one side."
- **Attacking the evidence is not attacking the conclusion**: "Me saying you're wrong for this reason doesn't mean you're wrong entirely." Disproving someone's reasoning does not commit you to the opposite conclusion.
- **No inferring beyond the text**: the author saying a reason for banning is bad does not mean he says banning should not happen. "Even to say it's implied, you technically wouldn't even be right about saying that."
- **Thematic-word traps**: "The LSAT loves to throw in words that feel correct with the theme but were never actually discussed." Cloning and individuality feel related; the argument was about ego, vanity, and ambition.

## Teaching moves

- **Enforces read, rephrase, recall as a rhythm.** When Dakota started reading the whole stimulus, he stopped her: "read rephrase, read rephrase, read rephrase." Later shortened to a chant: "read, rephrase, recall."
- **Diagnoses nearly all wrong answers as comprehension failures.** "Anytime the person gets the question wrong, it's almost certain the issue is an inability to rephrase the statement properly as you read it. It's 85% of problems, because you misunderstood the meaning of one of the claims."
- **The remedy is smaller units, down to one word.** "Start off from where you do understand and then add on to that. Even if that means one word. What does that one word have to do in relation to the next word. And there's no shame to it, it's actually what I do very often."
- **Reframes the whole question type as relative identity.** The strongest framing of the session: "If you have a sister, what's your relationship between you and that person? Are you a brother inherently, just a brother? No. You're a brother in relation to your sister. And when your mom is in the room you'll be a son. You're not a brother anymore." A claim has no role in isolation, only in relation to the conclusion. Dakota named her own error against it: "when I originally read it, I didn't say how does this relate to the conclusion. It was just me taking the sentence at face value."
- **Bite-sized answer-choice evaluation.** "Take it in bite-sized pieces. Does any of those words in the answer choice mischaracterize the portion of the argument they're referring to?" Every choice was walked half by half, with the trap always sitting in the second half.
- **Names the trap answer explicitly and explains the near-miss.** On A in the cloning question: "That's the trap answer here. It's very close, because it does have the same kind of vibe. It just goes too far." He then made Dakota confirm it herself by asking what the author said about the fears (reasonable), so "dismissing" fails.
- **Validates a counterfactual question.** Dakota asked whether A would have been correct had "many of them are reasonable" been absent. "Yes. Exactly. That's why it's so important to notice the concession. If it was just an opposing viewpoint, you would have been completely correct."
- **Refuses to move on when comprehension is shaky.** "You sure? I think so. I'm worried because I don't want to rush you. I don't feel like I'm pushing to go forward. I want to make sure you understand it." He then had her restate the reasoning back.
- **Teaches "example" by asking for the negative case.** Dakota asked what would not be an example. He gave "rich people all work very hard" as a broad statement supporting a broad statement, and she produced the fix herself: "if you would have said Bill Gates, then that would be an example."
- **Everyday analogies to isolate the evidence-vs-conclusion nuance.** Two versions, both political or religious, both careful: disputing a gas-price claim about Trump does not commit you to the opposite view of Trump; disputing "God is real because he speaks to me" is not disbelief in God. "That doesn't mean I'm going to the other side. I'm just saying that what you said was wrong. I didn't make a claim about the broader point."
- **Names the wrongly-written stimulus rather than defending it.** On the cloning-vanity question: "This is a very bizarre stimulus. I won't lie to you. Very awkward, poorly written from top to bottom. My head hurts reading this. I feel like dyslexic." He also admitted misreading a referent himself in real time: "I actually mixed that up there... this is why, and it tripped me up too."
- **Admits improvisation.** "I'm just doing this question with you in real time."
- **Builds the counter-objection habit early but defers it.** "As you're reading, you want to think of ways that you counter the argument as you read. Does that actually guarantee that the other side's wrong? We don't have to worry about that for now."
- **Uses vivid, slightly transgressive real-world parallels.** AI doomer fears for the clone-army fear; NBA parents who reappear after the draft for parents using children as vehicles for ambition. He self-checks when they get crude or dark.
- **Praises a rare catch and says exactly how rare.** When Dakota independently noticed the author only attacks the reason for banning and never says whether banning should happen: "That's a very tricky thing to catch. Very very almost almost almost nobody."

## Worked examples

- **Landfills (role of a claim).** Landfills are generally designed to hold ten years' worth of waste. Some people maintain that as active landfills dwindle over the coming decade there will inevitably be a crisis in landfill availability. However, their prediction relies on the unlikely assumption that no new landfills will open, and is therefore unsound. The underlined claim is the crisis prediction. Answer D, a claim the argument as a whole is designed to cast doubt on. Dakota answered correctly and unaided. Joshua's added point: the LSAT states the opposing viewpoint abstractly ("a claim the argument is designed to cast doubt on") instead of plainly, and decoding that phrasing is the skill.
- **Cloning, clone army (role of a claim).** Ethicists have fears about cloning human beings, many of them reasonable. But the horror movie image of a wealthy person creating an army of exact duplicates is completely unrealistic. Clones must be raised and educated, a long-term process that could never produce adults identical to the original in outlook, personality, and goals. More realistic is the possibility that wealthy individuals might use clones as living organ banks. Claim in question: that clones will not produce adults with identical outlook and personality.
  - Structure: claim 1 concession, claim 2 main conclusion, claim 3 evidence, claim 4 further evidence.
  - A rejected: "dismissing" contradicts the concession that the fears are reasonable. Named as the trap.
  - B rejected: correctly calls it evidence but mischaracterizes the conclusion as "genetic clones will never be produced successfully." The author expects clones to be produced, just not with identical personalities.
  - C rejected: "illustrates" requires a specific instance, and the claim is broad; also its stated conclusion (only wealthy people will have duplicates) is never asserted.
  - D rejected: correctly calls it evidence but attaches it to the organ-bank claim, which is not the conclusion. Flagged as "very dangerous" because it sits adjacent to the right answer.
  - E correct: a reason for discounting one specific fear, the clone-army or Terminator fear.
- **Cloning, vanity (role of a claim).** Some would ban cloning on the grounds that it is wrong to create a human being merely to indulge the vanity of the original. It is not illegal, however, to use one person as a vehicle for the ambitions of another. Some people push their children to achieve in academics or athletics. You do not have to have been born in a test tube to be an extension of someone else's ego. Claim in question: the "not illegal" line.
  - Structure: claim 1 opposing viewpoint, claim 2 the author's supporting claim, claim 3 a premise exemplifying claim 2 (Dakota first misread it as another counterargument, then corrected: "even though it's referring to other people, that doesn't inherently make it a counterargument. They're actually using it as a premise"), claim 4 the conclusion.
  - The conclusion is implied and narrow: the vanity reason is not a good reason to ban cloning. It is not a claim that cloning should or should not be banned.
  - A rejected: individuality is never the author's subject. Thematic trap.
  - B rejected: the children clause is a premise, not the conclusion, and the author never claims clones are not sub-people. "If anything he's fighting the other side. He's kind of psychopathic. He's like, we use people all the time, what's wrong with using people?"
  - D correct: it supports the ethicist's view that vanity as the motivation is not enough of a reason to ban cloning.
  - E rejected: it does describe a legal position, but the author never argues that the law should be changed. Fails the second half of the two-part check.
