# Dakota Brown, 2026-06-05

## LSAT concepts

- **Assumption** = the missing premise, "the missing evidence to guarantee the conclusion." An invalid argument is by definition just an argument with an assumption in it; a valid argument has zero.
- **Validity** re-established: premises guarantee the conclusion without appeal to anything outside the evidence.
- **Sufficient vs necessary assumption**, framed quantitatively. Sufficient must cover the whole gap or more, so stronger is better. Necessary is the bare minimum floor. "If you go even half a penny over, then you have a problem for necessary assumption, because then it's not absolutely necessary." The strongest necessary assumption and the weakest sufficient assumption intersect, so one answer can occasionally be both.
- **Necessary assumptions come in two forms**: things the author must actively affirm, and things the author must reject. Both are equally assumptions. Joshua names the frameworks **linking assumptions** (positive, connect evidence to conclusion) and **defender assumptions** (rejections of objections that would destroy the argument).
- **Assumption and objection are inverses.** "A negation of an objection is just an assumption negated, and an assumption negated is just an objection. They're the same thing." So the working method is to attack the conclusion, then negate your own objection.
- **Attack the conclusion, not the evidence.** "The evidence you have to respect it. You have to grant it to them. But you're trying to say, even if I grant it to them, how could I still play devil's advocate?"
- **Negation test.** Negate the choice; if the argument collapses, it was necessary. If it only gets wobbly, it was helpful but not required.
- **Negation must use the weakest force that crosses the line.** "You want to think of the negation as the weakest force to push you over the not line. It's not just the opposite." Negating "completely isolated" means one single contact ever, not widespread contact.
- **Quantifier negation.** none negates to at least one (think "one"); some negates to none; most means 51% or more and negates to 50% or below; "some are not" is just another way of saying "not all," and negates to "all." none is also equivalent to "all... not."
- **Answer strength for necessary assumption: weaker is better.** "most," "all," "must" are red flags because the author need not commit that far. Not an absolute rule, but a strong telltale.
- **The "not" scanning heuristic.** On necessary assumption questions, go to the choices containing "not" first. Double negation makes them trivial to test (just read the sentence without the "not"), and the choice containing "not" is very often the credited answer.
- **New concept in the conclusion** forces an assumption, and this holds for every argument-based question type, not just sufficient assumption. Joshua puts it at roughly 85% likelihood that the necessary assumption answer will contain the concept the conclusion introduced.
- **"Should" and "ought" are moral claims** and enormously demanding. "Anything that has should in a conclusion, if it doesn't have should in the evidence, is guaranteed to be a problem."
- **Qualifications are never accidental.** "If they give you a qualification, they mean that qualification. They purposely put it there and they hope you don't see it." Example: "assumptions that appear to guide legislators" (appearance, not actual belief).
- **Indicator words**: "so" introduces a conclusion, "after all" introduces a premise.
- Flaw questions are the most common LR type, but they lean heavily on necessary assumption. Sufficient and necessary assumption together are among the highest-frequency types.

## Teaching moves

- **Cash register analogy**, the spine of the whole session. A pack of cigarettes costs $20, the customer puts down $19.50 in change hoping you will not count it. The missing 50 cents is the assumption; the price is the conclusion; the money handed over is the evidence. Sufficient assumption: any amount that covers the 50 cents or more (an extra $20 also works, "thank God, you have lunch now"). Necessary assumption: does he need at least 10 cents? Yes. 40 cents? Yes. 50 cents? Yes. 51 cents? No. That last exchange is where Dakota locates the ceiling.
- **Negative space analogy**, tailored to Dakota's interests. He checked first ("if you're into art at all"), then, learning Dakota has tattoos, extended it to realism tattooing: the artist uses unshaded negative space to imply structure and contrast. "But the thing is, if it's not shaded in, we can't give them credit for it." He immediately flagged its limit: "this is not a good analogy, the second one I gave you."
- **The Michael Jackson guessing game.** Joshua thought of a person and had Dakota ask true/false questions (politician: false. celebrity: true. male: true. musician: true. rapper: false). After the reveal, he used the trait list to teach that necessary assumptions come in both polarities: hearing "I saw a celebrity" does not license guessing Michael Jackson (not sufficient), but hearing "it was not a celebrity" rules him out entirely (necessary). And "he is not a politician" is just as much an assumption as "he is a musician." Joshua noted only one student has ever guessed it.
- **The Ohio law school toy argument.** "Dakota applied to Ohio State, therefore Dakota must want to go to a great law school." Dakota supplies the linking assumption (Ohio State is a great law school), then Joshua shows the defender side: the author must also be rejecting "Ohio State is a garbage law school." Reused later to demonstrate the objection-then-negate procedure.
- **Jenga metaphor for negation.** "You're saying one of these pieces in the Jenga structure is needed. If it gets taken away, the whole thing will collapse... If it gets wobbly but doesn't actually fall, that means it wasn't needed. It was good for the argument, but not actually needed."
- **Court case framing for what you are actually fighting for.** "The case you're taking on is the conclusion. All the other things are just ways to win the case. You need good evidence to win the case, but you're not trying to win the award for the best evidence. You're trying to win the verdict."
- **Reframing Dakota's stated problem.** Dakota asked how to figure out the assumption on hard questions. Joshua refused the framing: "Don't think of what is he missing. Think of it more like, how can I show that he's wrong, and then work backwards." Attacking is easier to see than prospecting for a gap.
- **The comprehension thesis, delivered as the session's centerpiece.** "The solution to nearly all the problems for students on the LSAT is if you just understand the stimulus in natural English... All the other things are just ways to get to doing that." Restated as a diagnostic he applies to Dakota personally: "Whenever you get the description correct in a stimulus, you always get the question right. Like, I mean literally every time. Whenever you describe it in a way that's shaky, it's almost nearly certain you're not going to get it right."
- **Conclusion stated without the "because."** When Dakota said "the artwork is a forgery because of the brush strokes," Joshua cut the second half: "you don't have to say because, you're introducing evidence." The conclusion is the case you are fighting for, on its own.
- **Public self-correction.** Mid-negation of the "none" answer choice, Joshua realized he had negated only the "not" and stopped: "Sorry, I actually made a mistake here. No, the word none doesn't get negated, you would negate both of them." He then reworked it from the top.
- **Live quantifier drill.** Rapid-fire: most, negation of most, some, negation of some, "some are not," negation of "some are not," walked until Dakota derived "all" himself and asked to write it down.
- **Lawyer-drafting analogy for weak answers.** "When you're writing a contract, you want to give yourself the ability to play it safe." Product claims: "this product can make you healthier" leaves plausible deniability; "will make you healthier" is terrifying, because "will means 100%" and one failure is a lawsuit. "All I have to prove as a lawyer is that it was 1% possible, and then you can't call me a liar."
- **Prefiltering answers by the conclusion's new concept.** On the legislation question he had Dakota scan for the phrase about prohibiting behavior harmful only to the actor, striking A, B, C before reading them closely.
- **Admitting a question is genuinely awkward.** On the legislation stimulus: "it's really even getting me tripped up for a second... he almost didn't even try to prove his argument. It's so ridiculously out there that it's tricky to get your head around. That's why this question is tricky."
- **Working the questions blind alongside the student.** He said openly on two questions "I haven't seen this question" and "I haven't read the answer yet, but that's a very good contender," modeling live reasoning rather than recall.

## Worked examples

- **Neanderthal interbreeding (necessary assumption).** It was once believed prehistoric Homo sapiens interbred with Neanderthals, but DNA testing of Neanderthal remains indicates otherwise, because contemporary human DNA differs substantially from Neanderthal DNA. Dakota produced the objection himself: our DNA may have changed over time. Negating that gives the assumption. Dakota initially chose A (at least some Neanderthals lived at the same time and place as prehistoric Homo sapiens), which Joshua showed runs against the author's own conclusion. D (Neanderthals and prehistoric Homo sapiens were completely isolated geographically) was rejected: negated at its weakest (one single meeting ever), the argument survives, and it is a strengthener rather than a requirement. The credited answer is the one containing "not," that the DNA of prehistoric Homo sapiens ancestors of contemporary humans was not significantly more similar to Neanderthal DNA than contemporary human DNA is. Read without the "not," it confirms DNA changed over time and destroys the argument.
- **Cassatt forgery painting (necessary assumption).** The canvas and materials are consistent with most of her other works and the subject matter resembles her finest paintings, but the brush style is not found in any work known to be hers; therefore it is a forgery. Two attack vectors surfaced: the qualifier "known to be hers" says nothing about unknown works, and the argument imposes an arbitrary criterion that an artist can never produce a one-off or experiment with a new style ("they can change their mind, right? I want to do something different this time"). Used as the vehicle for negating "none" and "no."
- **Legislation and democracy (necessary assumption).** Broad acceptance of the idea that people cannot look after their own welfare is injurious to democracy, so legislators who value democracy should not propose laws prohibiting behavior harmful only to the person engaging in it. After all, the assumptions that appear to guide legislators often become widely accepted. The gap: the evidence is about appearances and public uptake, the conclusion introduces prohibiting self-harming behavior, and nothing connects them. C rejected for restating the evidence's assumption rather than the conclusion's new concept. Correct answer E: a legislator proposing a law prohibiting an act that can harm only the person performing it will seem to be assuming that individuals are incapable of looking after their own welfare. Negated: proposing such a law would not send that message, so the toxic idea never spreads and democracy is safe, which collapses the argument.
