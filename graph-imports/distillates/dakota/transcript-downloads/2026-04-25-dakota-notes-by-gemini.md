# Dakota Brown, 2026-04-25

A four-question Role/argument-part drill that was supposed to be a five-minute warm-up before causation and instead ate the whole session, because Dakota kept asking for one more. Ends with homework scoping and the LSAC accommodations process.

## LSAT concepts
- **Argument = conclusion + premise** — the base decomposition. Joshua's caveat that other parts exist: "it could be also a concession, or it could be background information... or it's going to be an opposing viewpoint."
- **Role / argument part questions** — the question type drilled all session. Joshua notes 7Sage labels them "argument part."
- **Reverse engineering the answer choice** — the core method for Role questions. Answer choices describe the argument abstractly; your job is to translate back. "We're trying to do almost like a reverse engineering of the conclusion back to the actual stimulus... when you're able to master both sides is when you really get to the higher score range."
- **Evidence-introducing words** — "by," "from," "on the basis of." "Whatever follows the word 'by' is going to be a premise or support. Same thing 'on the basis'... whatever follows those things is going to be evidence." Corollary: whatever precedes is the conclusion.
- **"Follows from" = conclusion marker** — "If statement B follows from statement A, it means that statement A is a premise and statement B is a logical result. The LSAT uses this phrase to test if you know the direction of where the argument is flowing, because the conclusion is the end point of the trip."
- **Concession** — a point granted to the other side that is not support. "Concessions are never premises. They're never going to be support... it's a neutral ground. I'm meeting you middle ground." Extending an olive branch. Indicators: "although," and by function "unquestionably."
- **Counter-argument / opposing viewpoint** — signalled by "some people maintain." Joshua's persistent metaphor: "the opposing counsel."
- **Intermediate conclusion** — a claim that both supports and is supported. Test: "What would it need for it to be an intermediate conclusion?" Dakota: "It would need support." If nothing supports it, it is a plain premise.
- **A sentence is not one thought** — "people naturally think a sentence is a complete thought. That's not the case... A sentence can contain more than one thought, but a sentence contains at least one complete thought." No comma is required for the split. Consequence: the conclusion may be a fragment inside a longer sentence.
- **Sufficient** — introduced here in passing, deliberately not taught. "Sufficient... that is maybe the most important term on the LSAT... it's the most tested concept." Working definition given: "sufficient, by the way, means enough" — enough on its own, without adding anything else.
- **Individually sufficient vs. jointly required (and vs. or)** — Dakota derived it himself; Joshua named it as a future full lesson. "It's going to be 'and versus or.'"
- **The one-limb fallacy** — knocking out one of two individually sufficient reasons does not defeat the conclusion. "If you just dismiss one of the reasons, that doesn't disprove that the other reason could stand still."
- **Hypothetical** — "hypotheticals are used to test the logic of the other side. That's all hypotheticals are, just logic checkers. They're trying to see, will your logic at maybe a potential extreme stay consistent or will it crack? You're putting it under the heat." Also: "you want to treat it as though it's true."
- **Disprove vs. weaken** — "whenever you see 'disprove,' it has to directly contradict what the argument's other side is saying... It has to be direct, saying you are wrong." Hedged language ("it would be less clear") is fence-sitting, not disproof.
- **Example / illustration** — a specific instance. Dakota's own retained mnemonic: "wealthy people versus Bill Gates. Bill Gates is an example."
- **Straw man** — rebutting a claim the other side never made. Here the author attacks "moderate" dietary change when the doctors only said "dietary change."
- **Specificity as a wrongness signal** — "the more specific they get, the more likely it's going to be less correct, because then it requires extra conditions to be met."

## Teaching moves
- **The no-L rule.** Stated up front as a standing policy: "there's a rule that we're never — we don't ever allow ourselves to leave on an L. So ideally this will be the only question, but if not, we'll linger on this a bit more." The session's whole shape follows from it. He softens it immediately: "no pressure."
- **Relocates the problem from the answers to the stimulus.** "Always remember, with almost 90 to 80% likelihood, whenever a student has any problem with the answer choice, it's not an answer choice problem, it's a stimulus problem."
- **Read-and-rephrase, sentence by sentence, warned in advance as unpleasant.** "It's going to be really annoying, especially at first. Students hate me for this."
- **Stop at every comma.** "Take that stop — obviously you learn in kindergarten, in young grade school — but this is so underrated. Stop at every comma, use that moment to rephrase."
- **Makes him use the indicator word in his own sentence.** Dakota flagged "although" as signalling a counter-argument. Rather than accepting it, Joshua said "use 'although' in a sentence. Make up a random sentence." Dakota produced "although I love milk, I'm lactose intolerant," and Joshua then asked what work "I love milk" was doing — which produced the concession concept from Dakota's own mouth. He reused the milk sentence as the running example for the rest of the discussion.
- **Disqualification, not selection.** The mentality shift he names explicitly: "Remember the answer choices, you're not looking to find the right answer. That's not your goal, Dakota. Your goal is to find the answer that doesn't have anything wrong with it. Very different game. You're disqualifying." Paired with the two-part check — read the first clause of an answer, and if it dies there, stop weighing it: "one landmine will blow up the answer. We don't need to find another thing."
- **Warns against contamination from later clauses.** "You don't want to let the information that follows it blemish [it] — you will get influenced to see the answer not in the light it is."
- **Substitution test for abstract answer language.** "I want you to substitute each of the concepts in, and if you can't, we have basically a landmine." Run on D word by word: does "concede" fit? does "a distinction" fit? does "is unclear" fit?
- **The Matrix woman-in-red image for bait answers.** "I don't know if you've seen The Matrix, but the woman in the red dress — beautiful, she's walking by. But not everything that glitters is good for you. This is the bait."
- **Deliberately overshoots difficulty and announces it.** "We're going to go even harder. We're going to go really really hard. So don't feel discouraged if it doesn't go perfectly."
- **Asks permission to continue, repeatedly, and lets Dakota drive the length.** After each question: "do you want to do another question like this?" He was open about his own preference ("I'd rather do questions with you, because I like to do questions in real time") and about the time overrun ("I know that we're past the time we're scheduled, but I'm good to go because I came late today"). Dakota chose to continue three separate times.
- **Names the good behavior, not just the right answer.** "I like that you didn't tap out. I was worried that you'd probably say I want to move on to causation." And on holding a position under questioning: "you stuck to your guns, which is... these are good signs, because you're building the confidence."
- **Predict the rebuttal before reading it.** "When you read 'some people maintain' this idea, even rephrase the rebuttal before you see it, because when you predict it, it sticks... plus you get fast at the question. You don't have to read it with the same veracity because you know the outcome."
- **Makes wrong answers earn their airtime.** "Even if it's not the right answer, it's really good practice, because you'll see it again, and when you know what it looks like in the wrong answer, you'll know [what it looks like] when sometimes it might be the right answer another day."
- **Calls out bad LSAT writing rather than defending it.** On the stray "therefore": "It's just terrible writing... if I told a fifth grader to write like [this]... even me saying it out loud, my head hurts." And on the multi-concession stimulus: "very bizarre stimulus. I won't lie to you... his argument is almost like just pleading. He's like, yeah, I get you guys are like this, I get it, I get it."
- **The gray-area framing for hard questions.** "They don't want good guy, bad guy — that's easy for people to see. The harder questions will require for you to be in the gray area. The gray area is where they get students really trapped... We're not thinking Democrat, Republican. Okay, now we're both maybe on the same side in some sense, but maybe somewhere else we're not. We want to identify where's the point of tension."
- **Hypotheticals explained through everyday argument.** The bar example: simulate the friend's plan and show what it entails. And a stress-test example: someone who says always choose friends over self-interest gets tested with "what if it's like you're about to die and it would cost your friend buying you a Pepsi."
- **Genuine surprise, expressed as praise with a joke.** When Dakota independently produced the individually-sufficient/one-limb insight: "How did you know that? Did you learn that somewhere else?... Dakota, have you been cheating on me?" Then the honest calibration: "that is usually — it takes me forever to teach that to students, because I have to use a billion analogies. That's usually a full lesson."
- **Names when he was unclear and asks.** "Do you need to go into that level? No. But I probably was not so clear there... Did that make any sense? I don't think I answered your question exactly." He also checked whether Dakota knew the word "hypothetical" and immediately disclaimed condescension: "not like I'm insulting or anything."
- **Ties question-type priority to leverage.** On main conclusion and Role: "These are really important questions to be not just good at but to be a master. You don't want to be shaky on these questions, because they will follow you for the rest of every other question type. And so many students are shaky — they have a kind of feel for it, but they can't say they mastered it."

## Worked examples
- **Q1, Role (inventors and engineers).** Stimulus: "Unquestionably, inventors of useful devices deserve credit for their ingenuity, but the engineers who help develop an invention get too little recognition. Although inventors sometimes serve as their own engineers, more often engineers must translate inventors' insight into something workable and useful. Therefore, engineers also deserve credit for their contribution." Target statement: "inventors sometimes serve as their own engineers." Dakota initially chose C, then D. Path: he identified "although" as the concession marker, then Joshua drew out that "unquestionably" is also functioning as a concession (Joshua: "a very bizarre stimulus... multiple concessions, you'll almost never see it"). Since concessions never support, C ("supports an earlier statement") dies on the word "supports." A dies as out of scope — no theoretical aspect exists in a purely practical argument. D was the bait, attractive because it contains "concede," but "a distinction on which the argument relies is unclear" is unsupported: nothing in the stimulus is unclear. Credited answer: it indicates that the problem identified in the argument does not arise in every instance — the concession admits the credit problem is not universal while the conclusion stands.
- **Q2, Role (landfills).** Stimulus: landfills are designed to hold ten years of waste; some people maintain that as active landfills dwindle over the coming decade there will inevitably be a crisis in landfill availability; however, that prediction relies on the unlikely assumption that no new landfills will open, and is therefore unsound. Target: "a crisis in landfill availability." Dakota nailed it cold, and Joshua flagged it as harder than Q1. Two things he got that Joshua called high-level: the conclusion is the fragment "is therefore unsound," not the whole "however" sentence; and the target claim sits inside the opposing viewpoint. He arrived at the fragment point through his own rule about conclusions not always being full sentences, worrying that the lack of a comma undercut it — which is where Joshua corrected the sentence/thought conflation. Answers: A dies twice over ("follows from" makes it a conclusion, which it is not, and it further specifies the first sentence as its premise, which is background not premise — "it's double wrong"); B calls it the main conclusion, false; D says it establishes the truth of the author's argument, i.e. supports, false; E calls it an intermediate conclusion, false. Credited C: "a claim which the argument as a whole is designed to cast doubt on." Joshua: "By definition, if it's an opposing viewpoint, we're opposing that point. That's how we're fighting back. It's opposing counsel."
- **Q3, Role (cholesterol).** Stimulus: doctors urge people to reduce cholesterol through dietary changes, but moderate dietary changes often do not work to lower cholesterol levels; one may need therefore to make more dramatic changes, such as switching to a vegetarian diet. Target: "moderate dietary changes often do not work." Dakota located the conclusion correctly by asking "why should we believe this?" — the test Joshua confirmed as exactly right. Two contenders sat adjacent, and the stray "therefore" threw him; he removed it and checked the sentence still read, a technique from a prior session. He also correctly ruled out intermediate conclusion by checking whether anything supported it. Answers: B dies on "vegetarian diets are more healthful than any diet containing meat," never claimed; D dies as an explanation of the success of vegetarian diets; E calls it a conclusion. Down to A and C, and he initially preferred A ("presented to counter the doctors' suggestion"). Joshua's kill: the author does not counter the doctors at all. "He's actually on the side, but he's just more of a fanatic." Worse, the doctors never said "moderate" — "he's addressing a straw man... What if the doctor's even more extreme than him? We have no idea." Credited C: a premise supporting the claim that reducing cholesterol may require greater than moderate diet changes. Dakota had first misread C by skimming past "greater," anchoring on "moderate." He then diagnosed his own error: "when I saw 'but,' I said 'why we shouldn't believe this' — and that's not inherently true... I have to be careful with the but." Joshua: "That's the trap. That's literally the trap."
- **Q4, Role (vegetarianism hypothetical).** Stimulus: some vegetarians argue there are two individually sufficient reasons not to eat meat — health, and an aversion to living at the expense of other conscious creatures. But suppose eating meat were essential to good health; then it would be less clear that the aversion alone is a sufficient reason. Target: the "suppose eating meat were essential" clause. Dakota read it correctly on first pass — "he's not necessarily saying you shouldn't be a vegetarian. He's just saying your evidence, one of your pieces of evidence, is insufficient" — but could not name it; Joshua supplied "hypothetical." Answers: A (used to disprove the vegetarian position) dies on "disprove," since the author only says "less clear" — "He didn't take a side... he's fence-sitting." B (used to show the two reasons are independent) is backwards: the vegetarians assert independence, the author is the one treating them as a package deal. C dies on "disprove" again. E ("there's no sufficient reason not to eat meat") is far too extreme: "he's saying there's no reason to not eat meat? No, he never said that. Not a carnivore." Credited D: it is used to weaken the claim that the consciousness of animals is a sufficient reason. Along the way Dakota generated the underlying fallacy unprompted: "if you disprove a single piece of foundation, it disproves the entire argument" — the error the author is making, given the reasons were stated as individually sufficient.
