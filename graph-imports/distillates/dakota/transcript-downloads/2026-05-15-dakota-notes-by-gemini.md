# Dakota Brown, 2026-05-15

## LSAT concepts

- **Causation**: a causal claim asserts the cause "is making the other thing happen or has an influence on the other thing happening directly." Three attack routes: reverse causation, a third factor causing one or both, or pure coincidence (no relationship at all).
- **Weaken**: two legitimate paths against a causal conclusion, deny the author's explanation or supply your own alternative explanation. Both do the job.
- **Strengthen**: the mirror image, protect the vulnerability you just identified.
- **Correlation vs causation**: "every causal claim is a correlative claim, but not every correlation is a causation." Correlation is necessary for causation but not sufficient. Joshua explicitly frames this in sufficient/necessary terms and warns students who chant "correlation does not imply causation" without knowing what it means: "correlation does not imply that it's not causation as well."
- **Causal language cues**: active, motion-carrying verbs signal causation (makes, produces, influences, leads to, has an effect). Static, descriptive verbs signal correlation (is, are, will be). "More likely / less likely" is the LSAT's favorite bait, it sounds causal but is correlational. Rule of thumb he gave: evidence introduces a correlation, the conclusion asserts causation.
- **Paradox**: reconcile two seemingly contradictory facts by introducing new information. Unlike most LR question types, the correct answer must bring in information from outside the stimulus.
- **Conditional logic**: named alongside causation as "the heart of LR," the two things everything else flows from.

## Teaching moves

- **Causation as criminal alibi.** "Think of it as like alibis from the perspective of law." Joshua works causal attack as defense-attorney work: my client's alibi is an alternative causal storyline. Could it have been him, or could it have been the other person.
- **Prosecutor / defender frame for weaken vs strengthen.** Every argument is read the same way regardless of question type: find the weakness first, then decide what to do with it. "When they want me to weaken, now I have to think like a prosecutor. How do I expose that weakness in court?" He extends it to a client analogy: "You want to figure out where my weaknesses are first. So then you can defend it. But you don't want to try to defend without knowing my weaknesses."
- **Satirical live example to make coincidence concrete.** Dakota started studying about a month ago; Trump's war with Iran started about the same time. Joshua deadpans "Dakota studying caused the war in Iran... I'm not saying you have anything to do with it, but I'm not going to deny you have anything to do with it either. What happens if Dakota stops studying? There's many people that can just not die because of Dakota studying." Then escalates to an obviously absurd version: every time you have woken from sleep, someone on Earth has died. He uses the joke to derive the real test, effect without cause and cause without effect.
- **The two-cell disproof test**, taught off the same example: show the war happening three months before Dakota studied (effect, no cause), or Dakota studying while the war stops (cause, no effect).
- **New York / US analogy** for the necessary-but-not-sufficient relationship between correlation and causation.
- **"Because has the word cause in it"** as a mnemonic for what a causal claim owes you.
- **Do it first, then walk it.** Standard sequence: hand Dakota the question cold, give him about two minutes, take his answer, then read from the top and rephrase before evaluating any choice.
- **Prehypothesize before reading answers.** On the chimp paradox he refuses to let Dakota go to the choices until he has produced his own hypothesis, and holds the constraint firmly: "this one factor has to explain both at the same time."
- **Escalating hints rather than the answer.** When Dakota stalls, Joshua drops to an everyday parallel instead of telling him: people who bark "get at me" and never fight versus people who just fight; then "when somebody acts out... after they act out, they're typically okay, right? Why?" Dakota supplies "release their anger" himself.
- **Plug-in test for answer choices.** "Plug in the causal factor to see if it explains both of them." Correlation tells you what is happening; causation tells you why. "The answer should give you the why."
- **Naming the trap of restatement.** On the wrong answer E: "It's just restating the paradox. It's just telling you what's happening... this is essentially restating basically half the puzzle. Gesture is attackless, an attacker is gestureless."
- **Correcting an over-generalized habit.** Dakota eliminated a choice for introducing outside information. Joshua flags it as a real insight but inverts it for this family: "you're okay with adding new information, this is part of the game... We are so trained to always stick within what's been said that this is the opposite of that. You're looking for something that's not been said." He notes it also applies to strengthen and weaken.
- **Pivot words as a mechanical entry point.** "Pay attention to that pivot word... pivots just mean a point of tension." Whatever sits immediately before "but," "however," or "yet" must be reconciled with whatever sits immediately after. He calls it a universal rule for paradox.
- **Naming the test's own habits.** Unintended consequences is "one of their most famous tricks in the bag." On animal-population paradoxes: "whenever you see a population decrease, always interpret it as the prey for that was also a victim... they have no creativity when it comes to this."
- **Balance sheet metaphor** for net population change: "I make more money but I spend more money... you might make more money but that does not mean you have more money left over."
- **Precision pressure on the goal.** When Dakota argues "many mosquitoes" is too vague to count as a reduction, Joshua pushes to the literal threshold: one fewer mosquito technically satisfies "reduce," so vagueness is not the crux. That reframing forces Dakota to find the real tension.

## Worked examples

- **Chimpanzee threat gestures (paradox).** Angry chimps make threat gestures; angry chimps also attack; but attackers rarely gesture first and gesturers are rarely followed by attacks. Dakota's initial answer was E. The needed explanation is a single C factor that makes the two behaviors mutually exclusive. Correct answer B: threat gestures vent aggressive feelings, thereby avoiding physical aggression. E was rejected as a restatement of the paradox; A (gestures preserve or enhance social status) was rejected for explaining only the gesture half.
- **Mosquito / fruit tree (paradox).** Fruit trees planted to attract birds to reduce mosquitoes; birds arrived and ate many mosquitoes; yet the planting had the opposite of its intended effect. Correct answer C: the birds ate many more insects that prey on mosquitoes than they did mosquitoes, so removing the predators outweighed the direct predation. A and B (most / the most numerous bird species did not eat mosquitoes) were dismissed as trying to "cheat the paradox" rather than address it. D (below-average precipitation, drier weather keeps mosquitoes down) deepens rather than resolves. E (populations follow a cyclical pattern) ignores the two introduced variables and does not explain this specific case. Joshua notes the answer would still work if the population merely stayed the same rather than rising.
